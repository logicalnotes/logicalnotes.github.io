Film history is filled with works that linger in public consciousness for decades after release; some even spawn their own subcultures. But if we talk about the most consequential movie ever made, we have to turn to *Taxi Driver*, Martin Scorsese’s 1976 drama. Its ripple effect stretches to an almost unimaginable degree. Among its countless viewers was a young man named John Hinckley Jr., who, after watching the film, resolved to assassinate the president of the United States, driven by what he saw on screen.

Born in 1955 in Oklahoma, John Hinckley Jr. grew up in a wealthy merchant family. His father, John Hinckley Sr., was a World War II veteran who built a profitable oil business after the war. The family moved in elite political and business circles and counted themselves among the Bush family’s donors. By all outward markers, Hinckley should have had every advantage.

Through elementary and middle school, Hinckley was quiet but functional; he enjoyed sports and seemed ordinary enough. High school marked a sharp shift. He withdrew deeply, cut off contact with peers, and retreated into writing and music. After graduation, his father pressured him into college. Hinckley flunked nearly every class. Unable to stay enrolled, he headed for Los Angeles, convinced he would make it big as a composer in Hollywood. Reality quickly crushed that fantasy. Many aspiring artists see themselves as geniuses, but the world does not indulge every delusion. Hinckley simply lacked the talent; his compositions amounted to little more than wasted paper.

To sustain himself in Los Angeles, he lied repeatedly to his family, claiming he was negotiating music deals and dating actresses, all to beg for money. He kept this fraud going for a full year before his father traveled to California, uncovered the lies, and brought him home. “You’ve embarrassed yourself out here for two years,” his father told him. “Come back and get your act together.”

Once home, Hinckley retreated into years of isolation. Being a shut-in in the 1970s offered none of the internet or video games that occupy reclusive people today. To pass the endless hours, he turned to movies. He watched films nonstop, fixating especially on *Taxi Driver*, which he rewatched at least fifteen times. A landmark of American cinema, it captured urban loneliness and apathy in groundbreaking ways; the later film *Joker* drew heavily from its template.

The plot centers on a lonely, self-righteous taxi driver whose romantic advances are rejected. He plots to kill a corrupt presidential candidate, fails at that, and then stumbles into a shootout at a brothel. Instead of being prosecuted for the killings, he is hailed as a hero for rescuing a child prostitute. Hinckley paid no mind to the film’s social commentary. He grew obsessed with Jodie Foster, the young actress playing the teen prostitute. Foster was only fourteen then, but Hinckley cast her as his ideal soulmate, the love he would devote his whole life to winning. He binged every film she appeared in, dozens of viewings apiece, and his mental state unraveled further.

Years passed while he stayed locked away. His father finally reached the end of his patience—not because he could no longer afford to support his son, but because he could not bear to watch him rot into a dead weight. Hinckley’s parents took him to a psychiatrist to figure out what was wrong. Hinckley volunteered his fixation: he was in love with Jodie Foster. The doctor did not treat this as alarming; celebrity crushes are common for young people. His assessment was simple: the boy had no severe illness, only an empty life and no purpose. He needed something to do.

His father confronted him. “What do you want? You have to do something. You cannot keep living like this.” Unexpectedly, Hinckley had an answer: he wanted to study literature at Yale University. His father was relieved. Getting into Yale was out of reach for most people, but with the family’s connections, it was arranged without much trouble. Hinckley enrolled in the autumn of 1980.

What his father did not know was that Hinckley’s choice had nothing to do with education. That same year, Jodie Foster had paused her acting career to attend Yale. Once on campus, Hinckley became her silent stalker. He avoided direct confrontation at first, slipping handwritten love poems under her dorm door. He also sent flowers and called her, but every advance went unanswered. To a famous actress, this kind of unwanted attention was routine.

Rejection left Hinckley devastated. He called his parents and asked to drop out and come home. But instead of examining his own behavior, he turned the question over in his head: what was missing in him that made Foster turn him away? He never guessed the simple truth—she was not attracted to men. He knew his family had money and thought he was passably presentable. The only gap he could identify was courage. To win her, he needed fame. It did not matter whether that fame was good or infamous. Chances of being remembered for greatness were slim; infamy, however, was a gamble he could take.

He first considered robbing a bank, then remembered *Dog Day Afternoon*, Al Pacino’s 1975 heist drama, and ruled the idea out. Hijacking a plane or a ship crossed his mind too, but those plans felt too complicated. Rewatching *Taxi Driver* gave him his breakthrough. He found the lowest-barrier path: assassinate a president. Nothing would grab headlines like that. Jodie Foster would not be able to ignore him.

He moved fast. Jimmy Carter was president then. Hinckley carried a handgun and followed Carter across multiple states. The Secret Service’s security was tight, and he never got an opening. He was detained at an airport for carrying the pistol, and his father had to travel to Tennessee to bail him out. Back home again, dejected by Foster’s rejection and his failed assassination attempt, he drifted aimlessly.

By March 1981, his father made a painful call. He could not keep enabling his son’s stagnation forever, so he kicked Hinckley out, giving him a substantial sum of cash. “I’m doing this because you cannot keep going like this,” he told him. “If you straighten out and live a normal life, you will still have your share of the family estate.” The warning fell flat. Hinckley went straight back to New Haven to harass Foster once more, then traveled to Washington, D.C., supposedly to buy her a gift.

He checked into a hotel in the capital. On the morning of March 29, a newspaper caught his eye: Ronald Reagan, the newly inaugurated president, would speak at the Washington Hilton the next day. Reagan had only been in office for just over two months. To Hinckley, the target was interchangeable. Killing Carter or Reagan served the same goal: notoriety. If the opportunity was there, he would take it.

On the afternoon of March 30, Hinckley positioned himself outside the Washington Hilton with a newly purchased Röhm RG-14 revolver loaded with Devastator hollow-point rounds.

Reagan was one of the defining U.S. leaders of the Cold War. A former actor, he was often described as surprisingly uninformed on fine points of history and policy, yet he possessed sharp judgment about whom to trust and empower. Under Reagan, the United States abandoned the policy of détente that had treated the Soviet Union as a permanent, equal rival. The Reagan administration recognized the USSR’s structural weaknesses and believed defeating it outright was possible.

“Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate!” The Reagan administration’s pressure campaign against the Soviet Union reshaped the Cold War and contributed decisively to its eventual outcome. None of this mattered to Hinckley. He was not driven by politics or ideology. He only wanted to be famous.

There was an eerie premonition, too. Days before the shooting, Reagan and his wife had visited Ford’s Theatre in D.C.—the same venue where Abraham Lincoln was assassinated. Reagan later recalled glancing up at the box where Lincoln was shot and thinking: even with the Secret Service watching, a determined attacker could still get close to a president. A few days later, that uneasy thought came true.

At 1:30 p.m., Reagan’s motorcade pulled up at the hotel entrance. Hinckley waited, ready to strike. Reagan entered the building unharmed; the hotel had a secure enclosed passageway, and normally the limousine stopped only three or four meters away. The president would step out and vanish into the building in seconds. Hinckley had no shot then. He waited patiently through Reagan’s speech, ready for the exit.

A string of coincidences aligned against Reagan that afternoon. The crowd was larger than expected, so the limousine parked roughly nine meters from the secure entrance, giving Hinckley a critical window. Reagan had decided against wearing body armor, assuming the short walk posed no risk. Then a reporter called out to him from the crowd. Reagan paused and turned toward the voice. That split second was all Hinckley needed. He drew his revolver and fired six shots in 1.7 seconds.

The first bullet struck White House Press Secretary James Brady in the head, leaving him permanently disabled. The second hit D.C. police officer Thomas Delahanty in the neck, damaging his spine and leaving his left hand impaired. The third missed entirely. The fourth hit Secret Service agent Timothy McCarthy, piercing his lung and liver. The fifth ricocheted off the limousine window. The sixth bounced off the car’s armored bodywork, tore into Reagan’s lung, and stopped less than three centimeters from his heart.

Secret Service agent Jerry Parr reacted instantly, shoving Reagan into the limousine. That quick action saved his life. The Secret Service had revised its protocols after a 1975 attempt on Gerald Ford: agents had rushed to throw Ford into the car without opening the door first, nearly injuring him severely. After that fiasco, the limousine door was to remain open whenever the president was nearby.

Hinckley was tackled and pinned to the ground by agent Dennis McCarthy immediately after the sixth shot. The agent held him down hard, partly to restrain him and partly to shield him from the angry crowd; he even had to fend off several bystanders who tried to join the arrest.

News of the shooting spread worldwide in minutes. The nation was stunned. No one was more shaken than Hinckley Sr. He never imagined his directionless son would attack the president. But his priority was simple: save his son. Attempting to assassinate a U.S. president carried an enormous sentence, potentially hundreds of years in prison. The man who had tried to kill Ford years earlier had served more than thirty years despite never hitting anyone. Hinckley’s crime was far more violent, and he seemed certain to spend the rest of his life locked away.

Where most families would have resigned themselves to the verdict, Hinckley Sr. deployed every connection he owned. He lobbied at high levels, reaching as far as Vice President George H. W. Bush. He assembled an elite defense team comparable to the famous Simpson defense, including top-tier attorneys such as Edward Williams and Vincent Fuller. Williams was one of Disney’s most trusted legal advisors at the time.

The defense did not aim for a lighter sentence. They set out to win an acquittal on the grounds of insanity. Their argument: Hinckley had no coherent political motive. His stated reason—to impress Jodie Foster—was so irrational that it could only be explained by mental illness. They brought in leading psychiatrist Dr. John Hopper, who presented years of treatment records. Hopper testified that Hinckley had been in a psychotic episode when he fired, and therefore lacked criminal responsibility.

Prosecutors pushed back sharply. If the family and doctors knew Hinckley was mentally unstable, they asked, why had he been allowed to move freely? Hopper answered that Hinckley had previously been diagnosed with depression and had not seemed dangerous, though depression could turn violent.

The prosecution realized they were trapped in a battle of psychiatric opinions. They brought in their own expert, who testified that Hinckley was not depressed at all, but a narcissist. The competing diagnoses cemented the central narrative: Hinckley suffered from a severe mental disorder. After seven weeks of testimony and three days of jury deliberation, the defense prevailed.

On June 21, 1982, the jury found John Hinckley Jr. not guilty by reason of insanity. His father broke down in court, saying he blamed himself for failing to control his son and would take the punishment if he could. Hinckley himself showed no remorse. He declared the shooting was a grand romantic sacrifice for Jodie Foster and expressed disappointment that she had not returned his affection. The court dismissed his words as the ramblings of a sick man.

Hinckley had indeed burned Jodie Foster’s name into history. Reporters swarmed the Yale campus the moment his motive became public. Foster hated the ordeal and refused to answer any questions about him. She would walk away immediately whenever journalists brought up Hinckley. The acquittal triggered national outrage. Americans had never seen the insanity defense used to excuse an attack on a president. States rushed to rewrite their criminal statutes to close that loophole.

The federal government, however, was not finished with him. He walked out of the courtroom “not guilty,” only to be taken straight into custody by medical staff. He would not be going home. If his lawyers insisted he had an unpredictable, dangerous mental illness, then he required compulsory treatment. Worse for the defense: the condition they described was impossible to disprove. No one could prove he was cured.

He was committed to St. Elizabeths Hospital in Washington, D.C., a historic psychiatric institution with a grim reputation. Its grounds contained old patient cemeteries, and its archive held more than 1,400 preserved human brains and 100,000 brain tissue slides collected from former patients. For Hinckley, it was a fate worse than prison.

He tried to kill himself four times in his first three years there, all attempts failing. In 1987, he applied for family visits. Before a judge ruled, FBI agents searched his room and found photographs of Jodie Foster. His request was denied; he remained confined.

Hinckley stayed institutionalized for thirty-five years. In 2016, at age sixty-one, he was judged to pose no further threat and granted conditional release. Even then, he was not fully free. Strict court orders limited his movements and contacts. By then, his father, Reagan, and James Brady were all dead. The national fury of the early 1980s had faded.

The shooting left a quiet legacy for two other men. Reagan said surviving the attempt reinforced his belief that he was chosen by God to lead America and defeat the Soviet Union. Agent Jerry Parr, who pulled Reagan into the limousine, had originally joined the Secret Service after watching a film Reagan starred in, *Code of the Secret Service*. Parr later became a pastor, convinced divine providence had placed him there to save the president.
