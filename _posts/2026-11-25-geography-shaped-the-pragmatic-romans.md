Italy, Greece and Spain all sit in Southern Europe, occupying three peninsulas that reach deep into the Mediterranean. Greece lies on the Balkan Peninsula to the east, Italy on the Apennine Peninsula in the middle, and Spain on the Iberian Peninsula to the west. The Italian Peninsula sits right at the heart of the Mediterranean, splitting the sea into its eastern and western basins.

A quick glance at the map makes this shape obvious: surrounded by water on three sides, it looks just like a tall, stiletto boot planted firmly into the Mediterranean. The rocky island off the boot’s toe is Sicily, widely known in modern times for the Mafia.

To the north of the peninsula rise the Alps, Europe’s largest mountain range. They formed a natural barrier, separating Italy from tribal groups farther north. Much of northern Europe was still home to less settled peoples back then. Greece and Rome stood as the two foundational civilizations of the West, both clustered along the Mediterranean. The Alps roughly marked the divide between the civilized world and the wild frontier in that era.

Running the full length of the peninsula is the Apennine Mountains, which give the landmass its alternate name: the Apennine Peninsula.

The Adriatic Sea separates Italy and Greece. They look close on the map, yet their living conditions shaped very different societies.

Greece is rugged and rocky, with thin, poor soil ill-suited for farming. Most Greek city-states huddled along the coast. Local residents turned to fishing and maritime trade. Generations of seafaring bred a culture drawn to freedom, imagination and individual expression — traits forged directly by their environment.

The Romans were cut from a different cloth.

Fertile lowlands spread along both sides of the Apennine range, bathed in strong sunlight and ideal for farming. At the centre of Italy lies the broad plain of Latium, where Rome itself was founded. Agriculture thrived here from early times, with wheat growing abundantly; wheat remains the core ingredient for pasta today.

One people built their lives around farming, the other around sea trade. Naturally, their outlooks diverged sharply.

Farming demands patience. You plant in spring and must wait steadily through the seasons until autumn harvest. Every step follows the rhythm of the calendar, with no shortcuts possible. This repetitive, grounded rural life shaped the Roman character: steady, unpretentious, hardworking and disciplined.

A common contrast puts it this way: the Greeks were a nation gazing upward at the stars, romantic and full of imagination. The Romans were a nation rooted firmly in the earth, focused on practical outcomes.

The Greeks left behind a rich legacy of literature, sculpture, athletic games and philosophy, steeped in romantic flair. The Romans cared far less for refined artistic expression. They prioritized tangible results.

What the Romans perfected — their legal frameworks, systems of governance and massive public engineering projects — all served practical ends, built on a grand scale. This stood apart from the delicate, ethereal style of Greek culture. Rome did not produce as many graceful works of art, yet it built an empire stretching across vast territories.
