In 1999, Eberhard Diepgen sat chatting amiably with Jean-Claude Juncker in Luxembourg. The two men bantered about everything from childhood eraser-stealing pranks to the twilight of old age, rambling on for hours. The Luxembourg prime minister left the conversation feeling bewildered—he sensed Diepgen had an ulterior motive behind all the small talk, but could not quite put his finger on it. Finally, he cut straight to the point: “Look, you did not come all this way just to chat, did you? Spit it out. If there is something I can do for you, I have got your back.”

Diepgen laughed it off, saying it was nothing serious. There was just one German citizen who had ended up in Luxembourg by chance, died there, and been buried locally. Germany wanted to bring their countryman home.

Juncker breathed a sigh of relief. “That is it? You did not need to make the trip yourself for a simple exhumation. After World War II, Europe is full of fallen Nazis—there are more buried around here than air in a bag of Lay’s. Any patch of dirt has got a few. Who do you want moved?”

Diepgen grinned. “You are a straight shooter, Prime Minister. You are right about the exhumation—but this man was not a Nazi. His name is Friedrich Wilhelm Voigt.”

Juncker froze. “I am afraid my government must refuse your request. We cannot comply.”

Diepgen’s tone turned sharp. “Prime Minister, you just said you would help. You are going back on your word?”

Juncker’s expression turned grave. “You can move as many Nazi graves as you want. But the Captain of Köpenick was a Luxembourg citizen. You will not take him.”

Friedrich Wilhelm Voigt was born in 1849 to a shoemaker’s family in Tilsit, East Prussia. To say his early life was brutal would be an understatement—it was so bleak it hardly feels fair to joke about it. Yet for all his humble origins, Voigt spent his life fighting, stubborn and unyielding.

His father was not just a shoemaker, but a compulsive gambler. Decent people usually excel at one thing; rotten ones tend to be terrible across the board. On top of gambling, the man drank heavily and beat his family. Voigt’s childhood was a cycle of going hungry three days out of every three, with a beating thrown in for good measure.

At 14, he had had enough. He ran away to Königsberg. No sooner had he arrived than the police picked him up. It was not for any crime he had committed—Königsberg’s police force was so corrupt and incompetent it made the CIA look like a charity. With terrible detective skills and quotas to fill, they regularly framed innocent people to close cases. Young Voigt was thrown in a cell and forced to confess to stealing clothes from a local nobleman.

He refused at first, but after a thorough “memory recovery session” at the hands of the police, he caved. The charge was minor, all things considered—two days in solitary confinement. It was his first time in prison. It would not be his last. For the rest of his life, he would bounce between cells and the outside world, rarely free for long.

With East Prussia unlivable, Voigt tried to flee to the Russian Empire, only to panic and hurry back to Germany. After weighing his options, he headed for Berlin to stay with his aunt.

Luck was on his side for once. His aunt took him in and got him a job at a shoemaker’s workshop. For the first time, his life felt stable. Voigt was careful with money; before long, he had savings and even lent a friend three thalers. A few months later, the friend paid him back with a money order, redeemable at the post office counter.

Staring at the “3” printed on the order, Voigt got a crooked idea. What if he added a digit in front? Would that not mean more money?

He acted fast. He bought ink, scrawled a “2” before the “3”, and took the doctored order to the post office. Half an hour later, he walked out with 23 thalers. For all its industrial prowess, German paper security of the era was shockingly flimsy.

Voigt was ecstatic. He had found a new line of work. He began forging and altering money orders regularly, getting away with it time after time. But success breeds carelessness, and he soon grew sloppy.

Back then, German money orders were printed in either black or blue ink. One time, too lazy to switch bottles, he altered a black-ink order with blue ink. The clerk spotted the forgery instantly, and he was arrested on the spot. The staff also recognized him from the earlier heist—he was a repeat offender.

Berlin’s court handed down its verdict: ten years in prison, plus a fine of 1,500 thalers. Voigt had made less than 200 thalers total from his scams. He could not pay the fine, so the court kindly added two more years to his sentence to cover it. This was his second prison term. He spent his entire youth behind bars.

He was released in March 1879, at the age of 30. He drifted all the way to Odessa, in the Russian Empire. Odessa was no ordinary city. Alongside Rostov, it was known as one of the twin capitals of the Russian underworld: Rostov for robbery, Odessa for smuggling, a port town built on shady opportunity.

Voigt settled in and put his family trade to use, working his way up to technical manager at a shoe factory. On the side, he made extra money smuggling goods, taking advantage of the city’s port location. Life was good. Then he went to prison again.

This time, it was a setup. His factory’s success had threatened local competitors, who framed him for theft. He served another year behind bars.

It was not a total loss, though. In prison he met a fellow inmate named Kallenberg, a man ruined by his own brother’s betrayal. The two were locked up together, released around the same time, and equally destitute.

With nothing left to lose, they decided to pull off a big score. A robbery.

They were so poor even petty thieves would have taken pity on them. Small-time stickups were not worth the trouble. They aimed high: the provincial treasury of the Poznań region in the Russian Empire, housed inside the courthouse and stuffed with public funds.

They had a target, but a very practical problem: how to rob it? Bank robbery had evolved over the years. The turning point came in 1918, when German robber Hermann Lamm invented the Lamm Technique, rewriting the rulebook and raising the bar for the whole profession. Robbers before him were “classical”; after him, “modern”.

But Voigt was planning his heist in the 1880s, well before Lamm’s time. He was firmly in the classical camp. The classical method was simple and brutal: plant enough dynamite against the bank’s outer wall, blow a hole in it, grab the cash, and run. It was not sophisticated, but to Voigt and Kallenberg, it might as well have been rocket science. Why? They could not afford dynamite.

Fresh out of prison and penniless, they could not even buy proper tools, let alone explosives. The plan seemed dead in the water—until Voigt spoke up. “The best crimes,” he said, “only need the simplest tools.” He walked into a hardware store and walked out with a crowbar. “This will do.”

That night, they snuck into the courthouse, found the treasury vault, and set to work prying the door open. All that cash was just on the other side. They heaved and strained at the door with the crowbar, metal screeching against metal, sparks flying. Maybe prison had rotted their common sense: Voigt seemed to have forgotten that no vault door in the world can be pried open with a crowbar.

Driven by greed, they kept at it. The noise drew the police, who surrounded the treasury room with the two men trapped inside.

Cornered, Kallenberg snapped. “Let’s fight our way out!”

“With what? A crowbar?” Voigt said. “Surrender. It is just attempted robbery. More prison time, that is all. I have done enough time to know how it goes.”

He spoke too soon. Russian police and officials burst in, arrested them, then proceeded to open the vault right in front of them. They hauled out huge stacks of cash and declared the two men responsible for massive state losses, a crime of the highest order.

Voigt wanted to cry. If we are talking robbery, you guys are the professionals. What should have been attempted robbery was upgraded to full-scale grand larceny with a huge sum involved. Voigt got 15 years. It was his fourth prison sentence. This time he was sent to the infamous Lavis Prison in Russia, and barely survived.

In 1906, at the age of 57, he was finally released. He had spent nearly 30 years behind bars in total. Free again, he had no grand schemes left—he just wanted a quiet, honest life. But back in Germany, he could not settle anywhere. City after city issued him residency bans, thanks to his long rap sheet.

Desperate, he ended up in Berlin, where he noticed something about the society around him. This was the German Empire, 1906—a state steeped in militarism, where soldiers held immense status and power, able to stride through life unchallenged.

Voigt watched as arrogant officials and policemen groveled before anyone in uniform. Landlords who had hounded him to move out would rush to the door the second a soldier wanted to rent a room, tripping over themselves to carry their luggage.

In that atmosphere, an idea took root—one that would change the rest of his life. He had heard of a town called Köpenick, east of Berlin. In early October 1906, he got to work. He took the train to Köpenick to case the town hall, and scouted several army barracks on the outskirts of Berlin.

On October 9, he bought a second-hand uniform from a pawn shop: the dress uniform of a captain in the 1st Prussian Guards Foot Regiment. This was an elite imperial unit, its officers almost all old Junker nobility.

The operation began. At noon that day, Voigt put on the uniform and stationed himself near an army bathhouse. He stopped five soldiers coming back from their wash—military policemen from the Friedrich Guards Regiment, a crack unit.

Voigt did not flinch. In a sharp, authoritative officer’s tone, he ordered the sergeant in charge: “Take me to your barracks at once. I am requisitioning eleven men for an urgent mission from the Imperial Cabinet. This is a matter of the highest priority. Disobedience will be severely punished.”

Regulations required a written order to deploy troops. But the sergeant took one look at the man’s age, his rank, the prestigious regiment number, and assumed he was some old Berlin Junker aristocrat. He did not dare ask for papers. He led Voigt to the firing range, rounded up six more soldiers to add to the original five, and presented him with a full squad of eleven.

Voigt marched his eleven men to Berlin’s train station and bought eleven tickets to Köpenick. The soldiers were confused. Army missions used military trains, did they not? Why were they taking a civilian train?

Voigt answered calmly: “This is a secret mission. We cannot afford to draw attention and tip off our target. Arriving in a military convoy would defeat the purpose.”

The soldiers nodded, satisfied. No more questions. Voigt added, approvingly: “You are all sharp men—true pillars of the Empire. Do this well, and you will be well rewarded.”

When they arrived in Köpenick, Voigt gave each man one mark to buy lunch, and treated everyone to a large beer. Fed and watered, he ordered the squad to fall in and laid out their mission.

The soldiers sprang into action. They sealed off every entrance to the town hall, then kicked down the front door and stormed inside. The building had plenty of security—nearly 80 guards and policemen combined. But none of them dared stand up to a squad of imperial grenadiers led by a high-ranking captain.

By coincidence, the Köpenick town hall was hosting a reception that day. Nearly every local official was in attendance, and Voigt rounded them all up in one go. Langerhans, a municipal engineer who had never broken a law in his life, was so terrified he did not make a sound as soldiers pinned him to his chair. Someone had the nerve to ask for an arrest warrant. Voigt roared: “Get out of my sight. This does not concern you.” The police chief turned and ran, not even stopping to call for backup.

With the building fully under his control, Voigt addressed the assembled officials. The Kaiser, he declared, had uncovered solid evidence of corruption. All accounts were to be sealed immediately, the vault opened, and the funds audited. They were going to find out exactly how much public money these men had embezzled.

The city treasurer, shaking like a leaf, unlocked the vault and handed over the ledgers. The captain went through the books publicly, and found a discrepancy of 1.67 marks—barely enough for two breakfasts. Before anyone could comment, Voigt flew into a rage.

“One point six seven marks is still imperial money!” he shouted. “Not a single mark of the Empire’s funds is trivial! Langerhans, you scoundrel—embezzling such a vast sum? You are guilty beyond measure!”

“I am seizing all accounts and cash on hand,” he announced, “and will report to Berlin immediately.” By law, removing public funds like this was strictly forbidden, but the treasurer did not dare stop him. He only begged for a receipt.

Voigt obliged. He wrote out a slip: *Received and seized from the Köpenick municipal treasury: 3557.45 marks. Seized by: Captain von Malzahn, 1st Imperial Guards Foot Regiment.*

Who was von Malzahn? He had been the warden during Voigt’s fourth prison sentence.

With the money in hand, Voigt ordered the soldiers to lock down the town hall, let no one in or out, and cut all telephone lines. Then he walked out the front door with the cash and ledgers, climbed into a horse-drawn cab, and vanished.

The officials inside stared at each other in disbelief. They waited for hours, but the captain never came back. Finally, the secretary got permission from the soldiers to call Berlin and confirm the order. When they learned the truth, everyone was stunned.

The absurd crime shocked all of Germany, then spread across Europe. Ordinary Germans howled with laughter at how gullible the bureaucrats and army had been.

Voigt made it back to his Berlin home calmly. The haul was not the rumored two million marks, but it was the first successful heist of his life. He watched as the reward for his capture climbed from 500 to 3000 marks in the newspapers, and felt a strange pride.

For all his flaws, Voigt had a sense of loyalty. He remembered his old friend Kallenberg, still poor and struggling, and gave him some of the money. It was a kindness he would regret for the rest of his life. To Kallenberg, 3000 marks was worth more than their friendship. He turned Voigt in to the police. The cops followed the tip and arrested him easily. So much for brothers in arms.

In custody, Voigt confessed to everything. But at his trial, things took a dramatic turn. The case was so bizarre, so widely reported across Europe, that Voigt did not come across as a vicious criminal—he was a folk hero, a man who had pricked the pomposity of German bureaucracy and militarism, and given the common people a good laugh. With public opinion running so strongly in his favor, the courts could hardly throw the book at him.

In December 1906, prosecutors charged him only with minor offenses: wearing an officer’s uniform without permission, and forgery of documents. He was sentenced to four years in prison. The irony was bitter: for most of his life, Voigt had been given harsh sentences for petty crimes. This, the biggest stunt of all, got off light.

The case became a national joke. The officials of Köpenick were mocked endlessly. People traveled from all over just to see the site of the legendary heist. Köpenick Town Hall became one of Germany’s most famous tourist attractions, so much so that the local government eventually moved its offices elsewhere.

Voigt was a celebrity now, and prison treated him well. On the day he arrived, crowds of citizens and reporters gathered to gawk at the audacious outlaw. The story only grew bigger. In 1908, Kaiser Wilhelm II himself read the case file and laughed out loud. The impulsive emperor called Voigt a remarkable man, and said the story proved just how disciplined the German people were.

In August 1908, Voigt was released from prison to a hero’s welcome. Reporters and onlookers packed the streets. That same afternoon, he was paid 100 to 200 marks to record a speech on a phonograph, released to his adoring public.

Over the next few days, Berlin police were run ragged. Wherever Voigt went, crowds of fans swarmed him. In just two days, police arrested 17 people for causing disturbances. Four days after his release, a Berlin wax museum added a full-size wax figure of Voigt, placing him higher in status than many contemporary celebrities.

Now a national superstar, Voigt took his show on tour. He reenacted the town hall raid on stages across Germany, sold signed photographs. Best of all, several of the soldiers he had commanded that day joined the act too—the whole gang reunited for the performance. The show was a smash hit in Germany, then toured Europe and even made it to the United States. Voigt made a fortune from the performances, earning well over a million marks. He became a household name: the Captain of Köpenick.

Rich at last, the Captain never had to worry about money again.

In May 1910, Voigt emigrated to Luxembourg. After a lifetime of hardship, his bizarre late-life crime had turned his fortunes around. In Luxembourg, he was wealthy—one of the first people in the country to own a car. He would often drive the children of local nobles on day trips. Later he bought a two-story house: he lived upstairs, and ran a small cobbler’s shop downstairs to pass the time, living out his days in peace.

When World War I broke out, German troops passed through the area. An army captain had the old man arrested, saying he had an old score to settle. It turned out the captain was the same sergeant Voigt had commandeered all those years ago, now back to pull a prank of his own.

On January 3, 1922, Voigt died in Luxembourg at the age of 72.

His neighbors gave him one final joke. On the way to the cemetery, the funeral procession met a company of French soldiers. Their sergeant asked casually: “Who is the big shot getting all this send-off?”

“Captain von Köpenick,” a neighbor replied.

The Frenchman heard the “von” and assumed he was a German Junker aristocrat. Following European military custom, he ordered his men to line the road and stand at attention, saluting the casket with full military honors as it passed to its grave.

His tombstone is carved with the figure of a soldier in a German helmet, barking orders. In life and in death, his story is known across Europe. From 1908 to the present day, it has been adapted into countless films, plays and books. In 1996, a statue of Voigt was erected in front of Köpenick Town Hall, and the city created a local festival in his honor. Every year, visitors dress up as Prussian captains and reenact the “raid on the town hall” for fun.
