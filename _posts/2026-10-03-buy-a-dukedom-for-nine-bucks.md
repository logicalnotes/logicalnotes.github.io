In the province of Imperia in northwest Italy sits Seborga, a small mountain town. It was once just another quiet Italian village, until an accidental discovery in 1963 changed its fate forever.

That year, a young man named Giorgio Carbone was going through old parish records written by the village priest. He stumbled upon a forgotten piece of history: Seborga had once been a tiny independent principality. From the 11th century onward, it was a vassal of the Holy Roman Empire, then swore allegiance to the House of Savoy of Italy.

Yet even though the House of Savoy ruled Seborga in practice, the tiny territory was never formally added to their official list of sovereign lands. Legally speaking, Seborga’s sovereignty was never transferred to Italy — it remained an independent principality on paper.

This story drives home one simple lesson: keeping proper books matters more than you’d think.

After uncovering this historical quirk, Carbone called on all villagers to break away from Italy and declare independence.

A proposal this absurd would normally get brushed off without a second thought. But Seborga’s residents liked the idea. After a vote among its 300-odd residents, Seborga formally declared independence in 1963 and founded the Principality of Seborga. Its population stood at around 300, with territory spanning four square kilometres.

The man who unearthed the secret was chosen as head of state. The principality crowned him their monarch, Giorgio I.

Once in power, Giorgio I focused on three main tasks: designing Seborga’s passports, stamps and currency to build a full set of sovereign symbols. He also set up a flower farmers’ cooperative to support local growers.

In 1995, Seborga took the act a step further. It adopted a constitution, claimed itself a sovereign entity separate from Italy, formed an eight-member cabinet and a 24-seat parliament.

Even with a parliament in place, no one questioned Giorgio I’s authority. Seborga’s officials could run businesses alongside public posts: the town restaurant owner doubled as foreign minister, the grocery shopkeeper served as finance minister, a cyclist took the sports portfolio, and an engineer looked after urban affairs. The principality also raised an army of fewer than ten men to guard its so-called borders and customs posts. They wore matching uniforms, though carried no weapons.

You might wonder: Seborga sits entirely within Italian land. Why did Italy never shut down this self-proclaimed nation?

Italy has long grown used to such oddities. Within its borders lie Vatican City and San Marino, both UN-recognised sovereign states. One more self-amusing micro-nation like Seborga hardly bothered them. What’s more, for all its talk of independence, Seborga still paid taxes to Italy, leaving no clear reason for intervention.

Most importantly, Seborga’s “independence” brought a flood of tourists. Visitors could get a souvenir entry stamp. Shopping at the “finance minister’s” grocery store let you browse what felt like a state-run shop.

In 2006, 70-year-old Giorgio I stepped down after 43 years on the throne. He passed away in 2009, and real estate developer Marcello Menegatto succeeded him as Marcello I.

Seborga’s tale is mostly a happy one. It turned an obscure mountain village into a tourist hotspot and boosted the local economy. But the next two private nations ended far less well.

In 1970, Australian farmer Leonard Casley flew off the handle and declared independence.

The trigger was a failed negotiation with local authorities over wheat quotas. He gathered his family and farm workers, claimed his farm as national territory, and founded the Principality of Hutt River.

The nation lay hundreds of kilometres north of Perth in Western Australia. At its founding, it had just 23 residents, but covered 75 square kilometres — bigger than the combined area of Vatican City, Monaco, Seborga and Nauru. It was a superpower among micro-nations.

Yet across all that land stood only two buildings. One housed the royal palace, central government and post office. The other served as tourism department, warehouse and souvenir shop.

After founding the principality, Hutt River printed its own banknotes, stamps and passports. It also announced it would no longer pay Australian taxes.

The Australian government’s stance was clear: taxes must be paid in full. Unpaid dues would accrue penalties.

At first, Hutt River was just an in-farm hobby, and officials paid little mind. That changed in the late 1980s when a young man named Kevin Gale came to serve King Leonard. Leonard made him a duke, then regent in charge of finances.

With control over the purse strings, Kevin Gale launched bold schemes. He ignored inflation and printed large volumes of Hutt River currency. He sold noble titles to buyers across Australia, Europe and America, and hosted fundraisers and celebrations, raking in large profits. It was under him that Hutt River gained international fame, known to many older Australians.

By the 1990s, the powerful regent plotted a coup. In 1995, he planned to buy an island in the Pacific, leave the farm behind, and build a truly independent Hutt River state. Before the coup could unfold, Kevin Gale fell ill and died.

As soon as he died, Leonard stripped Gale of all titles and offices, wiping him entirely from official records. History, indeed, is written by the victors.

Though the regent was erased from official history, his commercial legacy kept Hutt River thriving. Fame drew tens of thousands of tourists each year. Into the 2000s, the principality kept earning from tourism, refining its flag, coat of arms, currency and custom licence plates. It even set up a so-called national university.

The principality issued more than 14,000 passports in total. Strangely enough, some of these documents were once accepted by border control in more than a dozen countries including the US and France. Back then, visitors paid four Australian dollars to “enter” Hutt River, and King Leonard personally greeted and guided them. Few places offered a private audience with a head of state.

Inside the “central government office” for visitors worked just two staff: King Leonard’s son and daughter. The prince and princess doubled as customs chiefs, postmasters, tourism officers, souvenir sellers and office cleaners. State and family were fully merged, keeping them swamped with duties. Outside the government building stood a large statue of founder Leonard.

In 2017, old King Leonard passed away. His son Graham Casley took the throne. He is remembered as the last ruler, for Hutt River collapsed under his reign, brought down by financial ruin.

Australian authorities insisted the Casley family must pay taxes. The principality refused to recognise this obligation. Back in 1977, Leonard even sent a declaration of war to Australia, though quickly withdrew it before conflict broke out. For years, Leonard fought court battles in Australia to win tax exemption, but lost every time.

In 2017, the court ruled against the Casley family, ordering them to pay three million Australian dollars in back taxes and fines. Then the COVID-19 pandemic hit in 2020, bringing global tourism to a near halt. Stripped of its main income stream, Hutt River’s finances crumbled completely.

In December 2020, King Graham abdicated. The Principality of Hutt River was formally dissolved, and its land returned to Australia to settle outstanding tax debts. Hutt River became the world’s first private nation to fall because of COVID, after 50 years of existence.

Among all micro-nations, none is more famous than the Principality of Sealand. It is also the smallest, covering merely 550 square metres — smaller than a standard football pitch.

In 1942, Britain built dozens of offshore sea forts to fend off German attacks. Strictly speaking, this construction violated parts of international law at the time, yet wartime Britain had other priorities. Roughs Tower was one such sea fortress: built on a repurposed barge base with two towers on top. During WWII, between 150 and 300 British troops were stationed here across its 550 square metres. When the war ended, soldiers pulled out and left the tower abandoned. Sitting out at sea, Britain did not blow it up, leaving the structure standing. That oversight would spark decades of disputes.

In 1967, former British army major Paddy Roy Bates occupied the derelict tower. Using his own reading of international law, he claimed sovereignty over the tower and the 12 nautical miles of sea around it, boasting unobstructed 360-degree ocean views.

Bates had not set out to found a country at first. His original plan was to run a pirate radio station. Britain was cracking down on unlicensed radio broadcasts back then. Bates and his wife loved music and wanted a place free from regulation to play rock and talk shows. They found Roughs Tower, seized it, and broadcast programmes to ships passing offshore.

After running it for a while with almost no interference, they grew bolder. On 2 September 1967, watched by family and supporters, Bates proclaimed the Principality of Sealand. He crowned himself Prince Roy, his wife Joan became Princess Joan, and the pair ruled jointly as co-monarchs.

For a time, their antics went unnoticed. That changed in 1968 when a British patrol boat approached the tower. The boat had no specific mission targeting Sealand, yet Prince Roy and his son Michael fired warning shots and threw petrol bombs to force the vessel to retreat.

After this “defence of the realm”, Bates sought formal diplomacy, demanding Britain recognise Sealand’s sovereignty. The British government was stunned to find some of its own citizens had set up a kingdom on a wartime British sea fort and wanted diplomatic ties. Britain dispatched helicopters and speedboats. Troops boarded Roughs Tower, arrested Roy Bates and Michael, and brought them back to Britain for trial.

Britain follows common law based on precedents. Courts searched through legal records and found no existing case law for “founding a nation on an abandoned sea fort”. In the end, Bates and his son were acquitted and set free.

Bates read the not-guilty verdict as British acceptance of Sealand’s sovereignty. He then ran the country in earnest, issuing currency and stamps, gaining support from sailors and pirate radio operators.

Where there is a government, power struggles follow. Even with fewer than five permanent residents, Sealand suffered a coup.

In 1978, Alexander Achenbach, the prime minister Roy Bates had appointed, took advantage of Roy’s absence. He hired German and Dutch mercenaries to seize Roughs Tower, captured Prince Michael, and took control of Sealand.

But founder Roy Bates would not surrender. He recruited men in Britain, flew in by helicopter to storm the tower, reclaimed Sealand and arrested the prime minister and mercenaries. The hired fighters were expelled. The traitor Achenbach was convicted of treason and imprisoned on the tower.

Achenbach was a West German citizen. West Germany sent diplomats to negotiate with Bates. After months of talks, Achenbach was allowed to return to Germany. Roy Bates claimed these diplomatic meetings amounted to official recognition of Sealand. West German diplomats never confirmed this.

Prince Roy then travelled across Europe on commercial flights for years, lobbying foreign governments to recognise Sealand. Britain even sent agents to track him, to stop him securing diplomatic recognition.

People thought the troubles were over, yet after returning to West Germany, Achenbach set up a Sealand government-in-exile. He named himself prime minister and head of the privy council, claiming full authority over Sealand’s government. The 550-square-metre “nation” now had two rival administrations.

For decades afterward, the legitimate government and government-in-exile fought over legal claims. The exile group was far more aggressive in commercial schemes. Sealand’s home government issued fewer than 100 passports total, while the exile regime mass-produced and sold more than 150,000 fake Sealand passports. Later, a murder suspect was caught carrying one of these exile-issued passports. This forced the original Sealand government to declare all Sealand passports invalid.

In 2005, Sealand composed a wordless national anthem. It also tried a new business venture: hosting servers on the tower for pirated online content.

In 2006, Roughs Tower caught fire and nearly destroyed the micro-nation. British fire crews sailed across the sea to put out the blaze and save the structure.

After the fire, Prince Michael, the second ruler, raised reconstruction funds by boosting tourism and selling noble ranks. Online, you can buy a Sealand noble title for as little as nine US dollars, probably the easiest way for an ordinary person to own aristocratic status.

To visit Roughs Tower in person, you need to email a booking at least one month in advance. Once five visitors sign up, Prince Michael meets the group at a pub in mainland England, rents a boat, and captains it to the tower. Few heads of state personally ferry their guests; Sealand lacks money to hire boatmen, so whatever vessel they board depends on what Michael can rent that day.

Once ashore, visitors get souvenir entry stamps and can look around. The so-called foreign minister pitches nobility packages. A prince title tops the list at 2,000 British pounds. They also sell plots of the claimed territorial sea.

After the fire, the royal family grew discouraged and once considered abandoning their realm. They placed an ad in The Times offering the sovereignty and throne of Sealand for sale at 65 million British pounds. Hundreds enquired, yet no buyer ever made a serious offer.
