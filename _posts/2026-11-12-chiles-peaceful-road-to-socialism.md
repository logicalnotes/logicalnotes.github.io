There is a famous Chilean song, *El Pueblo Unido Jamás Será Vencido*, written by poet and composer Sergio Ortega. Its most iconic line — “The people united will never be defeated” — is so widely known that many mistake it for the song’s actual title.

In 1970, another Chilean poet, Víctor Martínez, was invited to rewrite parts of it as the anthem for a left-wing electoral coalition: Popular Unity, led by Salvador Allende.

Chile is a country that flies under the radar yet is instantly recognisable to anyone who has glanced at a map. Stretching along South America’s Pacific coast, it covers 756,000 square kilometres. Its shape is extraordinary: more than 4,300 kilometres from north to south, with an average width of just 180 kilometres. It looks almost like a phone case glued onto the edge of the continent. I have long wondered: could this oddly-shaped nation ever split apart along its narrow east-west axis?

Nature has been generous to Chile. For all its strange geography, it holds staggering reserves of copper, silver and nitrate. Conditions for farming and livestock are solid. Compared with many impoverished nations across Africa, Asia and Latin America, Chile had natural wealth that should have made life easier.

By the numbers, Chile once looked prosperous. Back in the 1950s, its per capita income hit 650 USD — a figure respectable not just within Latin America, but by European standards of the time. You might assume a country this well-off would have little appetite for left-wing politics. So why was a socialist candidate on the brink of winning the presidency?

The answer lay further north: the United States. After the Second World War, US firms controlled roughly 70 percent of Chile’s mineral wealth. Foreign capital dominated communications, power grids and transport. Under Washington’s influence, a local comprador elite hoarded most of the country’s gains. Average national income meant almost nothing for ordinary working Chileans.

By the late 1960s, Chile had a population of 9.62 million. Thirty thousand people held more than half the national income, while the remaining 9.3 million shared the other half. The nation functioned as a feast for a tiny few, while the rest laboured for foreign corporations for meagre reward.

Chileans did resist this arrangement before, but its political landscape was hopelessly fragmented. No movement serious about breaking US dominance could climb to power. Minerals kept flowing out of Chile, yet ordinary people stayed trapped.

For decades, the United States kept a firm grip on Chile and the wider Latin American backyard. But by the late 1960s, Washington grew nervous about one man: Salvador Allende.

Salvador Allende was born in 1908 into a distinguished Chilean family, often described in shorthand as middle-class. His roots ran deep in national history: his great-grandfather fought alongside San Martín in Chile’s war of independence; his grandfather was an educator; his father, a prominent lawyer who regularly took cases for poor people.

Allende’s father’s work kept the family moving across Chile, so Allende grew up seeing the country’s brutal inequality first-hand, sheltered from no comfortable bubble.

As a teenager, he formed an unlikely friendship with an Italian artisan named De Marchi, a fierce advocate for workers and the poor. Through De Marchi, Allende first encountered Marxist thought.

After secondary school, he served in the military, then enrolled to study medicine at the University of Chile. Doctors in the 20th century often leaned left, just as many lawyers leaned right. At university, Allende read Lenin and Trotsky, led student protests, and was repeatedly jailed.

In 1932, his father died while Allende was locked up. He was granted temporary release for the funeral. Standing there, he settled on a conviction: medicine alone could not save Chile. He would dedicate his life to the socialist cause.

It would have been logical for him to join the Communist Party — yet he never did. For a time, he was a Freemason, drawn to its spiritual tradition. In 1933, he helped found the Chilean Socialist Party, while continuing to work in public health.

He built a reputation as a respected doctor, and slowly shaped his political project: **democratic socialism**.

What did that mean? He wanted to work within Chile’s existing constitutional framework, win power through elections, and gradually transform the bourgeois constitution into a socialist one — all by peaceful means.

This was not pure fantasy. Chile was unusual in Latin America for its stable constitutional order. Its constitution had operated continuously since 1830, for more than a century, with governments formed through elections. Unlike armies elsewhere in the region, the Chilean military had long been highly professional, bound to political neutrality.

That same stable system, of course, had also let the United States drain Chile’s resources year after year. Still, Allende, a doctor who rejected violence, believed electoral politics was the only viable path.

He entered politics in 1937 and soon became Minister of Health and Social Welfare. In office, he pushed for workplace safety protections, built support systems for women, introduced free school meals, and founded Chile’s national health service.

His strong record won him a seat in the Senate. As a senator, he visited China and met China’s first-generation leaders; he travelled to Cuba in 1959 and met Fidel Castro; in 1967 he went to Moscow to meet Brezhnev.

By 1969, the 61-year-old senator had visited most socialist states, observing and studying different paths to build socialism. That same year, he resolved to run for president.

He had already tried three times before: 1952, 1958 and 1964. Each time he lost. Standing in his way was the United States.

At first, Washington wrote Allende off. Plenty of leftists ranted in the US backyard; none would really take power. But the Cuban Revolution shocked American planners. Communism could win in Latin America.

The US had spent decades propping up oligarchs and dictators across the region. Washington would back almost any tyrant who safeguarded US interests. But those regimes were deeply unpopular. A leader riding genuine mass support, like Castro, could overthrow them.

So when Allende declared his 1964 presidential run, the White House recognised him as more than another candidate. The CIA went all out to stop him.

For the 1964 election, the CIA poured 3 million USD into a nationwide smear campaign. Newspapers, magazines, leaflets, every available ad space: all deployed to destroy Allende’s reputation.

The campaign was clumsy, filtered through American cultural assumptions. Allende was popular with women voters, so the CIA leaned heavily on rumours of extramarital affairs. In 1960s America, still steeped in puritan social norms, that was a deadly accusation. In Latin America, it landed differently. Allende shrugged and openly acknowledged it, joking that he was simply affectionate. Chilean voters did not treat it as scandal.

Part of his broad appeal to women came from his advocacy for women’s rights, part from his natural charisma. Looks carry weight in politics, for better or worse. Some photographs read like fashion spreads; others look like missing-person notices. Think of revolutionary iconography: Che Guevara’s image adorns shirts everywhere, yet Trotsky, whose theoretical contribution to the workers’ movement was vastly larger, rarely appears on merchandise.

Seeing the smear backfire, the CIA shifted tactics. They warned voters that Allende would replicate Stalin’s USSR: build gulags, tear children away from their mothers. Allende quipped back: if anything, he was far more interested in the mothers than seizing their children.

They dug up childhood photos where Allende had soft, delicate features, looking like a little girl, and printed them on flyers: “See this sweet little girl? He is now a father of three. Do you want him ruling you?”

The CIA’s campaign was enough. Allende lost the 1964 election. Agency officials breathed out. After three defeats, surely Allende, growing older, would step away. Even Allende joked to friends that his tombstone might read: *Here lies Chile’s next president*.

Then 1970 arrived, and he launched his fourth presidential bid. Chile’s political parties were splintered, none commanding majority support on their own. Every serious contender ran as part of a coalition.

At 62, Allende united the Communist Party of Chile, the Radical Party, the Social Democratic Party and other centre-left groups under Popular Unity. Richard Nixon occupied the White House. Nixon viewed Chile as critical. A second socialist victory in Latin America after Cuba could spark a regional wildfire. Nixon issued a hard order: stop Allende from taking office, by any means necessary.

Henry Kissinger summed up the mindset: “We cannot allow a country, because its people are irresponsible, to fall into communist hands.” Translated plainly: even if Allende won through free democratic voting, it was not the democracy Washington wanted.

But the local elite had extracted so much wealth from ordinary Chileans that US smears no longer shifted public opinion.

On September 4, 1970, the election results were counted. Allende won 36.61 percent of the vote, finishing first, narrowly ahead of former president Jorge Alessandri. Under Chile’s rules, congressional confirmation was the final step before inauguration.

When Nixon learned Allende led the popular vote, he flew into a rage. Staff later said they had never seen him angrier. He ordered his team to block the confirmation at all costs: spend 10 million USD, more if needed, even if the odds were only one in ten.

In this fury, the CIA turned to the only remaining playbook: a military coup.

Yet staging a coup in Chile looked nearly impossible. The military had a long tradition of neutrality. The Commander-in-Chief of the Army, General René Schneider, firmly rejected coup plotting and watched conspirators closely.

With no senior military leader willing to act legally against the election result, the CIA drafted a three-point plan:

1. Keep gathering intelligence and find officers willing to launch a coup.
2. Spread rumours, run scare campaigns and stage violent provocations to create public panic, fertile ground for a takeover.
3. Promise sympathetic officers full US backing for a coup, even though US troops would not deploy directly.

There is a grim, striking quality to how the US operated. They wrote these extreme plans down on paper, records that would later become public.

Unable to recruit a credible senior commander, the CIA settled on two flawed figures: retired general Roberto Viaux and Santiago garrison commander General Camilo Valenzuela.

Schneider stayed vigilant. He uncovered and blocked Viaux’s initial coup scheme. The CIA knew Schneider stood in their way, yet hesitated to order the assassination of the army’s top general directly.

The CIA might hold back, but the two plotters grew impatient.

On October 13, 1970, Viaux told the CIA he planned to kidnap Schneider and his deputy Carlos Prats.

Congress would vote to confirm Allende on October 24. Time was running out.

The CIA replied that the plan was being reviewed; a kidnapping now had little chance of success. They urged him to lie low and wait. In plain language: his plot was underprepared, reckless, likely to fail.

But Viaux feared Valenzuela, who was also talking to the CIA and preparing his own kidnapping attempt. The two men were rivals, both chasing power in the future junta. To strike first and claim credit, Viaux set his kidnapping for October 22, just two days before the congressional vote.

Valenzuela also raced to beat him, moving his operation forward to October 19. That attempt failed completely: Schneider rode in a private car instead of his official vehicle, and the kidnappers missed him.

After seeing how clumsy both men were, the CIA judged them liabilities and cut off contact with both.

Even abandoned by the CIA, the pair pressed ahead.

On the morning of October 22, Viaux’s men intercepted General Schneider’s car on his commute, intending to seize him. Schneider recognised the threat instantly and drew his sidearm. A gunfight erupted. Outnumbered, he was struck in the chest and collapsed.

Three days later, on October 25, he died in hospital. His last words: **Defend the constitution.**

Schneider’s death shook Chile to its core. Police arrested Viaux and sentenced him to twenty years in prison; Valenzuela was captured and exiled for three years. Investigations uncovered the CIA links, which became public knowledge. The nation erupted in anger. Hundreds of thousands lined the streets for Schneider’s funeral.

His death destroyed the CIA’s plot to derail the transfer of power. It cleared Allende’s path.

On October 24, Congress voted to confirm Salvador Allende as President of Chile.

In his inaugural speech, Allende declared Chile would advance toward socialism without violent revolution. “We will reach it through red wine and empanadas,” he famously said. Chile carried a global mission: to test a second path for socialist transition.

Poor Chileans celebrated. Millions believed a new era had begun.

Thousands of kilometres away in Washington, senior officials watched the inauguration grimly. They resolved to strangle this new left-wing government. Nixon’s instruction: make Chile’s economy scream.

The US ambassador to Chile put it even more bluntly: while Allende remained in office, not one nut, not one screw, should flow into Chile. They would use every tool to push the country and its people into scarcity and poverty.
