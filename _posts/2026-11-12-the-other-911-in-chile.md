In November 1970, with covert backing from the United States, Salvador Allende was elected president of Chile and set out to launch his reforms.

Allende’s brand of democratic socialism was an untested path in the world at that time. He sought to transform Chile gradually into a socialist nation through peaceful, electoral means. This choice grew out of Chile’s social conditions of the era, as well as Allende’s personal temperament. In some ways, he resembled Leonid Brezhnev.

The Russian writer Mlechin summed up Brezhnev’s character in a well-known observation: Brezhnev lived by one simple rule — live and let live. His easygoing, agreeable nature was key to his rise to power. Many saw Brezhnev as mediocre in ability, yet few attacked his integrity. Nearly everyone who met him described him as a decent man. But history has repeatedly shown that a good person does not automatically make a good leader.

For a president who came to power via the ballot box, the ideal scenario was to lift workers’ living standards without eroding the privileges of white-collar professionals. It sounded nearly impossible. As a democratically elected leader, Allende dared not upset Chile’s middle class and domestic capitalists too severely. Still, if he could not target local elites, could he not target foreign ones?

Allende set his sights on the biggest economic players in Chile — and the largest of those were American. Copper formed the backbone of Chile’s economy, and U.S. corporations controlled ninety percent of Chile’s copper mines. Successive governments before Allende had bought minority stakes in some copper operations with large cash payouts. When Allende took office, however, he rejected that model: he would not pay to take back the mines. He nationalized them outright.

In 1971, Allende pushed through copper nationalization. The state seized Chile’s five largest copper mines held by U.S. firms, alongside iron and nitrate mines controlled by American capital. For Chile, reclaiming its copper was as transformative as Panama taking back the Panama Canal. For this reason, July 11, 1971, was designated **National Dignity Day**. On that date, this small South American nation reclaimed its most vital national assets.

Even after seizing U.S.-owned assets, Allende repeatedly stressed that this nationalization was not the same revolutionary collectivization seen in Cuba. He framed the expropriation as a legal, statutory action under Chilean law. His reasoning was straightforward: Chilean commercial custom recognized a fair 12 percent profit for these enterprises, yet American mining firms had extracted far greater returns. Between 1955 and 1975, U.S. mining companies siphoned hundreds of millions of dollars in excess profits out of Chile — sums that already exceeded the book value of the mines themselves. Factoring in environmental harm and disputes left behind by these corporations, Allende argued, Chile was being restrained by not demanding reparations from the American operators.

When this rationale became public, Washington reacted with fury. From the U.S. perspective, seizing assets was one thing; justifying the seizure with a coherent legal argument was far more dangerous. If other Latin American nations copied Chile’s example, American economic interests across the region would face cascading losses.

With copper revenues now flowing into state coffers, Allende gained the fiscal room to roll out the sweeping social reforms he had long envisioned.

First came education reform. Allende viewed universal access to education as the foundation of Chile’s transition toward socialism. His administration launched a nationwide literacy campaign, sending teams deep into mountain and rural communities to teach reading and writing. Funding for teachers rose sharply, primary education expanded, and more than one hundred new schools were built. Enrollment in preschools and universities doubled, while attendance at primary and secondary schools climbed substantially.

Next, he strengthened public healthcare. Building on prior initiatives, the state poured more resources into rural medical infrastructure. Infant mortality fell, and average life expectancy rose.

The centerpiece of Allende’s agenda was a dramatic improvement in working-class conditions. Chilean laborers who had spent decades toiling for U.S. companies won new rights to participate in factory governance. Wages across the workforce rose by 35 to 40 percent, supplemented by a suite of state subsidies. The government capped prices on staple goods, expanded public housing, lowered rents, distributed free food to poor households, and provided free milk for children. These policies delivered tangible, immediate gains for ordinary Chileans.

Allende’s first year in office brought a period of prosperity: GDP grew by 8.6 percent, inflation fell by 13 percent, more than 200,000 new jobs were created, unemployment hit a historic low, and the state broke ground on dozens of infrastructure projects. Allende’s famous phrase captured the vision: they were building socialism with red wine and pastries.

Yet Fidel Castro, Allende’s close friend, voiced a prescient concern. Socialist revolution, Castro argued, must transform production by reshaping relations of work and ownership. Allende’s program, by contrast, looked much more like a consumption revolution: it promised people more goods and benefits, without answering who would produce them.

Allende was not indifferent to production. He was simply overwhelmed by competing crises. Beyond nationalization and welfare expansion, he spent endless energy managing bitter infighting within his governing coalition.

Allende owed his presidency to the Popular Unity coalition, a loose bloc of disparate parties assembled solely to win the election. It was not a unified, disciplined ruling party, and internal factional clashes never stopped. The core dispute centered on one question: what path should Chile’s socialist revolution follow, and what goals should it pursue?

Moderates, led by the Communist Party of Chile, argued for steady, parliamentary reform. They favored winning change through legislation, bringing parts of the middle class and domestic business owners on board, rolling out nationalization and land reform gradually, and treating inflation as a serious threat.

Radicals, clustered mainly in the Socialist Party, pushed for rapid, sweeping land redistribution, worker-led social change, mass strikes and street mobilization, and, if necessary, armed struggle. They believed Chile’s entire state apparatus served capitalist interests and that genuine revolution could never be achieved through elections alone. Inflation, in their view, was an unavoidable cost of transformation.

Though both factions belonged to the left, their positions were directly opposed. Curiously, their foreign policy stances flipped: moderates favored close alignment with the Soviet Union, while radicals advocated for independent self-determination, open to Soviet friendship but rejecting subservience.

Many readers ask why the Soviet Union did not intervene forcefully to support Allende as U.S. pressure mounted. Was Moscow unwilling, or were there practical barriers?

The Soviet-Chile relationship was deeply ambivalent.
First, the USSR never fully trusted Allende. He was not a communist, and he repeatedly insisted Chile would not copy the Soviet or Cuban models. Under Brezhnev, the Soviet Union demanded strict loyalty from states in its sphere of influence. Allende’s public statement — “I am a Marxist, but my government is not a Marxist government” — left Moscow wary.

Second, the Soviet leadership doubted it could save Chile. Latin America was the United States’ backyard, just as Eastern Europe was the USSR’s. Brezhnev understood clearly that once Washington committed to toppling Allende, Soviet protection was unrealistic. The USSR could project power in Africa, but direct intervention against the U.S. in South America carried unacceptable risk. Soviet officials repeatedly urged Allende to maintain cordial diplomatic ties with the United States.

Third, large segments of Chilean society were hostile to Soviet influence. Even under Allende, Chile remained overwhelmingly a capitalist society. Soviet aid came with political strings: accepting it often demanded systemic realignment. Many Chileans feared Soviet meddling would lead to purges of domestic business owners and resisted Soviet penetration.

For all these reasons, Soviet assistance to Chile remained limited: roughly $100 million in loans, more than three thousand tractors, and tens of thousands of tons of grain. Compared with the scale of Soviet support for Cuba, the aid was far too small to turn the tide.

Foreign policy disagreements were only one layer of coalition strife. Worse, factional fighting sabotaged domestic reform itself, most visibly in land reform.

Allende planned a structured agrarian reform: the state would expropriate large estates, then redistribute land in an orderly process. Socialist Party radicals refused to wait for bureaucratic procedure and organized peasants to seize land directly. Allende’s original plan targeted only large and medium landowners, but these spontaneous occupations swept up many smallholder farms as well. Enforcement varied wildly from region to region, rural divisions hardened, and unrest spread.

Land was handed to peasants, but supporting systems for production collapsed. Without the capital and organizational management of the former estate owners, many new holders lacked the means to sustain output. Allende’s attempt to borrow collective farm structures from the USSR, while retaining private household plots, created institutional confusion. Large swathes of farmland lay fallow.

After land reform, Chile’s grain output plummeted. Wheat production fell from 1.36 million tons in 1971 to 700,000 tons in 1972, then to just 550,000 tons in 1973. Agricultural production verged on collapse.

The land reform failure was symptomatic of the coalition’s deeper paralysis. Factional conflict plagued Allende’s whole term. Though a founder of the Socialist Party, Allende leaned toward the Communist Party’s moderate approach as president. He constantly negotiated with centrists and the right to hold the system together. These competing pulls exhausted him. In barely three years, his cabinet reshuffled eight times; the final three cabinets each lasted less than a month. A reform-minded president spent most of his political capital navigating internal power struggles.

Facing collapsing grain supplies, Allende scrambled for solutions: he dipped into foreign reserves to import food and rolled out incentives for farmers. The food crisis eased briefly but was never resolved. The United States was preparing to strike.

The Nixon and Kissinger administration opposed Allende’s left-wing government from the start. Publicly, the U.S. described its posture toward Chile as “quiet and correct.” The phrase meant friendly official rhetoric paired with relentless sabotage behind closed doors. Kissinger even assured Chile’s ambassador that rumors of a White House plot against Allende were false. Formal diplomatic relations stayed intact until Allende’s fall.

Yet the full machinery of the U.S. state was mobilized. The CIA alone might have stumbled, but when a superpower focused its resources on a small nation, the pressure was devastating.

The U.S. strategy was to turn broad swathes of public opinion against Allende. That was difficult, given his enduring popularity among the poor. American planners seized on the agricultural crisis and amplified it into a national catastrophe. The core playbook: cut Chile’s revenue streams while squeezing its spending.

Chile’s economy was dangerously monocentric: copper exports supplied 87 percent of its foreign exchange earnings. Contrary to a common misconception, the U.S. and USSR were the world’s top copper producers at the time, with Chile ranking third. American control over Chilean copper was never about filling a domestic shortage; it existed to capture monopoly superprofits.

Now that the mines were nationalized and run by the Chilean state, the U.S. attacked Chile’s fiscal lifeline. Washington dumped copper from its strategic stockpiles — one single release equaled a third of Chile’s annual output. World copper prices crashed from $66 to $48 per pound, crushing mining profits. Simultaneously, the U.S. lobbied European buyers to shun Chilean copper, squeezing revenue both through lower prices and lost markets. Foreign reserves, which had recovered after nationalization, dried up rapidly.

Allende needed foreign currency more desperately than ever. The grain shortfall required imports, and rising consumer demand relied on foreign goods.

When reserves shrink, governments usually borrow. With copper as a steady export base, Chile had once been a creditworthy borrower. The U.S. blocked that path. Under American pressure, the World Bank and Inter-American Development Bank halted lending to Chile, and Western private banks refused new credit. Chile’s access to hard currency was effectively shut off.

Even before land reform, Chile could not feed itself; it ran a 25–30 percent food deficit and had long depended on U.S. grain shipments and agricultural credit. The United States cut all farm loans and restricted grain sales, while pressuring Argentina and Brazil to stop selling food to Chile. Chile was forced to buy grain at inflated prices on the open market.

Not all food aid was cut off. A small portion continued, deliberately channeled to Chilean opposition groups. The U.S. funded opposition parties, poured cash into the military, and deployed military advisers. Allende had hoped to politicize the military gradually and win its sympathy for the left, but an institution long saturated with U.S. influence was unlikely to embrace Soviet-aligned ideology.

Under this combined American pressure, the welfare gains of Allende’s first year evaporated. Chile’s industry and consumer goods depended heavily on imports, so scarce foreign currency was diverted almost entirely to food purchases. The U.S. and Europe also rejected Chile’s requests to restructure old debts, forcing the government to burn through reserves to keep servicing obligations.

Soon, workers who had received raises found empty store shelves. Hyperinflation swept the country. Inflation topped 400 percent in 1972 and exceeded 600 percent in 1973. Allende kept raising wages, but pay hikes could not keep pace with prices, and real purchasing power fell.

In desperation, Allende imposed rigid price freezes, a deeply controversial policy. This kind of administrative intervention against market forces has repeated across Latin America, with predictable consequences: with wages rising and selling prices fixed by decree, many factories shut down. Goods vanished from legal markets, and a thriving black market emerged.

Radical workers began occupying factories on their own initiative. Yet the government, bound by its commitment to orderly nationalization, sometimes deployed forces to evict workers from seized plants. The irony was stark: Allende, who had spent his life fighting for workers, was forced to stop factory takeovers. Even occupied factories struggled to restart production, because Chile lacked foreign currency to import raw materials and spare parts.

While the left argued over strategy, Chile’s middle class, students, and business owners coalesced into an opposition bloc. Shortages fueled wave after wave of anti-government protest. The CIA funded Chilean newspapers and members of congress, stoking strikes and demonstrations. The famous “empty pot marches” of October 1972 saw women marching through the streets, banging empty cookware to protest scarcity. Later declassified records revealed many of the wealthier marchers were paid CIA recruits with full pantries at home.

Strikes and protests eroded public order further. The U.S. deployed another lever: denying exports of machinery spare parts. Most Chilean equipment was American-made, and shipments of replacement components stopped under various pretexts. The damage accumulated through 1973. One-third of diesel engines in copper mines failed; thirty percent of city buses and twenty-one percent of taxis sat idle for lack of parts.

In October 1972, the CIA funded a national strike by Chile’s truck drivers’ association. The agency paid striking drivers in dollars and food, making strike pay better than regular wages. Chile is a long, narrow country, and trucking formed its circulatory system. The nationwide shutdown paralyzed logistics: farm produce could not reach cities, fertilizer and manufactured goods could not reach rural zones, and the whole domestic economy seized up.

By 1973, the economy had collapsed and society fractured deeply. Workers knew Allende fought for their interests, yet daily life grew harder by the week. One banner seen at strike rallies summed up the paradox: “This is a rotten government, but it is my government.”

Allende’s original plan had been incremental: hold executive power first, then win a legislative majority to build socialism step by step. But as the economy unraveled and opposition media hammered his administration, anti-Allende forces gained control of congress. At that point, the United States mostly needed only to wait.

Back in 1971, when Allende had just taken office and stability still held, Zhou Enlai assessed Chile’s situation. Electoral victory, he warned, was temporary and transitional. Elections alone could not secure state power; power required loyal armed forces, whether civilian militia or revolutionary troops. He also warned that if military officers were swayed by foreign powers, a coup was possible. Many Chilean outlets dismissed this analysis, claiming Chinese leaders misunderstood Chilean institutions. Events would prove otherwise.

Castro and Allende shared a close bond; Chile was the first Latin American nation to establish diplomatic ties with Cuba. Castro visited Chile for a month and admired Allende deeply. Still, like Che Guevara, he remained skeptical of a peaceful road to socialism. Che had inscribed a book he gave Allende: “To the man who tries to reach the same goal by a different path.”

Allende chose constitutional, peaceful transformation for practical reasons. Armed struggle would almost certainly have prevented him from winning the presidency at all. First, Chile’s constitutional order had functioned relatively reliably, and the military defined itself as a professional, apolitical institution bound to the constitution. Second, the lessons of Latin American guerrilla warfare weighed heavily. After the Cuban Revolution, hundreds of guerrilla groups emerged across Latin America in the 1960s, and nearly all failed outside Cuba. Conditions for armed uprising were poor: military governments maintained tight control, most people were not pushed to the brink of starvation, and U.S. intervention was overwhelming. As a constitutionally elected president, Allende could not legally form an independent armed militia. He tried instead to cultivate sympathy within the regular military, but decades of U.S. penetration made that path unworkable.

Without military backing, Allende’s government rested on fragile foundations. U.S. pressure and domestic right-wing organizing turned once-neutral officers against him. Senior commanders initially upheld neutrality, following the tradition of General René Schneider. What pushed them over the edge? The Socialist Party.

Socialist radicals viewed the military as an instrument of bourgeois rule, inherently untrustworthy. Party leader Carlos Altamirano openly urged rank-and-file soldiers to mutiny against their officers. This terrified many officers who had stayed out of politics; they feared a left-wing victory would bring purges of the military.

On June 29, 1973, Lieutenant Colonel Souper of the Second Armored Regiment acted on rumors of an imminent leftist purge and launched a hasty coup attempt, surrounding La Moneda Palace with eighty soldiers. The uprising was quickly crushed. Army Commander-in-Chief Carlos Prats arrived to persuade the mutineers to lay down their arms, and Augusto Pinochet, commander of the Santiago garrison, led loyal troops to stabilize the capital.

That failed coup was a warning sign of how brittle Chile’s politics had become. Even Castro advised Allende to tread carefully and avoid further provoking the right. But police forces aligned with the Socialist Party raided right-wing media outlets after the putsch, and rumors spread of mass purges, widening the divide.

On August 22, 1973, negotiations with centrist lawmakers over constitutional reform collapsed. Congress passed a resolution accusing Allende’s government of repeated constitutional violations and formally calling on the military to “restore constitutional order.” A coup was now imminent. The next day, Prats — Allende’s main protector in the high command — resigned under relentless pressure. Officers’ wives had gathered outside his home to shout abuse and humiliate him. Before stepping down, Prats recommended Pinochet, who had proven reliable during the June mutiny, as the new army commander-in-chief.

Allende was now surrounded: congress denounced his government, the military seethed with plotting, the economy was in free fall, and left-right hatred saturated public life. His balancing acts had failed. He resolved to call a national plebiscite, letting the Chilean people decide his fate. If he lost, he would leave office by popular verdict rather than military force. If he won, opposition legitimacy would collapse.

Coup preparations accelerated. On September 7, KGB intelligence intercepted a complete coup plan drafted by Admiral José Toribio Merino. Friends begged Allende to flee into exile, but he refused to step down except through the plebiscite. He left a grim promise: “Let them drag me out of La Moneda Palace by my feet.”

On September 9, Allende told the military command he would abide by the plebiscite result. The statement briefly paused the plotters, who assumed economic hardship would likely deliver an electoral defeat for the president. Within hours, however, the coup timetable restarted.

That midday, Altamirano heard of the plebiscite plan and delivered an incendiary speech. Dialogue and compromise were useless, he argued; the opposition must be crushed without mercy. If reactionaries struck, Chile would become another Vietnam, and the left would smash the counterrevolution.

Allende was the Socialist Party’s founder, yet he could not rein in its radical wing. Altamirano’s speech confirmed the worst fears of the conspiratorial officers. They interpreted the plebiscite as a delaying tactic: once Allende won, he would purge the military leadership. Waiting no longer made sense. The coup would proceed.

The plotters agreed on a code phrase to signal the operation: “It is raining victory.”

At three o’clock in the early morning of September 11, 1973, coup troops landed at Valparaíso and detained the navy commander. Allende received word of the uprising at six a.m. He tried to reach Pinochet, wondering aloud: poor Pinochet, he must have been taken hostage. Nearly all aides urged Allende to escape while he still could. He refused.

At seven a.m., Allende and his security detail moved into La Moneda Palace to make a stand, still hoping Pinochet would lead loyal army units to crush the rebellion. At 7:45 a.m., he called Altamirano. The man who had vowed ruthless resistance sounded shaken on the line. Altamirano fled abroad that same day, abandoning the party.

At 8:15 a.m., an air force officer called. A military junta led by Pinochet and Carabineros chief César Mendoza demanded the president resign. A plane stood ready to fly him to any country he chose.

In that moment, Allende understood fully: Pinochet was the mastermind of the coup, and there was no way back. His reply was clear: “Tell the general that the president of Chile does not run away. He knows how to fulfill his duty as a fighter.”

At 9:25 a.m., Vice Admiral Carvajal called again, demanding resignation and warning the palace would be bombed at 11 a.m. Allende answered briefly, ending with words that would become legendary.

At 10:15 a.m., Allende broadcast his final address to the nation over a makeshift radio link.

Fifteen minutes later, rebel troops advanced on the palace behind tanks and armored vehicles. The presidential guard fought back fiercely. Inside La Moneda, Allende directed the defense. In those final minutes, freed from endless factional bickering, he did not retreat and would not compromise. In Santiago on September 11, 1973, Salvador Allende picked up the rifle Fidel Castro had given him and faced his fate.
