Between 1947 and 1991, humanity saw unprecedented rates of scientific and social advancement. Both blocs strove to prove their system was superior. This all‑encompassing competition drove massive global progress. Few periods in history saw such a large‑scale contest over which social order delivered better outcomes.

The very existence of the Soviet Union forced Western states to make significant social concessions. Western Europe constructed comprehensive modern welfare systems; the brutal worker exploitation common before the Second World War diminished. A widely circulated saying, often misattributed to Ernesto “Che” Guevara (no verified primary source links the quote to him), runs: *“After we are gone, they will build schools and hospitals and raise your wages. Not because they have grown kinder, but because we once existed.”*

This was the great “catfish effect”. The Soviet Union was far from perfect, yet its mere presence pushed capitalist societies to reform and drove civilisational progress.

In science and technology, the USSR sent Gagarin into orbit and built the world’s first space stations. Driven by rivalry, the United States poured vast resources into the Apollo programme and landed humans on the Moon. Civilian science and medicine advanced rapidly. Even bold, high‑risk projects such as supersonic airliners received state backing, enabled by Cold‑War competition. New household consumer goods in the US were showcased to demonstrate the advantages of American‑style life. The United States earned its reputation as a beacon, funding large‑scale research, implementing the Marshall Plan for Europe, and contributing to the collapse of old colonial empires.

While Western nations built welfare institutions, living standards in the Soviet Union also rose rapidly. For example, Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, still a prisoner in 1954, received successful treatment for lung cancer at a hospital in Tashkent — evidence of substantial Soviet medical capacity. Sanatorium‑based rest and recuperation became a standard feature of Soviet social life.

The USSR enforced an eight‑hour working day and later explored moving to a seven‑hour work week. Cultural forms once reserved for aristocrats, most notably ballet, became widely accessible, lifting popular aesthetic standards. Many ordinary workers pursued evening‑school studies, shooting or parachuting as hobbies. The first female Soviet cosmonaut was an experienced parachutist before joining the space programme.

Stories about pervasive Soviet deprivation mostly apply to periods of wartime hardship under Stalin and the final chaotic years before collapse. Consumer‑goods variety was limited, yet basic material security was real. The USSR once led the world in output of woollen textiles, cotton cloth and sugar. It ranked among global leaders in dairy‑product consumption, and second worldwide for beer and ice‑cream consumption. A truly destitute society could not sustain super‑power status.

Soviet living standards lagged behind those of the United States, Western Europe and Japan, yet were among the highest outside the developed capitalist world. The famous “Kitchen Debate” between Nikita Khrushchev and Richard Nixon centred precisely on comparing respective standards of living. Though plain‑looking, mass‑built *Khrushchyovka* apartment blocks solved a severe housing shortage; housing was allocated free by the state. Khrushchev frequently contrasted this with homelessness in the United States to argue for his system’s strengths.

Each system had distinct strengths and weaknesses. America offered abundant, varied consumer goods and high market‑driven efficiency. The USSR prioritised equity and stability: unemployment was negligible, and capitalist‑style cyclical economic crises were absent. Still, the Soviet model had severe flaws: consumer demands were chronically deprioritised; manufactured goods often suffered poor quality; translating laboratory breakthroughs into everyday products was slow. These handicaps contributed to its relative decline in the later Cold War.

The United States, meanwhile, faced recurring recessions and unemployment, exacerbated by shocks such as the 1970s oil crises. Market dynamism remained its key advantage. During America’s post‑war golden age, one wage could support an entire family plus pets — testimony to US productive capacity, and a memory many Americans still cherish. After the USSR disappeared, Western capital no longer faced the same competitive pressure from an alternative ideological bloc, and many hard‑won worker protections gradually eroded.

Misallocation of resources plagued the Soviet Union. Heavy investment in defence crowded out civilian sectors. After basic material needs were met, improvements in quality‑of‑life stalled. Consumer choice remained narrow and product quality was frequently criticised. Except for the final collapse phase, ordinary Soviet citizens mostly faced problems of quality rather than bare shortage.

The 1979 East‑German comedy *Einfach Blumen aufs Dach* (*Just Flowers for the Roof*) tells the story of an ordinary worker who acquires a used government‑official’s car, creating a series of humorous episodes satirising bureaucratic privilege. The film also illustrates that by the late 1970s, private car ownership was attainable for average working‑class families in East Germany, a Soviet‑bloc satellite state.

Soviet‑era reforms powerfully advanced global gender equality. Gender equity was a core ideological principle. Large numbers of women entered paid work. The world’s first female military pilot, famous female snipers, and the first woman in space all came from the USSR. The Soviet Union became a global symbol for women’s emancipation.

Cold‑War rivalry likewise pushed civil‑rights progress in the West. The United States dismantled century‑old legal racial segregation, partly driven by the ideological competition of the era. Under pressure from both superpowers, old colonial empires crumbled, and dozens of new nation‑states won independence. United Nations membership grew from around fifty to nearly two hundred as peoples across the Global South threw off colonial rule.

The Cold‑War era was defined by rapid technological change, advancing equal rights, rising living standards and expanding modernisation. Capital dared not exploit labour without constraint, and unbridled exploitation of the Global‑South was held partially in check. It was an age where workers’ voices carried weight, yet humanity lived permanently under the shadow of nuclear annihilation. Brilliant progress and existential peril co‑existed. That was the paradox of the Cold War.

After the Cold‑War ended, the United States never again launched projects as ambitious as Apollo; the motivating pressure of ideological competition was gone. Financiers and corporate billionaires regained unrivalled social influence. Many nations of the Global South became trapped in disadvantageous positions within globalised trade. In large measure, these shifts followed from the disappearance of the USSR — the super‑power that once spoke for working‑class interests worldwide.

History is often written by victors. Films such as *Enemy at the Gates* feature a real‑life Soviet hero, Vasily Zaytsev, yet contain fictionalised plot elements that caricature Soviet troops. If selective storytelling goes unchallenged, future popular narratives could further distort Second‑World‑War history.

We must not avoid confronting the Soviet Union’s dark chapters, including the Gulag and the Great Purge. Such wrongs deserve criticism. Still, one should not dismiss the noble ideals at its founding. Every great super‑power rests on powerful motivating visions. Three modern global empires — the British Empire, the United States and the Soviet Union — each left unique, indelible marks on human development.

Why then did the USSR collapse?

Once the beacon for proletarians across the globe, the Soviet Union gradually transformed. The 1968 Warsaw‑Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia during the Prague Spring, and repeated strategic betrayals of foreign left‑wing movements (such as the abandonment of the Tudeh‑led People’s Party of Iran), revealed its drift toward great‑power expansionism. For all its military might, its original ideological soul had hollowed out.

Soviet elites grew increasingly unwilling to share state power with ordinary citizens. Under Brezhnev, the nomenklatura privileged stratum solidified. Even during Soviet rule, some officials trafficked scarce goods. After the collapse, a new class of Russian oligarchs emerged, exposing levels of predatory corruption most Soviet citizens had never witnessed before.

On the eve of dissolution, the Soviet army remained formidable. But a state that betrays its founding principles will eventually be discarded by history. When the dragon‑slayer turns into a dragon itself, its demise follows.

This does not mean we should erase its early courage and aspirations. We witnessed its birth and growth. Its ideas deeply influenced other nations, even as we opposed it for decades. Its collapse offers vital lessons for how societies can better sustain themselves.
