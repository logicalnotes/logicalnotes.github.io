On Christmas Day 1991, Soviet president Mikhail Gorbachev delivered what many saw as the ultimate Christmas gift to the United States. The colossal Red Giant, spanning 22 million square kilometres, fractured into fifteen independent nations and vanished from the world stage.

Nearly thirty years on, debates about the Soviet Union still rage. People wonder how such a formidable super‑power could dissolve without large‑scale bloodshed. They revisit its missteps and dark chapters, discussing the Gulag system and the Lubyanka Prison. Yet amid all this criticism, few examine exactly what gave the USSR its strength.

One popular online argument holds that blanket denigration of the Soviet Union implicitly undermines the United States. One can rightly discuss the horrors of the Holodomor, the Katyn Massacre, and the atrocities of the Gulag, while still acknowledging the USSR’s immense competitive power. The Soviet Union had grave flaws, but a state that could rival the United States for forty‑six years must have accomplished many things well. To paint the USSR as nothing but failure raises a difficult question: what does that make the United States, its Cold‑War adversary for almost half a century?

At the start of the twentieth century, Imperial Russia was described as “the weakest link in the imperialist chain”. Within mere decades, this same country grew into a global pole capable of shaping half the world. How did this transformation happen?

We know the US and the USSR were the two Cold‑War superpowers. But what allowed the Soviet Union to stand as America’s equal?

Its true strength did not spring from tens of thousands of tanks and nuclear warheads, nor from its pioneering space programme or its famously rigorous science education. These were only outward symptoms, not root causes. To focus solely on Exercise Zapad‑81 is to fixate on surface spectacle and miss deeper realities.

Since its founding, the United States has genuinely feared only one rival: the Soviet Union. Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan were formidable foes. Japan was the only nation ever to launch attacks on American home soil, while Germany overran most of Europe and threatened the Atlantic. Even so, US leadership understood from early in the Second World War that both Axis powers would ultimately be defeated. At the 1943 Casablanca Conference, Franklin D. Roosevelt declared the war would end with the unconditional surrender of the Axis. Even while Japan remained militarily potent, Roosevelt felt fully confident of eventual American victory.

Decades after the USSR collapsed, Russian characters still regularly appear as antagonists in Hollywood productions. Though Russia’s power has fallen several notches below that of the United States, America continues to view it with deep wariness. As quoted in *The Three‑Body Problem*: “In the cosmos, the highest form of respect is total annihilation.”

What genuinely alarmed US elites was not Soviet tanks, submarines or nuclear stockpiles. Soviet land power could threaten only Western Europe, and American nuclear capabilities were comparable. What haunted Western establishments was an ideological system capable of undermining the foundations of the existing global order. No other modern movement possessed comparable worldwide appeal.

Many casually describe the USSR as “a bigger Russia”. This is misleading. “Union of Soviet Socialist Republics” is a state name carrying no ethnic or geographical connotation — rare in human history. It was not a Russian nation‑state. It was an alliance founded first and foremost on a political‑economic ideology.

Many modern perspectives on international communism are shaped by decades of Western media framing. Still, one basic fact remains: no set of ideas can inspire tens of millions of people to sacrifice their lives unless it holds genuine persuasive force.

Across the snow‑covered forests of Northeast China, cities of Western Europe, rainforests of Latin America and high plateaus of Africa, revolutionaries of different nationalities, languages and backgrounds united around shared ideals, ready to struggle for common goals. States thousands of kilometres away offered support for foreign revolutions out of ideological conviction, with little expectation of material gain. Such large‑scale behaviour had few precedents in earlier history.

Figures such as Norman Bethune, Albert Camus, George Orwell, Ernest Hemingway, Josip Broz Tito, Pablo Neruda and Jean‑Paul Sartre travelled to join the International Brigades in Spain, fighting fascism in Madrid, despite having no direct personal stake in the conflict. This level of idealistic commitment is hard for people of the twenty‑first century to fully grasp.

After the Cold War, the United States came to dominate global mass media. Many people came to accept American values as the universal standard. It is difficult today to appreciate how fiercely competing worldviews clashed during the Cold War.

America’s faith in its own system was not innate; it developed gradually. Its Cold‑War triumph reinforced the conviction that its model represented universal values. The United States lacks a unifying ethnic identity; its population is ethnically diverse, held together above all by shared founding values. Americans may joke openly about unhealthy lifestyles or thin cultural heritage, but they react sharply when their conception of “liberty” is challenged.

Real panic set in among Western elites when the ideal of “liberty” was confronted by demands for social equity, when claimed universal values met the vision of human emancipation.

Before its collapse, the Soviet Union compiled an impressive record, comparable in many respects to that of the United States. Up through the early 1970s, its global soft‑power appeal matched America’s.

Built by working people, aiming to abolish aristocracy and private capital, the USSR emerged from the ruins of tsarist Russia. It survived foreign military intervention in its early years and industrialised rapidly through successive Five‑Year Plans. In just twenty years, the former “weakest link of imperialism” became Europe’s largest industrial power.

While the rest of the capitalist world sank into the Great Depression of 1929‑1933, the Soviet Union maintained rapid economic growth, a development widely seen at the time as extraordinary.

In the Second World War, the USSR defeated Nazi Germany. Soviet troops raised the Red Banner over the Reichstag in Berlin and liberated much of Eastern Europe. Post‑war progress continued: rockets soared into space, submarines cruised the oceans. The USSR launched humanity’s first artificial satellite, the first crewed space flight, the first space station, and the first lunar probe. Even under constant hostility and blockade, Soviet citizens won fifteen Nobel Prizes and collected 440 Olympic gold medals. No amount of Western propaganda could erase these concrete achievements. Yuri Gagarin was a hero not only for the USSR but for humanity, and earned wide admiration even in Western countries.

Few modern readers can imagine how powerfully the Soviet example encouraged working‑class people worldwide. The USSR demonstrated that ordinary labourers, without emperors, nobles or wealthy owners, could build magnificent civilisations. For thousands of years prior, recorded history had largely centred on the rise and fall of ruling elites.

This reality is partly reflected in the social origins of Soviet leadership. Joseph Stalin was the son of a cobbler; Vyacheslav Molotov came from a shop‑assistant’s family; Mikhail Kalinin was born to poor peasant parents; Anastas Mikoyan’s father was a carpenter; Leonid Brezhnev’s father worked in metallurgy; Alexei Kosygin’s father was a railway worker; Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko grew up in peasant households. Both Mikhail Gorbachev and Boris Yeltsin were also children of peasants. Important caveat: by the Brezhnev era, a consolidated privileged nomenklatura class had formed. Working‑class origins among top officials did not guarantee that power continued to serve ordinary citizens.

From the 1930s onward, Soviet intelligence built remarkably successful networks across Western nations. Many agents were motivated by ideological conviction rather than cash bribes. The Manhattan Project remained top‑secret; Vice‑President Truman knew nothing about it until Franklin Roosevelt died. At the Potsdam Conference, Truman casually told Stalin the United States possessed a devastating new weapon. Understanding this helps clarify the nature of US‑Soviet rivalry: it represented a fundamental contest between competing social models, leaving little room for lasting compromise.

Had the USSR merely been a continuation of tsarist Russia, it would have had no pressing reason for global‑scale competition. The planet could easily accommodate two great powers. But for both superpowers, the struggle was far more than conventional great‑power rivalry. The USSR poured immense resources into global competition, and the United States paid heavy costs as well.

The Cold War erupted because these two products of modern civilisation arrived at a crossroads for humanity. Each side believed its path was correct, and each felt a moral duty to guide humanity toward a better future. Reducing the Cold War purely to imperial power‑politics underestimates its historical weight.
