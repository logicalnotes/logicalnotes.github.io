You work as a rail operator. A tram has gone out of control and is racing down the tracks, heading straight for five workers up ahead. You can flip a switch to divert the tram onto a different track to stop the tragedy. The catch? There is one worker on that other track.

What would you do? Would you hit that switch, sacrificing one life to save five?

This is the trolley problem, a famous thought experiment that pits utilitarianism against deontology.

Faced with this scenario, many people would flip the switch on instinct. Saving five over one feels right — pick the lesser of two evils. That’s utilitarian ethics in action: a moral act is one that produces the greatest good for the greatest number, prioritizing the best overall outcome.

Now let’s tweak the setup and see if your answer shifts.

Once again, an out‑of‑control tram is bearing down on five workers. This time, there is no switch. You are standing on a footbridge above the tracks. Next to you is a large stranger heavy enough to stop the tram. If you push him off the bridge, his body will block the tram and save the five workers — but he will die.

Would you push this stranger?

On paper, this scenario is almost identical: one person dies so five can live. Yet people react very differently here. Most would refuse to push the stranger.

Why is that? The math — one life lost for five saved — stays the same.

Deontology, most famously championed by Kant, argues morality does not hinge on outcomes. What matters is whether your action itself follows moral rules. One core rule is that human beings are ends in themselves, not mere means. You cannot intentionally harm an innocent person, even if good consequences follow. Deliberately using someone as a tool to reach a goal violates that ethical principle.

Let’s move to another striking, extreme case.

Again: sacrifice one person to save five. The setting is a hospital, and you are a doctor. Five patients will die without organ transplants. A healthy stranger walks in for a routine check‑up. His organs happen to be a perfect match for all five dying patients. It crosses your mind: if you secretly kill this visitor and harvest his organs, you can save the five.

Would you kill this innocent stranger?

Almost everyone would say no. Intentionally killing an innocent person to help the greater good crosses a hard moral line for human beings.

This shows raw numbers alone cannot decide right and wrong. Whether harm is inflicted actively, and whether a person is exploited as a tool, shape our moral judgements.

The real power of the trolley problem lies in exposing how messy human moral reasoning really is. Our gut moral sense is neither purely utilitarian nor purely deontological. It blends both frameworks. Judging morality means weighing many things: outcomes, how you act, your motives, and whether you treat people as tools. There are no universal moral rules that work for every single situation. Morality calls for careful weighing within real‑life contexts.

As Thomson put it, the trolley problem teaches us that morality is not a book filled with fixed answers. It is an ongoing conversation that demands reflection. It forces us to face our own moral limits and ask: how much are we willing to give up for the greater good of the many? That is why this thought experiment is still debated, more than half a century after it was first conceived.
