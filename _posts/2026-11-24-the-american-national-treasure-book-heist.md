Autumn 2003 brought another college freshman orientation season. At Transylvania University in Lexington, Kentucky, a new batch of students arrived. As one of the better private liberal arts schools in the state, the university pulled out its finest treasures to wow the incoming class.

A group of freshmen were taken to the campus library, built with a donation from Dwight D. Eisenhower. Betty Jean Gooch, the librarian who had worked there for years, walked them through the holdings. The library had accumulated a stunning archive of rare material: dozens of fifteenth- and sixteenth-century volumes, a first edition of Darwin’s *On the Origin of Species*, and the legendary national treasure — an original folio set of *Birds of America*.

Most people would assume a library holding such priceless works would have tight security. That assumption misses the local context. Lexington, Kentucky, was known as a safe, low-crime city with steady employment and a modest population. The university therefore ran its rare-book room with minimal safeguards. The collection sat open to students during daytime hours, the priceless volumes locked only behind glass display cases. The only person watching over them was Ms. Gooch.

For decades, this loose setup never caused trouble. But among the freshmen arriving that fall was Spencer Reinhard.

Spencer grew up in an affluent Lexington neighborhood. His father was a highly skilled mechanical engineer, well-respected locally. The family was not ultra-rich, but comfortably middle-class, and could afford to support his expensive hobby: drawing.

Spencer loved art, yet he was no shut-in. He had a fairly niche hobby in the U.S. — soccer, thanks to his childhood neighbor Warren Lipka. The two boys had known each other since age eight. Warren came from an athletic family; his father was a professional soccer player and head coach of the Kentucky women’s soccer team. Warren inherited his athletic talent and was famous at school for bear-hugging nearly every teacher and classmate.

Schoolwork was never his priority. He joined the college soccer team early on, aiming to build a career through sports. Spencer, the art student, and Warren, the athlete, stayed close despite their different paths.

With comfortable homes and little enthusiasm for school, the pair had been local troublemakers all through high school — their antics even landed them in the local newspaper. Their grades were weak, but they scraped into good universities through art and athletic scholarships. Spencer enrolled at Transylvania University, while Warren went to the nearby University of Kentucky. Both had solid schools, so even with their lack of focus, family connections meant they could probably muddle through to stable lives. But two young men overflowing with energy craved something more than a quiet, predictable existence. They were bored.

Then Warren’s family life fractured. His father’s gambling and infidelity split the marriage, and the household sank deep into debt. Warren suddenly needed to find his own way. He started making fake IDs with another friend to bring in cash, and mostly unwound by smoking weed and rambling with Spencer.

One night, high and chatting, Spencer told Warren about the rare-book library on his campus. He said the library held a legendary book worth an absurd amount, guarded almost carelessly. “It’s only a matter of time before someone robs it,” he said. Warren, foggy from marijuana, asked straight away: “What book? How much is it worth?”

To understand the stakes, we need to talk about *Birds of America*. Created by John James Audubon and first published in 1827, it is a landmark collection of wildlife illustrations. Audubon was both a naturalist and a painter. In an era before practical photography, scientific knowledge of wildlife depended heavily on skilled illustrators, and Audubon was among the greatest. His birds were painted life-sized, vivid and lifelike. The full set contains more than four hundred plates.

Because every illustration was printed at the actual size of the bird, the folios were enormous, too large for ordinary printing presses. Fewer than two hundred complete sets survive worldwide. An original Audubon folio set is widely regarded as an American national treasure. In 2003, one set sold for £5.7 million; by 2018, the price climbed to £7.3 million. During Spencer’s orientation, the university cited an estimated value of $12 million for their copy.

Warren perked up immediately after hearing the number. “If you say it’s gonna get robbed eventually, why don’t we do it?”

Spencer’s judgment was clouded. The idea sounded exciting, and he agreed it was worth thinking about. They parted ways, and Spencer mostly wrote it off as stoned talk — just idle chatter.

A few days later, Warren came back, and this time Spencer started taking the plan seriously. For him, the thrill mattered as much as money. He signed on.

But the two friends had zero criminal experience. They were not master heist planners like Hermann “the Baron” Lamm. In fact, their amateurish attempt would be a perfect contrast to Lamm’s meticulous work.

With no clue where to start, they turned to Google. They searched for heist planning guides, bank robbery tutorials, and anything about pulling off the perfect crime. They studied heist films too: *Ocean’s Eleven*, *Dog Day Afternoon*, and others. Once they felt they had a basic framework, Warren decided the first priority was finding a way to sell the stolen books.

He thought he had connections. He knew Dean Carter, a local figure he saw as a crime contact. Warren invited Carter to McDonald’s and laid out the problem: he had fine art he planned to move and needed a buyer.

Carter was confused. “I steal soda from grocery stores and get chewed out for it. I don’t know anything about fine art.”

Warren refused to give up. He told Carter he would pay a finder’s fee if Carter tracked down an art broker. After handing over $500, Warren got a lead: an email address for a Dutch fence who supposedly handled high-value stolen goods across Europe.

Thrilled, Warren opened a Yahoo account and messaged the Dutch contact, saying he would soon have rare books to sell. After several email exchanges, Warren dropped another $2,500 and flew to Amsterdam for an in-person meeting.

When he arrived, the broker looked at his list and was unimpressed. “You don’t have the goods yet. Why are you here?”

Warren stumbled through an explanation: he just wanted to know what price he could expect.

The Dutch fence was exasperated. “You could have asked that over email. The standard cut is 30 to 40 percent of the formal appraised value, but you need an official valuation certificate first.”

Warren barely registered the last part. His mind locked on the 30–40 percent figure. A cut of $12 million was enough to make him giddy. He flew back to Kentucky and focused fully on planning the robbery.

They had a buyer contact, a target, and now they needed to figure out how to pull the job off. After talking it through, both agreed two men were not enough. *Birds of America* was huge and heavy. They needed a third accomplice: strong, quick, and calm under pressure.

Warren recruited Eric Borsuk, his old fake-ID partner. The two had fallen out over split profits, but Warren patched things over with a pizza and a sales pitch: ordinary life was dull, this job was thrilling, and if it worked, they would never need to work again. Eric said yes.

To plan undisturbed, Warren rented the basement of a two-story house as their headquarters. He was already sinking hundreds of dollars into the scheme before anything had happened.

Next, they needed a getaway driver and a car. Warren found Chas Allen II, a genuine heir to a local real estate fortune. Chas had started his own business at twelve and raced cars for fun. Warren picked him simply because he lived upstairs from their rented basement — convenient.

Chas laughed off the idea at first. He was wealthy, and stealing a book sounded ridiculous. Warren told him the book was worth $12 million. Chas immediately wanted in.

The crew was complete: mastermind Warren Lipka, artist Spencer Reinhard, accounting student Eric Borsuk, and getaway driver Chas Allen.

Now they mapped out how to get the books. Warren asked Spencer to draw a floor plan of the library. Spencer, confident in his spatial sense as an artist, insisted he could sketch it from memory instead of tracking down official blueprints.

Based on Spencer’s drawing, Warren laid out a three-stage plan.

First, preparation: the rare-book room had an alarm system, so they had to strike during opening hours. Only Ms. Gooch staffed the third-floor rare-book room. If they subdued her quietly, no one else would notice. They would disguise themselves as elderly men to blend in.

Second, execution: as students, they might run into acquaintances, so Spencer would stand lookout by the entrance. At the first sign of trouble, he would call Chas, who would wait by the side exit. Warren and Eric would enter the rare-book room pretending to be visitors. Warren would use a stun gun on Ms. Gooch’s neck, tie and gag her, take the keys, wrap the giant Audubon folio in a bedsheet, and carry it out.

Third, escape: they would take the staff elevator down to the basement, leave via a basement fire exit, and meet Chas at the side door. After pickup, Chas would drive them to a west-side Lexington neighborhood, drop off the group, and drive the books away separately. The men would split up and vanish.

Hermann Lamm’s plans were tight and disciplined; Warren’s plan was riddled with holes. Still, the four felt confident. They spent more than a year preparing.

December 15, 2004, the day they set for the heist, brought immediate setbacks. Eric was supposed to arrange a borrowed Ford, but the friend who promised it backed out. Warren’s stun gun, ordered online, never arrived.

They improvised. Chas offered his mother’s beat-up 1990s Dodge, a car that blew thick black smoke when it ran. For a weapon, Warren used a small stun pen instead. They tested it on each other, and the shock left marks on their faces. They packed rope, duct tape, and the bedsheet, put on their old-man disguises, and headed out.

Then they hit another problem: parking. The university hosted an event that day, and the library lot was packed. The smoky Dodge drew stares as Chas circled three times before squeezing into a basement parking spot.

Their disguises made things worse. None of them knew how to apply makeup. The cheap wigs and wrinkled prosthetics looked absurd, impossible to miss.

When Warren and Eric reached the rare-book room on the third floor, they froze. Instead of only Ms. Gooch, a group of people were meeting inside.

Eric asked Warren what to do. Warren said there was no way they could take on a whole room. They left without touching anything.

Back at the safe house, Spencer suggested they call it off. Warren refused. He had poured so much time and money into the plan, and he was not quitting. He called Ms. Gooch and rescheduled the appointment for noon the next day. He picked that time because he had an exam in the morning; school still came first, and he expected fewer people around the library after his test.

The next day, the crew reassembled. Same smoky Dodge, same stun pen. They skipped the bad disguises. This time they found parking easily.

Warren and Eric walked into the rare-book room, and Ms. Gooch greeted them. As she began her tour, Warren lunged and jabbed her with the stun pen.

Ms. Gooch was sturdy. The low-power stun pen failed to knock her out. She reacted violently and fought back. Warren struggled to restrain her and shouted for Eric to help. Eric stood frozen for a moment before snapping out of it and helping bind her.

Once she was secured, they stuffed smaller rare books into backpacks, grabbed the keys, spread out the bedsheet, and lifted the heavy Audubon folio onto it. They each took an end and hurried toward the staff elevator. So far, no alarms had sounded. They rode down to the basement.

When the doors opened, Warren was stunned. The “fire exit” on Spencer’s map was nothing but a storage closet, solid walls all around.

They rushed back into the elevator to head up to the third floor and escape through the upstairs fire stairwell. The elevator stopped on the second floor, and the doors opened. A multimedia classroom full of students and a librarian stared straight at two men carrying an enormous book.

The librarian knew instantly something was wrong. Warren reacted fast, grabbed Eric, and bolted for the fire stairs. The librarian chased them. Eric panicked and lost his grip on the bedsheet. The priceless Audubon folio fell and stayed in the stairwell.

They ran anyway, bursting out through the front doors.

Spencer, posted outside, saw the chaos and called Chas. “They came out the front, not the side. People are chasing them. Get over there.”

Chas gunned the Dodge toward the main entrance. He hit Warren as he emerged from the bushes, slamming him back into the landscaping. Eric scrambled into the car. Warren shook off the impact and sprinted 200 yards to catch up and climb aboard.

The smoking Dodge peeled away. Warren, shaken and nauseous from the crash, vomited all over the passenger seat. Chas lost his temper. His mother planned to sell the car soon, and he would get in trouble for the mess. When they reached the west-side neighborhood, he kicked Warren and Eric out to clean up.

The two men, covered in vomit, stranded in a rough neighborhood in broad daylight, decided they needed police protection. They looked for patrol cars but found none. Local residents spotted them and gave chase. They ran again until Chas returned after cleaning the car and picked them up.

By four that afternoon, all four were back at the basement safe house. They counted their loot. They had lost the *Birds of America*, but they left with a first edition of Darwin’s *On the Origin of Species*, a full set of hand-colored plates, fifteenth-century volumes, and original Audubon pencil sketches. They hoped these items still fetched good money.

The blame game began. Warren yelled at Spencer for the bad floor plan. Spencer yelled at Eric for dropping the Audubon folio. Eric said Warren had failed to control the librarian, and binding her had exhausted him. Then all three turned on Chas for throwing them out of the car mid-escape.

Chas just wanted the goods sold so they could split the money and end the whole mess.

Warren messaged the Dutch fence again: they had the goods and were ready to close the deal.

The fence replied with two blunt questions. “How do I know these are real? And how do I know what they are worth?”

Warren was furious. “Haven’t you seen the news? This is the big library heist everyone is talking about.”

The Dutch broker answered calmly. “I don’t follow American news. Even if I did, how do I know you are the thief? Even if I believed you, how do I know you haven’t swapped the books for fakes? And where is your official appraisal certificate? I told you that from the start.”

Warren had no good response. He knew the value of *Birds of America*, but he had no idea what the other stolen books were worth. Then he thought of Christie’s auction house, one of the most respected names in the trade.

A few days later, the four drove to New York. They walked into Christie’s with the stolen rare books and asked the specialists to authenticate and appraise them.

The staff politely examined the volumes, took photos, and told them the books were genuine treasures. They said the valuation would take time and asked for contact details. The men happily left their email and phone number and drove back to Kentucky.

That was the mistake that sealed their fate.

On February 11, 2005, more than twenty FBI agents surrounded the basement headquarters. They broke down the door. Spencer and Eric were arrested immediately. Warren was dragged out from the basement. Upstairs, Chas heard the noise and thought rival robbers were attacking. He grabbed a weapon and headed down, only to see federal agents’ guns pointed at him. He surrendered before he was shot.

The case shocked the U.S. public. Everyone was stunned by how incompetent the amateur crew had been. The judge sentenced all four to seven years in prison.

After release, they stayed out of trouble and built ordinary lives.

In 2018, the story was adapted into the documentary drama *American Animals*. The film does not retell every detail exactly, but the real four men appeared in interviews within the movie, talking about their choices and the boredom that pushed them to commit the crime.

Their bungled heist is a funny, tragic counterpoint to Hermann Lamm. Lamm planned with cold precision and still failed against forces outside his control. This crew had almost everything go wrong, yet they nearly pulled it off. If Lamm’s ghost watched this heist, he would have been hurt — all that luck wasted on a group of kids who could barely execute a basic plan.
