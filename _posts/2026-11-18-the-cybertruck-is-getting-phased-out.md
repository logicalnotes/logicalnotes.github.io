At first, plenty of people wrote it off as a rumor. But Elon Musk confirmed it himself: Gigafactory Texas will wind down Cybertruck production by mid-2027. The factory space will be reallocated to build the Cybercab, Tesla’s autonomous robotaxi.

What’s going on here?

Millions put down reservations for this sharp, head-turning pickup. How did its production line get marked for shutdown barely three and a half years after launch?

Gigafactory Austin is the world’s only plant building the Cybertruck. Once this line closes, every Cybertruck rolling out becomes a collector’s item.

Musk once called the Cybertruck Tesla’s best vehicle. So how did it end up here?

The core trouble started the moment Tesla tried turning the flashy concept into a mass-produced car: its famous bulletproof stainless steel exoskeleton turned into a manufacturing nightmare.

A regular car body uses mild steel or aluminum, which can be stamped quickly with standard dies. The Cybertruck’s body is made of ultra-hard cold-rolled stainless steel, and it gets no paint coat at all.

This steel is tough, but it creates a fatal problem in stamping. The rigid sheet easily wrecks tooling and springs back out of shape after being pressed.

Tesla had to engineer custom high-power laser cutters and special air-bending brakes from scratch. It even installed a 9,000-ton giant stamping press inside the Texas plant.

All that bespoke equipment pushed the launch back four years and blew production budgets wide open.

When deliveries finally kicked off in late 2023, the advertised $39,900 base trim was scrapped. Early production units sold for $80,000 to over $100,000, nearly double the original target price.

The hundreds of thousands who put down a $100 reservation deposit mostly walked away and canceled their orders.

How poor were the real sales numbers after the price hike?

Musk originally targeted 250,000 Cybertrucks per year, and the Texas line was engineered for over 125,000 units annually.

Actual U.S. sales hit only 38,965 in 2024. By 2025, the pool of eager early adopters dried up, and sales nearly halved to 20,237 — less than a tenth of the original goal. Annualized sales for 2026 have hovered around 30,000 units.

On top of weak demand, the Cybertruck racked up 11 NHTSA recalls, including issues with the accelerator pedal pad trapping itself and stainless trim panels separating from the body.

Some might ask: why not just keep building it in small runs as an expensive niche toy?

It is not that simple. When a mainstream car model underperforms, factories can swap stamping dies, reprogram welding robots and tweak paint booth settings to shift to a more popular model.

The Cybertruck’s workflow is entirely custom. Laser cutting, bending and welding stations were built exclusively for its stainless shell, and its production line shares almost nothing with Tesla’s other vehicles.

A heavy-duty production line sized for more than 100,000 units a year making only 20,000–30,000 trucks annually creates a brutal math problem. Depreciation on that massive specialized hardware, plus the premium factory floor space, piles impossible per-unit costs that can never be recouped.

Does that mean Tesla flushed billions down the drain?

Right after Musk announced the phase-out, Wes Morrill, lead engineer of the Cybertruck, said something telling: the Cybertruck served as an incredible technology development platform, and its innovations will live on inside the Cybercab.

What does that mean?

Strip away that bulky stainless steel shell, and you see Tesla used this pickup as a full-size engineering testbed.

Cars ran on 12V low-voltage systems for decades, requiring thick, heavy copper wiring everywhere. The Cybertruck was Tesla’s first production vehicle built around a 48V low-voltage architecture. Current draw drops to one quarter of the old standard, cutting low-voltage harness weight by roughly 70%.

It also eliminated the mechanical linkage between the steering wheel and wheels, bringing steer-by-wire and high-speed in-vehicle communication networks to a production Tesla for the first time.

These are the foundational building blocks for the Cybercab, the pedal-less, steering-wheel-free autonomous taxi.

In the auto industry, rolling out a brand-new electrical and control architecture on a high-volume mainstream model is risky. Any hidden design flaw could trigger catastrophic mass recalls.

Instead, Tesla used the Cybertruck. Those tens of thousands of buyers willing to spend six figures to be early adopters helped find and resolve the sharpest bugs in this new hardware stack.

Now that the architecture is proven, the stainless steel body has become a liability.

That is why Tesla is clearing out the Cybertruck line and handing the Texas factory floor to the Cybercab, the vehicle built for mass robotaxi deployment.

This angular stainless pickup may stop rolling off the assembly line, but the underlying electronic nervous system it pioneered is just getting started.
