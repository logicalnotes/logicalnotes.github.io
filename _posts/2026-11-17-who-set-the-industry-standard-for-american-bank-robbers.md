Back in 2009, Universal Cable Productions launched *Warehouse 13*, an American sci-fi mystery show packed with gags and dark humor.

The series centers on a warehouse that houses all kinds of supernatural artifacts, each with bizarre powers. One standout item is Al Capone’s submachine gun: it fires endless bullets that somehow dodge every poor person in the room and zero in on the richest person present.

There is also Stalin’s sleep mask. Slip it on, and you drop straight into deep sleep, complete with red smoke and a swinging axe effect. The catch? You have to chug massive amounts of vodka once you wake up.

Among these curiosities is Herman Lamm’s pocket watch. When you start its timer, it instantly turns you into a master criminal. The downside is that you develop crippling, obsessive-compulsive attention to detail. Every use makes the OCD worse, until you finally lose your mind.

So who exactly was Herman Lamm?

The history of American bank robbery splits into two eras, divided by Lamm’s technique in 1918. Robbers before him are known as the old-school, classical bandits. Those after him are the modern school. The man behind this system is the spiritual father of American bank robbers, a walking case study in terrible luck, and a poster child for obsessive attention to detail — the revered founding master of bank heists: Herman Karl Lamm.

Born in 1890 in Kassel, Germany, little is known about his family background. Records only confirm he trained as a butcher’s apprentice in his youth.

He never stayed out of trouble, and one constant curse followed him his whole life: catastrophic luck. He spent his days trying to outrun bad fortune with his own methods.

Gambling was already a losing game, and with his terrible luck, he lived in a permanent state of being broke. To get cash, he turned to robbery — though he lacked the stomach for violent holdups.

His trick was slipping sedatives into strangers’ beer. Once they passed out, he’d rummage through their pockets and bolt.

But with his luck, disaster was inevitable.

In 1910, he drugged a passerby and began robbing him. When he opened the man’s wallet, he found plenty of cash, plus an ID card: the victim was a municipal police officer from Hesse.

Early 20th-century Germany was a heavily militarized state, where police and military authorities held enormous power. If caught robbing a cop, the consequences would be devastating.

Lamm believed there was only one safe hiding place in Germany at that moment: the army. Under the German Empire, men with criminal records were barred from enlistment, but if you committed a crime and evaded capture, the military could shield you. Police would not dare enter a barracks to arrest you.

He enlisted before officers could close in, and his street smarts quickly earned him a junior officer rank. Even in uniform, he could not stop gambling, now against his fellow soldiers.

His luck remained reliably awful. He steadily lost his military pay at the card table. After losing so badly, he began to think his bad luck was permanent. If fortune would never favor him, he would have to rely on skill. He learned to cheat at cards, hiding cards up his sleeve.

He was quick and deft. Soon, instead of losing his wages, he was cleaning out his comrades.

Then fate struck again.

One early day in 1912, Lamm played poker with his commanding officers instead of regular troops. He was winning hand over hand with his sleight of hand, until one leg of his chair snapped. He crashed to the ground, and all the hidden cards spilled out of his sleeve. Worse still, the players included his regimental commander.

Lamm was beaten and dishonorably discharged. If he left the army, the police would arrest him. Cornered, he made up his mind: he would leave Germany.

By the end of 1912, he arrived in the United States.

The new country changed nothing. He gained American citizenship, found work as a butcher, kept gambling, stayed perpetually broke, and went back to drugging strangers. Same old story.

His luck stayed just as rotten. In 1914, he slipped a sedative to an American man, but the victim was a heavy drinker with high tolerance. He woke up quickly, noticed his money was gone, and called the police. Lamm was arrested.

In prison, he reflected: “I was foolish. I knew drugging people would get me caught in Germany, but I never thought it would happen in America too.”

He decided the risk of jail was not worth such small hauls. If he was going to risk prison, the payout had to be bigger. If he was going to rob, why not rob banks?

Before the 1929 Great Depression, the U.S. had already weathered three economic crises in the early 1900s, stoking widespread social unrest. Prohibition, enacted in 1920, further fueled the rise of organized crime. Outlaws from the Wild West had never fully vanished, and urban crime surged. Budget shortages left police departments underfunded every time the economy tanked. Crime spiraled out of control.

One telling example: robbery became so competitive that a remote Chicago post office was robbed six times in a single day back in 1930.

The trade Lamm chose was a brutally crowded market.

After release, he did not rush into action. He studied old heist cases, mapped out existing tactics, and hunted for weak points in the standard playbook.

Back then, American bank robbers fell into two schools.

The first was the rough Wild West style, familiar to anyone who has played *Red Dead Redemption*. Bandits charged into banks in broad daylight, fired shots into the air or at people, forced bank managers to open vaults, and grabbed whatever cash they could carry. This worked out west where towns only had a handful of sheriffs. But as cities grew, police numbers and firepower increased, and this approach became a death sentence.

Since the first method was too reckless, robbers in Lamm’s era favored the second — even more brutal tactic: sneaking to a bank vault wall at night, planting explosives, and blowing the wall open to grab the cash.

This came with a steep technical challenge. It was hard to calculate the right amount of explosive. Too little, and the wall stayed intact. Too much, and the money would be shredded, making the whole job pointless.

And that was not the worst outcome. In 1893, a robber named Johnny West loaded five times the needed charge, terrified the wall would not break. The blast blew him sky-high along with the vault wall.

Lamm settled on the explosive method. After release, he traveled to Utah, got explosives, and picked his day. He lit the fuse. Boom.

The wall collapsed exactly as he calculated. But behind it, instead of stacks of cash, stood a brand-new solid steel safe, untouched.

Stunned, he barely processed what he saw before the alarm blared. Police arrived fast and arrested him.

Officers told him: “If you’d come just one week earlier, you would have pulled it off. The bank upgraded its safe and alarm system seven days ago. You drew the short straw.”

He went back to prison for the failed heist. This time, he truly rethought everything. He realized that with his terrible luck, ordinary plans would never beat fate. To succeed, he had to cover every possible outcome, using exhaustive preparation.

He spent one year in a Utah prison. During that time, he drew lessons from the German military: discipline, meticulous detail, and professionalism.

When he got out, he built his system step by step.

First, he assembled a specialized team. Old robber gangs rushed in as an unruly mob. Lamm assigned clear, separate roles for every member: guards to control hostages, men to take over teller stations, and others to open the vault. Everyone had a job.

Second, he scouted every detail. Most importantly, he checked whether the bank held enough cash to justify the risk.

Beyond cash stock, he mapped the building layout, vault location, guards, and staff. He noted nearby police stations, response times, and officer firepower. He plotted escape routes: a primary route, backup routes, and spare getaway cars. Because of his rotten luck, he layered multiple contingency plans to keep the job from falling apart.

Third, rehearsals. Lamm believed speed was everything. He rented an empty warehouse on the outskirts, set up counters and props to mimic a bank, and drilled the gang repeatedly until every man knew his route by heart. The entire robbery had to wrap up before police arrived, within three minutes.

Fourth, custom gear. He designed special backpacks with openings under the armpits for fast cash loading. He modified high-speed cars for escapes. Before every heist, he test-drove the escape routes, even accounting for weather and safe turning speeds.

He planned everything to the limit, all to fight his lifelong streak of bad luck.

He left prison in February 1918 and began putting his system into practice, refining his tactics with each heist. His rigorous planning brought him a string of successful jobs. One famous example was the Northwest National Bank robbery in December 8, 1924.

On a regular business morning, just after the bank opened at nine, Lamm stood across the street. His scout walked in posing as a customer, confirmed everything matched their rehearsed intel, and gave Lamm a signal.

Lamm waved to two cars parked nearby. Six robbers rushed inside. They quickly forced tellers behind the counter onto the floor. Two men watched the hostages, while the other four controlled the vault manager and cleared out cash and bonds.

The disciplined robbery lasted five minutes. At Lamm’s whistle, all six robbers withdrew in order and climbed into the cars at almost the same moment. Their drivers were skilled racers. By the time police reached the scene, the gang was already ten minutes away.

The bank later counted its losses: in five minutes, the robbers made off with $300,000 in cash and bonds, setting a new record for heist efficiency.

That afternoon, the gang met to split the loot, then scattered immediately, no lingering.

This was just one of dozens of successful robberies. It seemed like he had finally outsmarted his luck through careful planning.

But fate always found a way to strike back.

Between 1920 and 1930, Lamm and his crew operated across the United States, pulling dozens of major heists and stealing more than a million dollars in total. He was the top bank robber of his era. Using constant aliases, few people connected all these jobs to the same man.

So what did he do with his time between robberies over those ten years? He went to prison, repeatedly.

Even though his heist plans were flawless and his identity as a bank robber remained hidden, he kept getting locked up for absurd, unlucky reasons.

In 1920, he visited a friend in Kansas. He stopped at a grocery store, forgot to pay for his items, argued with the shopkeeper, and fought him. He won the fight and got a one-year sentence.

In 1927, he drank at a bar, stepped outside to relieve himself, and mistook a woman’s front yard for an alley. He was arrested for indecent exposure and locked up for six months.

There were more such run-ins. In 1929, he was arrested again in Illinois. Police suspected he was behind bank robberies, but they lacked evidence and released him.

He had come dangerously close to being exposed. Would he retire?

After weighing the risks, he chose to keep going. He gathered his crew for another job. The team included himself, a demolition man named Gault, his protégé Clark, and a 70-year-old veteran robber known as “Dad” Landy. They were all trusted, skilled partners. After a quick run-through, they were ready.

Their target was Citizens State Bank in Clinton, Indiana. It held little cash, and security was loose — a quick, easy hit.

But they were short a getaway driver. He hired a local race driver, Edward Hunter. Hunter had never worked with them before, but Lamm thought a driver only needed speed. Under his rock-solid plan, this new variable would not break everything.

On December 16, 1930, a brand-new Buick carrying the five-man crew parked outside the bank. At nine, the bank opened. Four robbers walked in right on schedule. They moved like clockwork: securing the room, opening the vault, gathering cash. Everything went smoothly.

Seven minutes later, time was up. They had $15,567. They stepped outside to leave — and froze. Where was their getaway car?

Lamm turned and saw the Buick trying to turn around. A local barber stood in the street with a shotgun.

“Barbers” were members of civilian vigilance groups in 1920s America. Policing was underfunded, so communities organized armed men to guard neighborhoods and stop robberies.

On this day, Lamm’s luck ran out again. The barber was just patrolling and had not spotted the robbery. But Hunter, the driver, panicked the second he saw the shotgun. He tried to abandon the gang and drive away.

Lamm and his men emerged at that moment. Lamm reacted fast, firing at the barber to cover the crew as they climbed aboard.

But Hunter was overwhelmed. Before he finished the turn, he slammed on the gas. The front right wheel hit the curb and blew out.

Lamm stayed calm. He told Hunter: “Don’t panic. A car with a flat tire is still faster than running feet. He doesn’t have a car. Keep driving.”

The barber could not catch them on foot. They followed the escape route and pulled away.

But the gunshots had alerted police. The local police chief, Hermanus, was unusually determined. When the gang slipped away from town, he led officers to search the highways. By pure chance, the police spotted them on Route 68 while they were stopped to change the tire.

Lamm still had contingencies. He told the crew: “Second intersection ahead, turn right. There’s a farm there with a high-powered Buick. We’ll take it. Plan A.”

They reached the farm and found the Buick exactly where he expected. They knocked the elderly owner aside and stole it.

Almost immediately, they noticed something wrong. The gas pedal would not go all the way down.

Investigators later learned the car belonged to farmer Nelson. The old man behind the wheel was Nelson’s father. He loved driving fast, so his son installed a governor that capped the car’s speed at 35 miles per hour (56 km/h).

Lamm had no idea the car was limited. Behind them, police and armed vigilantes swelled to more than a hundred people. Speed was the only thing keeping them alive.

Lamm stayed steady. “Plan B. Keep straight ahead. There’s a heavy-duty Chevrolet truck coming up. We’ll take that.”

They stole the truck without trouble. It had tough tires and no speed limiter. They pulled away.

Minutes later, the radiator began leaking. The truck soon overheated and smoked.

Lamm still refused to give up. “Plan C. Turn right for Dana Town. There’s a Ford waiting for us there.”

They reached Dana Town, seized the Ford. Tires intact, no governor, radiator full, tank in good shape. “Let’s go!”

When they turned the key, they found the tank only held one gallon of gasoline.

The whole crew looked at Lamm. Even he could not believe it.

One gallon would get them a little farther. They had no other cars nearby, so they kept driving.

Then they ran into Deputy Sheriff Joe Walker. He was already leading a hundred men hunting a different gang. When he saw the chase, he joined the hunt. The mob chasing Lamm grew to two hundred people. The quiet streets of Dana Town turned into a chaotic pursuit.

With almost no fuel left, they drove until they spotted a farm outside Sidell, Illinois. A powerful truck was parked there. For a second, Lamm thought fortune had finally cut him a break.

The farm belonged to Leo Moody. Moody knew highway robbers were common, so he built defenses. He had a tower on his land with a mounted Maxim machine gun.

When Moody saw them trying to steal his truck, he opened fire. Hunter, the driver, was killed instantly. The gang could not match the machine gun’s firepower. The two hundred pursuers closed in.

Lamm was cornered. He barely got a chance to surrender. A deputy shot him from 350 meters away with a single, lucky shot to the head. The founding master of American bank robbery died, defeated by bad luck one last time.

After Lamm’s death, his gang broke apart; some died, some surrendered. A few years later, two of his former students passed the Lamm Technique to John Herbert Dillinger. Dillinger refined and popularized the method.

Unlike Lamm, Dillinger was an unrepentant outlaw. He used the Lamm Technique to rob banks and even raided four police stations. He pulled off creative stunts, once posing as a film crew to rob a bank. Bystanders watched the whole heist, thinking they were watching a realistic action scene.

Dillinger was gunned down on July 22, 1934, in an FBI trap set by J. Edgar Hoover. One detail sealed his fate: when he spotted agents and tried to draw his pistol, the gun snagged in his coat fabric. He could not free it in time to fight back.
