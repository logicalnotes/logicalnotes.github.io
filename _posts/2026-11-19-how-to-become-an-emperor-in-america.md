In June 2013, several California state legislators co-sponsored a bill. They wanted to rename the western span of the San Francisco‑Oakland Bay Bridge after a public figure.

Before the bill even got a hearing, San Francisco residents erupted in anger. Faced with the proposal, locals spoke up loudly: “What disloyal schemer dreamed this up? William Brown is nobody. He has no right to claim the Bay Bridge for himself. If we must rename it, it cannot bear the name of a mere subject. It belongs only to our sovereign ruler — His Imperial Majesty, Emperor Norton I.”

The 1820s were a rough stretch for Britain. The victory at Waterloo in 1815 finally crushed Napoleon, but glory comes and goes. The British had paid a terrible price to win the war. Years of fighting had drained the treasury, unemployment soared, and demobilized soldiers had no work. Many were restless and ready to stir up trouble. If Karl Marx had been older then, the October Revolution might have arrived a century early.

The unrest spilled out in quick succession: the Pentrich Rising in 1816 and 1817, the Peterloo Massacre in 1819, and the Cato Street Conspiracy in February 1820. A London tavern keeper had proposed assassinating the entire cabinet, and shockingly, many men were willing to join. The plot was foiled just in time, or it might have become Britain’s version of the February 26 Incident.

Worn out by domestic unrest, Prime Minister Lord Liverpool turned to a familiar British strategy: send the trouble elsewhere. Starting in April 1820, more than four thousand British settlers sailed for the Cape of Good Hope. They became known as the 1820 Settlers, a group that would later form the backbone of the white population in South Africa.

Among them were John Norton and his wife. Unlike most migrants, they were not poor. They traveled to South Africa to expand their business empire, bringing along their young son, Joshua Abraham Norton.

John Norton thrived in the Cape. He traded goods, bought plantations, and pushed inland. But Joshua clashed with his father. Once grown, he left South Africa to trade across Britain and America.

John Norton died in 1848. As his sole heir, Joshua returned to claim the estate. To his shock, his father’s business was insolvent. Joshua poured his own savings from years of trading in Britain to settle the debts, left with only a handful of struggling plantations.

In October 1849, Joshua sold all his South African holdings for $40,000 and boarded a ship. He arrived in San Francisco on November 23, 1849.

Norton did not come to pan for gold. He came to profit from the gold rush. As prospectors flooded the city, he supplied them with lodging, food and tools. Gamblers might lose everything, but the house almost always won. Within two years, his $40,000 stake had grown to $250,000 — a staggering fortune that put him firmly among San Francisco’s elite.

Wealth tempted him to gamble bigger. In December 1852, he heard news of Hong Xiuquan and the uprising in China. Norton knew little about the Taiping Rebellion, but he understood that San Francisco’s rice mostly came from China. Rumors spread that China would halt grain exports.

The panic drove rice prices from four cents to thirty-six cents per pound. Norton bet everything. He mortgaged his assets and bought a large rice shipment from Peru at twelve and a half cents per pound, hoping to cash in on the shortage.

Before his Peruvian rice reached the docks, dozens of ships carrying rice from Japan arrived. Prices crashed to three cents a pound. By the time Norton’s cargo unloaded, the game was over. He lost nearly everything. The butterfly effect of the Taiping conflict had ruined a San Francisco merchant halfway across the globe.

His capital evaporated, and he declared bankruptcy in August 1856. The banks seized all his property. He vanished from high society. San Francisco had endless new stories to talk about, and Norton faded away — until the day he returned like lightning.

September 17, 1859. George Fitch, editor of the *San Francisco Daily Evening Bulletin*, sat at his desk. Local news was scarce, and he struggled to fill the paper. There was a knock at the door.

Norton stepped inside. He wore a rumpled overcoat, miner’s trousers and a canvas cap. He looked odd, but spoke with the quiet polish of an English gentleman. He handed Fitch a slip of paper and offered to pay for it to be printed.

Fitch read the note and understood he was looking at something extraordinary:

> 
> At the peremptory request of a large majority of the citizens of these United States, I, Joshua Norton, formerly of Algoa Bay, Cape of Good Hope, and now for the last nine years and ten months past of San Francisco, California, declare and proclaim myself the Emperor of These United States. By virtue of the authority vested in me, I order the representatives of the several states to assemble at the city’s Musical Hall next February, to remedy the evils plaguing this nation and restore confidence at home and abroad in our stability and integrity.
> 
> 
> Norton I, Emperor of the United States

Most people would have turned him away, but Fitch saw the story. He told Norton he would run the proclamation free of charge, and even gave him five dollars. Norton agreed.

The next day, the proclamation ran on the front page, headlined “Have We An Emperor Among Us?” The paper sold out instantly. San Francisco had a new celebrity. The year was 1859, tensions were rising ahead of the Civil War, and the city embraced this playful, provocative figure.

Word spread that the Emperor’s palace was a tiny 5.5-square-meter boarding house. Crowds gathered below to catch a glimpse of him. Local army officers visited to pay their respects, presenting him with a navy marshal’s uniform, gilded epaulets and a red military cap. Norton rewarded the officer with a royal trinket, which was later lost down a sewer.

City officials also wanted to serve. They offered to mend and replace his clothes whenever they wore out. Norton named one a lifelong nobleman and wrote out an official patent of nobility.

At first, the public treated him as a silly diversion. No one expected his reign to last more than a few days. But Norton would walk San Francisco’s streets for more than twenty years.

One month after his coronation, on October 12, 1859, Norton issued a decree dissolving the corrupt United States Congress. Washington ignored him. He then wrote directly to General Winfield Scott, ordering the army to remove Congress. The general ignored him too.

Frustrated, Norton issued a third proclamation: abolish the United States entirely and replace it with an empire, asking the Pope to crown him to stop the coming civil war. Even San Francisco residents ignored this one, dismissing it as a fake edict.

Still, Norton was a working monarch for San Francisco. Every day, he patrolled the streets in his marshal’s uniform, often carrying an umbrella. If he spotted broken public infrastructure, he would rally neighbors to fix it, and sometimes repaired roads and drains himself. He mediated quarrels between residents. Under his watch, San Francisco’s streets stayed in better shape, trash was cleared, and sewers flowed.

When he saw bad behavior, he would point his umbrella and shout “Freeze!” — the offender owed a twenty-five-dollar fine. Norton never abused this power. Many San Franciscans deliberately committed “acts of lèse-majesté” just to hand him the money as a quiet donation.

Not everyone respected him. In April 1878, Denis Kearney, the inflammatory leader of the Workingmen’s Party of California, held a rally to stir up hatred against Chinese immigrants. Norton marched to the rally and stood opposite Kearney, pointing his cane in protest. Kearney brushed him off.

Norton spoke out publicly, urging the crowd to send Kearney away. The crowd turned on the agitator, jeering and throwing eggs. Kearney’s reputation collapsed, and he soon faded from public life.

Norton’s ideas were decades ahead of his time. At the height of the Civil War, he advocated for an international league to settle disputes peacefully; he denounced the two-party system as corrupt; he demanded an end to racial segregation, equal access to transit and schools for Black people, women’s suffrage, separation of church and state, religious equality for Jews, and tax dollars spent on public infrastructure. He also pushed for a bridge connecting San Francisco and Oakland.

The federal government ignored every proposal. Lincoln was busy fighting the Confederacy and had no bandwidth to follow imperial orders.

Norton wrote to Lincoln and Jefferson Davis, inviting both sides to come to San Francisco and settle the war before him. Davis replied, jokingly asking for five hundred dollars for travel money, since his trousers were worn out. Lincoln answered more formally: he thanked the Emperor, explained he was occupied with the rebellion, and promised to visit once the fighting ended. He added that Norton’s imperial troops would be held in reserve, to be called up only after the two million federal soldiers were exhausted.

San Francisco grew kinder because of him. Norton was gentle and polite. Mark Twain wrote that he behaved calmly and reasonably.

The city granted him many privileges. Restaurants served him free meals, and proud owners hung bronze plaques reading “A Favorite Restaurant of His Majesty Emperor Norton.” Theaters kept three front-row seats reserved for him and his canine companions. The local Freemasons covered his rent and laundry. Builders refurbished his boarding room. Farmers gave him walking sticks, the zoo keeper gave him a beaver hat, and schools and businesses invited him to speak.

Though poor, Norton gave generously to charity and spoke out against discrimination.

He also issued his own imperial scrip. These notes, printed with his portrait, functioned as bonds. He used them to pay for goods, and when he had cash, he went back to redeem them. Many local merchants accepted the notes, and they remain valuable collectors’ items today, often selling for more than ten thousand dollars each.

Trouble came in May 1867. A new patrol officer named Armand Barbier saw Norton walking with his two dogs, Bummer and Lazarus, trailed by a crowd of onlookers. Confused by the Emperor’s strange uniform, the officer arrested him for vagrancy.

San Francisco erupted. Newspapers covered the arrest immediately, and crowds gathered at the jail to demand his release. The police chief rushed back from an out-of-town case, apologized to Norton, and set him free. Norton forgave the officer. From that day on, San Francisco police saluted the Emperor whenever they passed him on the street.

The 1870 census listed his occupation plainly: “Emperor.”

His fame stretched beyond California. King Kamehameha V of Hawaii corresponded with him and wrote that Hawaii would recognize Norton as its sovereign when the time was right.

Norton never married. San Francisco newspapers ran a lighthearted campaign to find him a wife. Readers suggested Queen Victoria of Britain. Norton sent several letters to her. She never replied, but the story spread widely. Reporters even asked Alexander II of Russia for his opinion; the tsar offered his best wishes, and the joke went viral.

In the 1860s, as France invaded Mexico, Norton added a new title: Protector of Mexico. His full style became “Norton I, Emperor of the United States and Protector of Mexico.”

A rival claimant appeared in New York in 1865. A journalist named Daniel Norton also declared himself Emperor and Protector of Mexico. But Daniel was arrogant and unpleasant, so almost no one recognized him. Norton I issued an order for New York police to arrest the pretender. Police did not lock him up, but they committed him to an asylum. There was only one true Emperor.

Norton’s reign lasted more than twenty years. His two famous dogs, Bummer and Lazarus, did not outlive him. Lazarus died after eating poisoned beef, and a reward never found the culprit. Bummer was beaten to death by a drunk man named Reppy. Police arrested Reppy to protect him from the angry public. Another man, David Popely, got himself locked up just to beat Reppy in the jail cell.

Bummer’s funeral was a grand public event. Cartoonists drew portraits, and Mark Twain wrote an obituary printed in the *Daily Morning Call*.

After losing his dogs, Norton grew lonely. The children who had cheered him on were now adults. He still gave speeches and attended events, but his strength faded.

On the evening of January 8, 1880, the California Academy of Sciences invited him to speak at eight o’clock. Dressed in his navy coat, gilded epaulets, beaver hat and carrying his cane, he set off from California Street toward Dupont Street. Near St. Mary’s Church, he collapsed. Bystanders and police rushed to help, but he was dead before the ambulance arrived.

Every San Francisco newspaper ran the news on the front page. George Fitch, the editor who had first printed his proclamation, now worked at the *Telegraph*. He wrote the headline: “Norton I, by the Grace of God, Emperor of the United States and Protector of Mexico, has departed this life.”

His belongings revealed his poverty. The Emperor carried only six dollars on his person. His estate held one two-and-a-half-dollar gold coin, four canes, three hats, a worn saber, and twelve unredeemed imperial bonds.

The coroner ruled the cause of death as hemorrhagic stroke. He also settled a persistent rumor: Norton’s brain was perfectly normal, and slightly heavier than average. He was not insane.

His funeral was held on January 10, 1880. The Merchants’ Association paid for a fine mahogany coffin and a public service. Ten thousand people lined up to view his body. A woman laid hyacinths on his chest and wept, saying she had grown up watching him.

At eight in the morning, four pallbearers lifted the coffin. The three-kilometer route to the cemetery was packed. Rich men, paupers, priests and thieves all came to pay their respects.

He was buried at ten o’clock. The next day, a total solar eclipse darkened the San Francisco sky.

Biographer William Drury summed up the story: *San Francisco lived off the Emperor, not the Emperor off San Francisco.*

In July 1933, James Rolph, who had once been a boy watching Norton and later became governor of California, secured funding for the San Francisco‑Oakland Bay Bridge. The bridge opened three years later. Half a century after Norton’s death, his decree was finally fulfilled.

A memorial plaque honors him on the bridge:

> 
> Pause, traveler, and be grateful to Norton I, Emperor of the United States and Protector of Mexico.

The Emperor who dreamed of the bridge is gone, but his bridge still stands watch over his loyal San Francisco.
