Do you like extreme weather?
Why do some people feel exhilarated at the sight of violent wind and heavy rain?

We may find some answers in Kant’s concept of the dynamical sublime.
He argues that when we confront hurricanes or thunderstorms, our cognitive faculties first collapse. The forces of nature exceed the bounds of everyday experience, and this cognitive dissonance stirs a momentary sense of fear. Soon after, however, the rational subject recognizes its own transcendental existence. It mentally comprehends the violence of nature, transmuting fear into the joy of self‑affirmation.

Think of observing a tsunami from a safe vantage point: we marvel at nature’s immense power, yet take pride in the safe haven humanity has built.

From an existentialist perspective, extreme weather resembles Heidegger’s notion of “Angst”. Howling winds sweep over rooftops, torrential rains flood streets. They tear us away from the trivialities that usually veil our being, and restore our sense of “being‑in‑the‑world”.

Subconsciously, you sense these winds blowing from distant corners of the earth, and raindrops falling down from the clouds. Your vision expands infinitely. You truly grasp that you exist on this planet, within the universe — not merely inside man‑made cities. Only in these moments do you genuinely connect with all things.

What thrills us is not the tempest itself, but the grit‑laden wind rushing through the cracks when the fabric of ordinary order splits open.
