Do you have a stable inner core?

Why are some people sensitive and fragile, hesitant in everything they do, while others carry an easy‑going poise, striking presence, and rock‑solid inner self?

Setting aside those raised in exceptionally supportive families, people with rock‑steady inner strength have most likely weathered hardships few others can fathom. Their sense of self has shattered and been pieced back together. Through fire and pain, they grow a far tougher mental resilience.

In psychology, there is a concept called **black vitality**. It means early trauma does not always warp one’s personality. Some people grow more resilient by wrestling with suffering over time. Much like scars left on healed skin — marks of past hurt, yet badges of a hard‑won will to live.

People with black vitality tend to share these traits:

First, a richer, broader understanding of emotions.
Having lived through intense negative feelings and known deep pain, they experience emotions more deeply and multifacetedly than most, and are better at empathising with others.

Second, they balance tenderness with sharp boundaries.
Early on, they burn themselves out trying to honour both their own needs and other people’s. They may even compromise themselves to keep the peace. Over time, they learn to say no and stand their ground. Tenderness born out of brokenness, paired with the certainty of having hit rock bottom and come back up, creates their distinctive aura.

Third, sharp, clear insight into human nature.
They accept both the light and dark sides of humanity. They do not deny their own desires, and can make peace with other people’s flaws. They stand up for their own interests openly, keep clear boundaries in tangled relationships, and turn the grey complexities of human behaviour into real‑world wisdom.

Fourth, a deeply stable, self‑reliant inner core.
Getting through trauma shows them just how strong they truly are. This strength rises from ruin, a rebirth after crushing suffering. The knowledge that “I have survived my darkest hours” builds unshakable mental ground. They rely more on themselves, and pour energy into self‑growth and self‑fulfilment.

You may be going through a tough patch right now — academic strain, workplace troubles, or messy emotional ties. Accept that it is okay to feel overwhelmed. See this hardship as a chance for your own black vitality to awaken.

Take an artist who spent three years pouring themselves into work, only for it to go unnoticed. Years of effort amount to nothing. Crushed by despair, they hit rock bottom. Yet they pick themselves back up. They turn anguish into fuel for their craft, creating work that is more profound and meaningful. Suffering does not snuff them out; it makes them burn brighter.

Why then do so many get stuck in anxiety or depression?
Depression traps people in the past; anxiety locks them onto the future. They dwell on old mistakes, catastrophise about what lies ahead, and keep beating themselves up. Negative feelings take hold. They overplay their own powerlessness, until hope feels out of reach.

What can we do about it?
Stop pointless self‑criticism. Accept reality. Shift away from resistance and complaint, toward coping and solving problems. Truly strong people can pull themselves together even at their lowest. They hold onto courage amid hardship, and meet life with calm acceptance.

We are far more resilient than we think.
