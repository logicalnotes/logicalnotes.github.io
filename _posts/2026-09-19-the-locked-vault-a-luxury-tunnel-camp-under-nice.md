July 19, 1976, Monday.

The southern French port city of Nice was sweltering in summer heat.

Pierre Bigu, a stout man and manager of the Société Générale Nice branch, trundled down the street, desperate to get into his air-conditioned office. At 8:28 a.m., he pushed open the bank’s front doors, greeted by a rush of cool air.

Two bank employees walked over, keys in hand.
“Good morning, sir.”

Bigu smiled. “Heading down to the vault?”

“Yes.”

“Client volumes have been strong these past weeks. It might be a long day for you two. Off you go.”

Bigu settled into his office and poured a cup of tea. Minutes later, those same employees burst in, panicked.
“Sir — the vault door won’t open.”

Société Générale’s Nice branch ran on old-school security. No vibration sensors, no CCTV cameras. Protection relied entirely on meters of reinforced concrete, a 20-tonne steel vault door, and an old mechanical dual-key lock.

Mechanical locks were reliable, but not unbreakable. Bigu hurried to the vault.
“Walk me through what happened.”

“We inserted both keys as usual. We heard the lock disengage, but the door would not shift an inch.”

“Try it again. Sometimes these old locks stick.”

“We’ve tried three times already. Nothing.”

Bigu’s stomach sank. This vault lock had only failed once in fifty years. He called a locksmith crew. When they arrived, they inspected the mechanism and delivered a blunt verdict.
“The lock works perfectly. The problem is the door itself.”

“What can we do?”

“Your only option is to break through the wall.”

Workers arrived with power drills. Three hours later, they bored small holes into the thick concrete. The locksmith peered through the opening and jumped back.
“My god, Mr. Bigu. You’ve been robbed.”

Bigu leaned in to look. The vault was ransacked. Carpet lay strewn across the floor. He slumped to the ground. “Call the police immediately.”

Police raced to the bank. Senior detective Jacques Albertin and his team arrived and widened the hole in the wall.

“Leclerc, you’re the slimmest. Climb inside and take a look.”

A minute later, Leclerc scrambled through, trousers torn by rough concrete. The vault looked like a storm had torn through it. Drills, hammers, blowtorches and gloves littered the ground. Rings, cheques and contracts spilled out from pried-open safety deposit boxes.

Deep in the vault wall gaped a metre-wide hole. Burglars had dug a tunnel.

Leclerc called out from inside: “There’s a tunnel leading toward Rue Gustave Doyer.”

“City sewers,” Albertin said at once. “Search the street for manhole covers.”

Officers quickly found a manhole near a bicycle shed. They descended a metal ladder into the sewer and shone their flashlights straight at the tunnel entrance.

Albertin crawled inside, stunned. This was no crude burrow.

The tunnel stretched over 1.5 metres wide. Its roof was shored up with timber and steel bracing. The walls were smoothed and cemented, and grey carpet lined the floor — nicer than many hotel entryways.

Wires ran along the steel framing, with lights mounted at regular intervals. The whole tunnel ran roughly eight metres long, built with surprising care.

By evening, the crime scene search wrapped up. Albertin sat reviewing the inventory his assistant projected.

“Recovered at the site: forty empty bottles, three welding machines, welding torches, two inflatable boats, one industrial exhaust fan.”

Albertin raised an eyebrow. “An industrial fan? How did they get something that large down a manhole?”

“Investigators think they floated it in by kayak from the Paillon River upstream.”

“Clever. What else?”

“One hundred metres of electrical cable, twenty waterproof overalls, seventeen pairs of rubber boots, fourteen work gloves, three raincoats, three pairs of goggles, seventeen bottles of wine.”

Albertin shook his head. “They brought wine while committing the robbery?”

“That’s not all. We also found two picnic tables, seven camping chairs, twelve portable gas stoves, five inflatable mattresses, two tents, five pounds of raw steak, twenty-two tins of soup, candy, chocolate biscuits, and three and a half boxes of cigars.”

Albertin stared in disbelief. “Are these robbers, or a touring camping group? This robbery feels like a holiday trip.”

“They were incredibly relaxed the whole time.”

“Any estimate of total losses? Anything unusual?”

“The bank’s safety deposit boxes were tough to crack. Out of four thousand boxes, they only forced open 371.”

“That’s a small mercy.”

“The bad news: they hit the cash and gold reserve vault. Stolen value sits somewhere between 35 and 50 million francs.”

Albertin sighed. “We have plenty of physical evidence. Fingerprinting should give us a lead. Get forensics on this right away.”

His assistant’s voice fell flat. “Forensics found no usable fingerprints at all. The gang wore double gloves. Anything that might hold prints was taken away. I suspect they left all this gear just to taunt us.”

“All these items, and not a single lead?”

“One partial clue. There was writing on the vault wall, later painted over.”

“Can forensics recover it?”

“The damage is too heavy for full reconstruction, but we deciphered the original line: *No weapons, no violence, no hate.*”

Albertin’s hope faded.
