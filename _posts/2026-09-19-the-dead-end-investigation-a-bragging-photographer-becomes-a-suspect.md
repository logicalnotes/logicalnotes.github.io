“If the physical evidence yields nothing, we follow external leads,” Albertin told his team. “Gold and cash are heavy. Even if they ferried loot by kayak, there must have been cars waiting at the exit. Keep tracing that line.”

“Already underway. We pulled tyre prints from the sewer outlet, matching a Land Rover.”

“Run wide searches for Land Rovers in the area. Another question: they ran nearly one hundred metres of wiring for lights and ventilation. Where did they tap power? If it was a private property, the owner is a suspect. If it was public infrastructure, there may be witnesses.”

July 31, 1976, evening. A joint police and gendarmerie meeting opened in Nice police headquarters, led by Commissioner Albert Murat.

The Société Générale heist dominated headlines. Reporters flooded the police phone lines, and nervous depositors packed the bank lobby.

Gendarme Dufour frowned. “You have piles of evidence. How can you have no real leads?”

Albertin slammed his notebook down. “We’ve worked round the clock for two weeks, and we have nothing. This gang is bizarrely careful. They left all this equipment, yet not one fingerprint.”

“Surely the gear can be traced? That exhaust fan, does it have a serial number?”

“Stolen from a construction site in Marseille.”

“What about the vehicle?”

“The tyre trail vanishes on the main road. They tapped power from an underground parking lot light socket, no witnesses. Every trail we follow hits a wall.”

Commissioner Murat spoke up. “The evidence path is dead. Let’s rethink. They dug this eight-metre tunnel from the sewer. Power tools would create too much noise, so they used hammers and chisels, hand work only.”

“Exactly, they avoided electric tools to stay quiet.”

“Hand-digging eight metres of tunnel. How many people, how long?”

Albertin thought. “At least ten people, five months minimum.”

“Not five months. Winter rains flood the sewers. Their usable window is at most three months. That means more than ten people, working rotating shifts day and night underground.”

Dufour nodded. “A group that big will leave traces. I’ll send gendarmes to interview bars and hotels across the region.”

“Also track the fencing operation,” Albertin added. “They can’t hide this much gold forever. Melting gold bars takes work, that trail is worth pursuing.”

Murat tried to stay optimistic. “Don’t say we have no clues. Brainstorm, and we’ll find something.”

Months passed. By early October, the investigation had barely moved. No trace of the loot, no trace of the robbers. Newspapers hammered police incompetence, and Murat burned out under pressure.

Midday, October 11, 1976. Murat sat in his office, exhausted, when his phone rang. It was Robert Pandraud, national police director.

“Still no progress on the Société Générale case?”

“Sir, there are what look like clues, but every single one is blocked. I’m starting to fear this case may never be solved.”

Pandraud lowered his voice. “I spoke with CIA contacts yesterday. Back in August, a French man named Albert Spaggiari tried to apply to join the CIA.”

Murat was confused. “What does that have to do with our robbery? I’m French police, not intelligence.”

“To prove his credentials to the interviewers, he told them he was the mastermind behind the Nice Société Générale heist.”

Albert Spaggiari’s first forty years of life were defined by restlessness.

Born in 1932 in a small town in the French Hautes-Alpes, he had a French mother and Italian father. His great-grandfather lost the family fortune gambling at age eighty, forcing the family to flee to France. Reckless risk-taking ran in his blood.

As a boy, Spaggiari hated school and devoured adventure novels. At eleven, he ran away from home, hoping to join the famous Italian bandit Salvatore Giuliano. He left a note in the kitchen: *I reject dull mediocrity; the end justifies the means.*

He ran away repeatedly, until news arrived that Giuliano had been killed by police. Still, he could not settle. In 1950, he joined the French paratroopers and deployed to Vietnam.

Spaggiari was an accurate marksman and brave in combat, but stubborn and refused orders. After three years of fighting, he remained only a corporal, never promoted.

In 1953, while on leave in Hanoi, he and his comrades heard of a place called the Milk Bar. Expecting something entirely different, they arrived and felt cheated. That night, Spaggiari broke in, stole the shop radio and cash, splitting the money with his friends. He was convicted and sentenced to five years’ hard labour.

Released in 1957, he lived quietly for two years. Bored of routine, he left for Africa in 1959. He’d learned lockpicking in prison, and earned money opening safes for clients who’d lost their keys, in Senegal.

In his spare time, he followed the Algerian War. Enraged by the French government’s concessions, he joined a far-right underground group.

In 1961, Spaggiari travelled to Spain to meet group leader Pierre Lagaillarde, waiting for orders. Charles de Gaulle planned a visit to the nearby town. To the far-right faction, de Gaulle was a traitor for abandoning Algeria. Spaggiari saw his chance to make a name.

November 8, 1961. Spaggiari carried a rifle and set up a sniper position along de Gaulle’s route. Before he fired, he opened a letter from his faction leader. The message was blunt: *If you want to die, do not drag us down with you.*

The assassination plot collapsed. A year later, he was arrested for illegal firearms possession and sentenced to another five years.

Released in 1966, he learned photography in prison. He moved to Nice and opened a photo studio. Though still friends with far-right associates, he kept out of trouble. His photography work was respected; he regularly took commissions for the town hall, and counted local officials among his acquaintances.

In 1972, he built a large farmhouse villa. At a social dinner, he bragged about the property. Pierre Bigu, the Société Générale branch manager, joined their conversation.

Bigu remarked that Spaggiari’s villa brick walls were not sturdy. Then he boasted about his bank’s vault: two metres of reinforced concrete, a 20-tonne steel door, mechanical locks, no modern alarms.

Spaggiari listened carefully. A downtown bank vault, no security alarms. He remembered novels where robbers broke into banks through sewers.

Spaggiari was already wealthy. Money was not his only motive. He craved to pull off a world-famous heist, to make his name known forever.
