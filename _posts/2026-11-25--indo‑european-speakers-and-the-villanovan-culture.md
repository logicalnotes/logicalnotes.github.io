Before the 8th century BCE, Rome’s early story is hazy. No one can say for sure which groups lived there. Civilization had not yet taken root, and tribes moved around constantly.

Scholars think the earliest builders of complex societies in western Eurasia were likely speakers of Proto‑Indo‑European. This reconstructed ancient tongue is sometimes called Aryan. Linguists noticed a striking pattern: languages from India all the way to Spain and Britain share deep similarities. That led them to hypothesize they all descended from one ancestral language.

This proto‑language probably first spread across the Pontic‑Caspian steppe, between the Black Sea and Caspian Sea. As nomadic groups migrated, it travelled outward, reaching the Indus River in the east and the Atlantic coast in the west. It is critical to understand: **Aryan is only a hypothetical linguistic term, not a label for a race or ethnic group**. Later, the word was twisted and abused, giving rise to the harmful myth of Aryan racial superiority.

Besides Indo‑European speakers, Eurasia was home to another ancient group: the Arameans. Their homeland stretched from Mesopotamia to the eastern Mediterranean. They spoke a Semitic language, entirely separate from Indo‑European tongues. Semitic languages were widespread across southern Eurasia; the Jewish people are one Semitic‑speaking group. Indo‑European languages, by contrast, were mostly used by nomadic peoples further north.

In prehistoric times, both Indo‑European nomads and Arameans followed seasonal pasturelands, roaming widely across Eurasia. Many early civilizations in West Asia and southern Europe bear traces of these two broad populations.

Archaeology shows that by 2000 BCE, the Minoan civilization on Crete in the Aegean was already flourishing. At that same time, Italy remained largely wild and unsettled.

Centuries later, groups of Indo‑European speakers moved south from central Europe. They crossed the Po River in northern Italy and settled in the north‑central parts of the peninsula. They built fortified settlements, survived by fishing and hunting, and worked with iron. For their era, this was a fairly advanced way of life.

A quick timeline of human development: humans spent roughly three million years in the Paleolithic, followed by ten thousand years in the Neolithic. Around five thousand years ago, societies entered the Bronze Age. The famous civilizations we know — Mesopotamia, ancient Egypt, the Harappan culture, China’s Xia and Shang dynasties, and the Aegean civilizations — all emerged during this period.

Ironworking grew out of bronze technology. Iron tools are far tougher. Whether ploughs or weapons, they do not snap as easily as bronze. Iron technology therefore marked a major leap forward. Still, mastering iron did not automatically mean a society had become “civilized.” Some tribes, without writing or states, learned ironwork from more advanced neighbours. The settlers who arrived in Italy more than three thousand years ago were exactly such a group.

Before 1000 BCE, fortified, iron‑using communities existed in north‑central Italy. These form the earliest identifiable native culture in Italy known from archaeology. They had no writing and had not formed states; they were still tribal societies, though they already worked iron. This culture is known as the Villanovan culture.

Villanovan metalcraft was highly influential for later migrants. Its strongest impact fell on the Etruscans. Through the Etruscans, Villanovan techniques and cultural ideas fed into what would later become Roman civilization.

After the Villanovan culture, more peoples kept arriving from the east and north: Etruscans, Umbrians, Sabines, Latins and others. The Latin Plain in central Italy grew steadily more populated. These varied communities together laid the foundations of Roman civilization.
