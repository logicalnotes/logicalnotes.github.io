October 27, 1976. Nice police arrested Albert Spaggiari and searched his farmhouse villa.

At 2:30 p.m., Commissioner Murat walked into the interrogation room. Spaggiari sat calmly smoking, waved when he saw him. “Long time no see.”

Murat’s tone was cold. “Do you know why you’re under arrest?”

Spaggiari shook his head. “I can’t guess. Give me a hint.”

“The Société Générale heist.”

Spaggiari nodded. “I’ve been following that famous case closely.”

“You planned it, didn’t you?”

Spaggiari feigned shock. “Where does this accusation come from? I run a successful business, I have a family. Why would I rob a bank?”

Murat played the audio tape, recording Spaggiari’s CIA confession.

“Explain this.”

Spaggiari laughed. “Fine, I admit. I bragged to the Americans. Bragging is not a crime.”

Murat slid Bournat’s interrogation statement across the table.

Spaggiari read it and sighed. “People can be cruel. I know this man, but we were on bad terms. He blackmailed me, so I fled to South America. Now he’s caught, and he drags me down to save himself. It’s hard being an honest citizen.”

Murat could not help admiring his nerve. “So you had no part in the robbery?”

“None. If I had, the CIA and Bournat would have real proof to convict me. Where is their hard evidence?”

Murat fell silent. There was no physical evidence tying Spaggiari to the heist.

Searching the villa, officers found only wine and cigars matching the brands recovered at the crime scene — not enough to convict. But they uncovered a large cache of firearms and ammunition in the chicken coop, an illegal weapons charge that could keep him detained.

Police held Spaggiari and ran a 37-hour marathon interrogation. He readily admitted to the gun offences. But every time the questioning turned to the Nice heist, he repeated the same line: bring proof, and I will confess.

And police had no proof.

News of Spaggiari’s arrest spread nationwide. The public thought the case was solved. But police could not secure a conviction. Murat despaired: “We look foolish catching no suspect. We look even more foolish catching the suspect and failing to charge him.”

Paris sent top interrogator Honoré Gevaudan. French authorities avoided torture; his strength was psychological games.

Gevaudan skipped aggressive questioning. He chatted casually with Spaggiari, building rapport.

“I hear you know Lagaillarde, Le Pen and other right-wing figures.”

Spaggiari was proud. “Yes. Back then, one order could have changed de Gaulle’s fate.”

Gevaudan looked confused. “Then why would you help the left-wing rise to power?”

Spaggiari denied it instantly. “I never helped the left.”

Gevaudan laid out the political picture. The current government was right-wing, but François Mitterrand’s left-wing movement was gaining strength. The Société Générale robbery had wrecked the cabinet’s reputation. If public outrage kept growing, the right could lose the next legislative election, and Mitterrand would win.

Spaggiari stayed quiet for a long time.

Gevaudan pressed on. “If you confess and close the case quickly, you can calm the public and save the right’s chances. The 1978 election is still within reach.”

After a long pause, Spaggiari spoke slowly. “I will confess and describe every detail of the robbery. But I will never name any of my accomplices.”

This controversial plea bargain was struck between Spaggiari and French justice. He laid out the full story of the heist, and kept every gang member’s identity secret.

Murat and his team saw it as a national humiliation. Worse was yet to come.

After confessing, Spaggiari was held in Nice prison. He hired Jacques Peyrat, a friend of Le Pen, as his defence lawyer. At their first meeting, Peyrat told him bluntly: “You were manipulated by Honoré. This robbery won’t swing the national election. Only Nice local police lose face. Paris higher-ups are untouched.”

Reading polling data, Spaggiari realised he had been tricked.

At a later lawyer visit, Spaggiari looked him in the eye. “I’m escaping.”

Peyrat panicked. “Calm down. France still had the death penalty; don’t risk it.”

Spaggiari was firm. “Don’t interfere. I’ve made up my mind.”

March 10, 1977, 3 p.m. The fourth court hearing opened. The judge presented evidence, including three hand-drawn tunnel sketches by Spaggiari. The judge struggled to understand the civil engineering drawings.

Seeing the judge puzzling over the blueprints, Spaggiari stepped from the defendant’s bench and walked toward the judge’s bench. The gendarmes relaxed, assuming he would explain the drawings.

A few steps forward, he sprinted and hurled himself through a nearby courtroom window.

The guards first thought he meant suicide. Then they realised: the court was on the second floor. The fall would not kill him.

By the time they reached the window, Spaggiari had crashed onto the roof of a parked Renault car, rolled unhurt onto the street. A pre-positioned motorcycle pulled up, pulled him aboard, and sped away.

The mastermind of the century’s heist escaped from the courtroom, metres from armed guards. The scandal cemented the French police’s public shame.

Within two hours, police and gendarmes mobilised nationwide, setting roadblocks across every highway. Nice officers raced to recapture Spaggiari.

Two days later, the owner of the smashed Renault received a cheque for damages. Spaggiari was already gone.

According to Spaggiari’s later account, the motorcycle took him to an underground parking garage a kilometre from the courthouse. He hid inside a car boot while police searched roads far outside the city. Days later, he was smuggled inside the boot of a Rolls-Royce to a train station, then travelled by train and car past checkpoints to Paris, before leaving France.

Spaggiari became a global fugitive, yet travelled freely. He spent his years on the run doing two things.

First, taunting French police. He mailed self-portraits to newspapers from his hiding places, dressed as Santa Claus, a sailor, an Apache. In 1979, he was sentenced in absentia to life imprisonment. He even sneaked back into France to attend the Tour de France, and sent newspapers photos of himself cycling along the Seine. Police seethed, and never caught him for decades.

Second, writing books. In 1977 he published *Faut pas rire avec les barbares* (Do Not Laugh at the Savages). He later released *Les Égouts du paradis* — translated widely as *The Sewers of Paradise* (also published as *Sewers of Gold*). He kept publishing while a wanted man.

In his later years, his travelling stopped, not out of mercy for police, but terminal cancer.

Ever the dramatist, he planned his final “crime”. Associates smuggled the dying Spaggiari inside a camper van roof compartment, evading French customs, to bring him back home.

June 9. Spaggiari’s mother received a phone call. Her son, on the run for over a decade, was coming home. Late that night, his body was delivered to his mother’s house.

His mother called police. The fugitive they had hunted for years was dead.

French police were furious, yet powerless.

The man who lived by his teenage motto, *I reject dull mediocrity*, had played one last trick on French law enforcement, and came home to rest.
