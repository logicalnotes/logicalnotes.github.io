Are you a pessimistic person? Why are smarter people more likely to become pessimists?

When it comes to pessimism, many people frown immediately, as if it were a label reserved for losers, an excuse for the incompetent. Yet the truth is quite the opposite.

Pessimism was first systematically formulated in the 19th century by the German philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer. Up to the present day, its core ideas can be summed up as follows: suffering is the normal state of human life; happiness is only a fleeting illusion; the world has no inherent meaning; reason cannot resolve suffering; face reality squarely and reject false optimism.

Put more simply: do not readily buy into empty slogans like “hard work will fix everything”. Human desires and suffering are endless. Life is inherently more suffering than joy. Come to terms with this reality early on. To live in painful clarity is better than mindless cheerfulness.

Pessimism makes us aware of the tragic nature of the world, yet it does not necessarily lead to inaction. It is fundamentally different from nihilism or clinical depression.

Take students for example: some know full well that top exam scores may not change their fate, yet they refuse to idle away time in dormitories and choose to study hard. Others feel their major is not what they love and have no idea what awaits them after graduation, but they still show up for every class on time. These are manifestations of the pessimistic mindset: seeing through the meaningless nature of things, yet still choosing to act.

Take some junior‑high students as another example. They attend classes earnestly every day, yet remain convinced they will never get into a key high school. This carries clear pessimistic undertones.

Returning to our original question: why are smarter people more prone to pessimism?

The sharper one’s mind, the clearer one sees the truths of the world and human nature. One notices injustice and absurdity everywhere, and consequently feels more pain. Schopenhauer wrote: “The clearer the consciousness, the more genuine the suffering one feels.” An animal is bound to be happier than a philosopher, for an animal never wastes time pondering questions such as “Who am I”.

Many dismiss pessimism as useless, but that is far from true. Pessimism offers us a clearer, more rational lens for understanding the world. It compels us to confront harsh reality and curb blind optimism, so we do not sink into deeper despair when our illusions shatter. It does not urge us to give up; it encourages us to prepare for the worst‑case scenarios. When setbacks strike, we will not fall apart. If things turn out well instead, we are met with unexpected relief.

Pessimism is not the destination of life, but a pragmatic starting point. Acknowledge that the world is imperfect, accept that effort does not always yield rewards — and still choose to move forward. That may well be the calmest and bravest attitude one can take toward life.
