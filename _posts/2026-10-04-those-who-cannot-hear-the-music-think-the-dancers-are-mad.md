Should people keep a low profile? Or ought we stay humble? Don’t steal the spotlight, don’t flaunt yourself, don’t seek attention — this is what we’ve been taught since childhood. Many people, even philosophers, hold this view in high regard. It aligns neatly with our intuitive sense of morality.

But some push back. Nietzsche is one of them, and he scorned this mindset deeply.

The unspoken premise behind “stay modest and low-key” goes like this: don’t stand out, or others will resent you. Even after you succeed, act ordinary, or you’ll come off as arrogant. What looks like a culture of humility is, in part, a defensive rejection of other people’s achievements. In some ways, it is a mild modern form of what Nietzsche called slave morality.

What is slave morality? Let’s use an example.

Lucy took a solo trip to Iceland. She posted a polished, vivid Facebook update: three days trekking alone across the ice sheet. She was freezing, yet every step felt worthwhile. Life, she wrote, ought to be spent chasing the things that matter to you. Her photo showed her standing atop an Icelandic mountain, calm and confident, smiling wide.

Still, the post rubbed some people the wrong way. A few coworkers whispered behind her back: “She’s just showing off her money. It’s only a trip; no need to flaunt it. Young people should keep their feet on the ground. She comes across as pretentious.”

This is a classic example of slave morality at work. Deep down, those coworkers may feel envy. Instead of acknowledging that feeling, they flip the values to soothe themselves and label Lucy boastful, flashy or over-the-top.

This kind of self-soothing is where slave morality begins. When people lack power or freedom, they use morality to belittle the strong and justify their own circumstances. This is how such moral codes emerge.

In *On the Genealogy of Morality*, Nietzsche laid out the distinction between slave morality and master morality, a cornerstone of his value philosophy.

Master morality arose among the powerful: ancient nobles, warriors, conquerors. They possessed strength, willpower and creativity. This morality springs from abundant life force. Those guided by master morality define their own worth and affirm themselves without craving validation from others.

Slave morality stands in direct contrast. It comes from the oppressed and dispossessed, those who cannot control their own fate. Their weapon is not strength, but moral condemnation directed at the powerful. They brand traits of the strong — pride, authority, passion — as “evil.” Meanwhile, they cast their own qualities: obedience, meekness, endurance and pity, as “good.”

It is a psychological tactic to claim victory. Using morality, they gain the upper hand spiritually. Nietzsche called this the fruit of ressentiment. Long-suppressed and unable to release their frustrations openly, they twist anger into moral judgment, and shame the powerful under the guise of “kindness.”

Take a workplace example. Employee A stands out for sharp skills and quick learning. Young and passionate about client proposals, he consistently wins clients over. For every project presentation, he polishes his work carefully. Even the most complex client slides are nearly flawless: clean structure, sharp logic, and a precise grasp of what clients truly need. His projects land one deal after another.

Yet within the company, colleagues grow uneasy around him. Some joke he loves putting himself on display, that fancy slides are just for show. Others remark coldly that clients value substance and dedication, not flashy techniques. Some mutter he craves promotion too badly, calling his effort cutthroat. Over time, A gets tagged as opportunistic, hungry for attention, and lacking humility. This is slave morality playing out in real office life.

Nietzsche saw Christian morality as the prime example of slave morality. It exalts poverty, endurance, humility and compassion as virtues, while treating strength, desire and self-assurance as sin. European civilization, he argued, withered as a result. People stopped focusing on creation and got trapped in guilt and fear instead.

Nietzsche sought to overturn this state of affairs. He wanted humanity to reclaim the creativity and vitality of master morality, and called for the birth of the Übermensch — not someone who dominates others, but a self-transcending creator of values.

Nietzsche wrote: “Those who cannot hear the music think the dancers are mad.”

To him, most people live by slave morality. They chase safety, conformity and stability, and avoid criticism at all costs. True visionaries and creators dare to break old value systems and craft meaning for their own lives.

“The dancers” represent people with unique souls who follow their inner rhythm. They hear music others cannot — the voice of self-worth, life’s impulses and the drive to create.

“Those who cannot hear the music” are people bound by old moral norms and social expectations. Unable to understand this creative freedom, they dismiss it as madness. Put another way: if you live the life many dare not live, you offend the small compromises they settle for.

Nietzsche did not urge us to become cold, ruthless overlords. His warning is simpler: do not let resentment take hold of you. Be someone who builds meaning. Master morality means living with life’s full force. Slave morality means living consumed by resentment.

Formal slavery no longer exists in modern societies, yet spiritual enslavement remains widespread. We should cast off mental shackles and become creators of value.

That said, Nietzsche’s ideas are radical and will never win universal acceptance. Some treat his writings as gospel; others dismiss them outright. Views naturally differ. We do not need to embrace any theory wholesale. If we can take even a spark of insight from it, that is enough.
