Do you have a pet? Why do more and more young people choose to keep cats, dogs or other small animals as companions? Sure, part of it comes from how cute they look. But is there something deeper beneath their appearance?

From a philosophical perspective, our bond with pets touches on big questions: love, loneliness, and the meaning of life. Many thinkers have written about human loneliness. As separate conscious beings, we may never be fully understood by another mind.

This deep‑rooted sense of loneliness led philosophers to come up with the idea of “the Other”, which helps explain the struggles of individual existence. We long to be seen and acknowledged by the Other, to have our own existence validated. We want to see ourselves mirrored, recognised and loved through another free, conscious human being.

Yet human relationships are messy. They hold expectations and power dynamics. We fear being judged, rejected or reduced by others, even as we crave their recognition and attention.

Is there, then, a purer, less threatening form of the Other that can give us this sense of validation? There is: pets. They make for nearly ideal, low‑risk others. Their existence is undeniable. They watch us, respond to us, and depend on us. In their eyes lies unconditional acceptance and affection. That gives us powerful, immediate proof that we matter — that our presence counts for something.

To our pets, we feel almost god‑like. We care for them and supply everything they need to survive. This gives us tangible responsibility and a sense of purpose. The world can feel hollow, and everything you do can feel meaningless. But the second you get home and feed your pet, meaning becomes undeniable. Skip their meal, and they go hungry. Hold back water, and they grow thirsty. Pet them, play with them, and they light up. The thought “my cat needs me” becomes rock‑solid, unshakable ground for meaning in life. It works remarkably well against feelings of existential emptiness.

Cats also offer immense emotional comfort. Their gaze, little gestures, purring, or simply lying quietly beside you can soothe deep‑seated loneliness and anxiety. I know this from personal experience. When I’m stressed or stuck in overthinking, if my cat rubs against me or just sits nearby, it heals me. Most of my worries fade away. Because of that unconditional company, I always want to give back to them.

Communication with pets carries no misinterpretation or distortion. It is pure emotion rooted in presence, touch and instinct. It answers our fundamental human urge to escape existential loneliness.

Philosophically speaking, our deep attachment to pets is about far more than cuteness or just having someone around. It is an existential act. As lonely individuals, we reach out for connection, affirm our self‑worth, and create meaning for our lives by caring for another living creature. Pets are our allies against loneliness. They guide us back to the richness of everyday life. Through loving them and taking responsibility for them, we ultimately confirm who we are and what we value.
