In 1978, Denard had a visitor. It was Abdallah — the very man Denard had helped topple. Losing the presidency to Denard was one thing, but business was business. Denard didn't refuse the meeting.

Over conversation, Abdallah laid out his request: help him overthrow Soilih and put him back in power in the Comoros. Even for a mercenary, taking a contract against his own former employer gave Denard pause. He hemmed and hawed, claiming he could no longer tell good men from bad. Then Abdallah offered six million dollars, and Denard had his epiphany: turns out you were the good guy all along.

Old enemies, new partners. Denard decided to return to the Comoros.

The man then in power, Soilih, had done plenty of damage. He'd put a fifteen-year-old in charge of the police, and the boy burned the country's entire national archive. Soilih was deeply superstitious: a local witch doctor had prophesied he'd be killed by a white man leading a black dog. So Soilih ordered every black dog on the island killed. As if that stopped anyone from simply bringing one in from outside. Under a ruler this absurd, the regime was ripe for the taking.

Denard had little trouble turning Soilih's own bodyguard commander. In May 1978 his crew sailed from Europe in fishing boats, reaching the Comoros on the night of the 13th. They landed, moved with their inside man, surrounded the presidential palace, and made straight for Soilih's bedroom.

Denard kicked in the door and arrested the tyrant in the middle of his pleasures. The coup that toppled a country took three hours. Ten Comorian soldiers died; the mercenaries' only casualty was a man who twisted his ankle. Two weeks later Soilih was executed, and Denard's men dragged his corpse through the streets — Denard at the head, his black Alsatian shepherding at his side.

A new era had begun for the Comoros.

Abdallah was back in the president's chair, and Denard started a new life. To buy his loyalty — or rather, to buy him off — Abdallah had promised not only six million dollars but something better: the defence and transport portfolios, handed to Denard outright, with authority to raise and command the presidential guard. His few dozen mercenaries took commissions as officers up and down the ranks. Denard himself lived like a king, and the locals called him the White Sheikh.

He built a 1,800-acre estate with three hundred servants and sixty cars in the garage. To marry multiple wives legally, he nominally converted to Islam — six wives in all.

More to the point, under Denard the Comoros became a giant mercenary base. He recruited, drilled, and used the islands as a staging ground to export soldiers and grow his business across Africa. He became a billionaire with seven multinational companies.

The little island nation had become a state run for a private company. Who'd have thought that in the 1980s, cyberpunk came true first in Africa — the Comoros as a real-world Night City?

Denard's grip enraged the rest of Africa; several countries refused to recognise the regime propped up by white guns. He kept it up for eleven years.

In 1989, under pressure from South Africa, a finally fed-up Abdallah decided to get rid of Denard. He staged a lavish state banquet and, over dinner, tried to take his command away gently — the old cup-of-wine gambit. At the table he told Denard: "You can leave with honour, a national hero, head held high. Come back any time as a tourist." He was rather moved by his own words.

He never imagined it would be him, not Denard, who reached the end of the road.

In the small hours of 27 November 1989, President Abdallah was shot dead in his own bed. Almost everyone assumed Denard had ordered it. The killing ignited the country. Crowds took to the streets demanding that Denard and his men get out. Under the weight of public fury, the French government had to send three thousand troops to throw him out. Denard and his family flew out on a French C-130 to South Africa to lie low.

He spent three years in South Africa, then moved home to France in 1993, aged 64. Africa breathed a sigh of relief, convinced the ageing mercenary was finally done with the continent. But was he?

Even while living in South Africa, Denard kept plotting a return to the Comoros, and the idea never died after he came home.

On the night of 27 September 1995, at sixty-six, Denard landed again with thirty-odd mercenaries in rubber boats. Once more he succeeded almost effortlessly: his men seized the airport, the barracks, and the radio station, and dragged the elderly, ailing president, Djohar, out of bed. By the next morning Denard controlled the whole country.

His comeback staggered Africa. President Chirac condemned the coup in the strongest terms. On 4 October France landed a combined force of special forces and warships. After a short, half-hearted exchange of fire, they arrested Denard and his men.

By all rights, this time Denard should have faced real punishment. Back in France, he merely waited for trial. In 1996 he was acquitted over Abdallah's assassination; in 2006 he was sentenced to five years, but got it suspended on grounds of age and ill health. Through the whole legal circus, he never actually spent a day in prison.

The reason, as Denard himself liked to say, was that although he never held written authorisation, his operations had the tacit blessing of the French state. He preferred to think of himself not as a mercenary but as a privateer — a licensed pirate. Many believe Jacques Foccart, de Gaulle's "Mr Africa", was an old friend of his. At trial, Denard's lawyer read out a deposition from a former head of French counter-intelligence: "When the official services found it inconvenient to act, we had to rely on other means. Denard was one of those means."

After the French crushed this coup, the ousted president Djohar never got his office back; the pro-French Mohamed Taki Abdoulkarim became the new president. The whole affair, in all its details, is hard not to read as a choreographed performance.

When he was shipped back to France in 1995, Denard finally retired. No more armed operations. He settled in his home village and seemed a gentle old man, nothing like the storm-bringer of the old days. He'd boast to the neighbours about where each scar on his body came from. But if you didn't know, he was just a plain French pensioner with a bad leg.

On 13 October 2007, Denard died in his homeland at seventy-eight. The soldier who'd spent a lifetime fighting and churning up a dozen countries was gone. His coffin was draped in the French flag and buried to military music, attended by two hundred friends and fellow veterans. A few unmarked cars pulled up; officials climbed out, laid unnamed bouquets, and drove off.

To the very end, Denard insisted he had done nothing wrong: "I am a pirate of the French Republic. I represent the interests of France."

Reactions to his death split sharply. Africa celebrated, saying God had finally swept away France's long shadow. British and French media mourned: "The benchmark of the mercenary trade has fallen; the era of the mercenary is over." The most widely circulated French headline was blunt: "An old soldier has died."

The French said Denard was a product of his era. Against the backdrop of Cold War East-West confrontation, he served as France's advance man in Africa — the high-water mark of the individual mercenary. Many outlets argued that with the Cold War's end and the corporatisation of the industry, a legend like Denard would never appear again. The age of the swashbuckling private soldier was gone, and his records would stand forever.
