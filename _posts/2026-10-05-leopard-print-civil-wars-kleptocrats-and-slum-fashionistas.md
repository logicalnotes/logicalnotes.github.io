What city on Earth is home to the largest French‑speaking population?
Most people would instantly answer Paris. In truth, Kinshasa, capital city of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), holds this title. Its population exceeds 12 million, surpassing Greater Paris. Yet both the DRC and Kinshasa remain largely invisible on the global stage.

Kinshasa’s 12‑million‑plus metropolis outranks New York and Hong Kong in raw population size, yet it rarely makes international headlines. In a country with a per‑capita GDP below 600 US‑dollars, mainstream media mainly fixates on landfills, slums and HIV. For decades, the outside world knew the DRC chiefly for systemic corruption, some of Africa’s bloodiest conflicts, and its leopard‑hat‑wearing president Mobutu Sese Seko.

In recent years, however, the *Société des Ambianceurs et des Personnes Élégantes* — better known as La Sape — has captured public imagination in Kinshasa. Ordinary Congolese men stride through slum‑streets dressed in immaculate designer suits and luxury accessories. Many garments are genuine high‑end pieces; a single outfit can cost thousands of US‑dollars. These fashion devotees are far from wealthy. They work as street‑vendors, miners or taxi‑drivers. They drain years of personal savings to buy these ensembles. Men draped in Armani may go without steady meals.

This surreal scene in modern‑day Kinshasa cannot be understood without unpacking the DRC’s tortured past.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo is commonly shortened to DRC or Congo‑Kinshasa. Across the Congo River sits the Republic of the Congo, whose capital is Brazzaville, nicknamed Congo‑Brazzaville. The distinction draws little global attention.

The DRC ranks 11th worldwide in land area and 16th in population, a major country by global standards. In 2019, its GDP stood at only 47.3 billion US‑dollars. It is not merely poor — it is catastrophically poor.

Unlike arid nations starved for natural resources, the DRC is blessed with extraordinary endowments. Nearly half its territory remains forested; rivers criss‑cross the land, and climate conditions favour agriculture. Its mineral wealth earned it the moniker “the world’s raw‑material warehouse”. It holds 15 percent of global copper reserves, 23 percent of world diamond reserves, plus vast deposits of oil, gas and non‑ferrous metals. Large swathes feature flat, mine‑friendly terrain.

Yet such bounty coexists with crushing poverty. Congolese history is a chain of successive tragedies.

Indigenous African kingdoms, most notably the Kingdom of the Congo, once flourished here. In the 19th century, Belgian colonisers seized the territory as the private domain of King Leopold II of Belgium. Leopold’s regime unleashed horrific exploitation and brutality against local peoples, including widespread mutilation. Between 1884 and 1908, an estimated ten‑million Congolese perished under his rule. Mass death created acute labour shortages across formerly populous regions.

To fill labour gaps, Belgian authorities sought to recruit Chinese indentured labourers. They lobbied Li Hongzhang of the Qing Empire to conclude the *Tianjin Chapter*, which would grant the Congo Free State most‑favoured‑nation privileges inside China. Li Hongzhang was reassigned as Viceroy of Liang‑Guang before ratification, and the treaty never entered force. Had it passed, Qing China would have been the only great power to sign an unequal treaty with an African state.

Belgian colonial oppression persisted until 1960. That year, under anti‑colonial icon Patrice Lumumba, the country won independence. Lumumba was a fierce nationalist determined to eject foreign influence. Seeking counter‑weight against Western interference, he turned toward the Soviet Union. This made him a prime target for Western powers.

Within months of independence in 1960, Mobutu staged a coup to overthrow Lumumba’s government. Lumumba, the founding father who fought for national freedom, never got to implement his vision. He was eliminated, his fate intertwined with the shattered early hopes of the young nation.

Mobutu Sese Seko Koko Ngbendu Wa Za Banga — universally known as Mobutu — looms large in modern Congolese and African history. He is often grouped with figures such as Jean‑Bédel Bokassa among “Africa’s big‑three tyrants”, though historians debate this label. Mobutu did not organise large‑scale massacres. Instead, he perfected state‑sponsored kleptocracy on an epic scale.

Unlike many unschooled military strongmen, Mobutu came from a cook’s household and excelled academically. He studied in Brussels, Belgium. Returning home in 1949, he joined the colonial police‑force and attained the highest rank open to Black soldiers: sergeant‑major.

Upon discharge in 1956, he built a career in journalism, publishing regularly in Kinshasa newspapers. Media connections brought him into Lumumba’s inner circle; he often represented Lumumba at official gatherings. After independence, Mobutu accumulated immense power within the national military.

In September 1960, the Central Intelligence Agency of the United States encouraged Mobutu to remove Lumumba. Though initially hesitant, he drank heavily inside a CIA safe‑house before deciding to imprison his former patron and seize power. Four years later he deposed the nominal president Joseph Kasa‑Vubu and formally assumed the presidency.

While consolidating power in 1967, a white‑mercenary force led by legendary French mercenary Bob Denard launched an incursion from Zimbabwe; Mobutu’s forces defeated the raid.

Mobutu was a shrewd political operator. Through bribes and co‑optation he absorbed regional armed factions. Public executions of political opponents took place before mass crowds. His rhetoric claimed politicians had ruined the nation and that a strongman must rescue the country from decline.

Early in his rule Mobutu delivered some tangible progress. He nationalised most Belgian‑owned mining assets, generating significant state revenue. By 1970 government debt fell, currency remained stable and economic indicators improved. American Cold‑War backing was decisive here: Washington supported any reliably anti‑Soviet leader. Geographically central, staunchly anti‑communist Zaire became a major US‑client. Billions in aid flowed in, alongside encouragement for US corporate investment. Foreign direct‑investment reached over 2 billion US‑dollars by 1974.

This brief period of relative improvement soon ended. With no domestic counter‑balance remaining, Mobutu grew unrestrained. He promoted “Mobutuism”, an ill‑defined official ideology propagated through state media and legislation. Rather than standard capitalism, he steered Zaire toward neo‑feudal patterns.

His “Authenticité” cultural programme mandated sweeping renaming. The nation was renamed Zaire; Léopoldville became Kinshasa. Cities and personal given‑names across the country were forced to adopt indigenous‑style designations. Mobutu’s own adopted title translated roughly to “the rooster who never loses”. He piled dozens of honorifics upon himself: Father of the Nation, Super‑Warrior, Grand‑Strategist. His maxim ran: one chief per tribe, one Mobutu for all Zaire.

He banned Western‑style suits for ordinary citizens and imposed the “abacost”, a collar‑less tailored national garment, alongside iconic leopard‑skin hats for official ceremonies. Mobutu transformed himself into a supreme tribal‑style paramount‑chief. State propaganda saturated public‑life: *Mobutu created Zaire; Mobutu brings good weather, plants and flowers; without Mobutu Zairians are nothing; Mobutu answers to no‑one yet all owe him loyalty*.

Many authoritarian Cold‑War leaders pursued national reform or unity. Mobutu’s autocracy existed chiefly for plunder.

In 1973 his “Economic Independence Programme” nationalised more than two‑thousand foreign‑owned firms. These confiscated enterprises were treated as personal spoils, doled out to relatives and loyalists. Mobutu kept the richest prizes for himself: fourteen large plantations supplying one‑quarter of national cocoa and banana output, employing nearly thirty‑thousand workers; the country’s dominant cattle‑ranch holding 75 percent of national livestock. His agricultural holdings made him arguably one of history’s largest private landowners. The most valuable copper, cobalt and diamond mines passed under his personal control, granting him partial leverage over global diamond pricing.

He was also the dominant shareholder of the Bank of Kinshasa, mandating all state‑linked businesses to hold accounts there. Treasury funds were accessible to him without oversight.

Beyond corporate assets, Mobutu struck a deal with parliament: every state‑funded public‑works project automatically diverted 30‑50 percent of its budget directly to the presidency. Few pre‑modern monarchs practised such naked extraction. In peak‑years this single channel netted him 65 million US‑dollars annually.

Top‑down plunder cascaded through the whole civil service. Zaire listed 490 000 civil‑servants on paper; at most one‑third actually reported for duty. The rest were ghost‑employees invented to siphon salaries. Non‑existent public‑works projects drained state coffers. Foreign financial analysts estimated at least 40 percent of all government spending vanished untraced.

Even with ghost‑payroll fraud, civil‑servants, teachers and medical‑staff frequently went unpaid. A grim popular joke circulated: *the state pretends to pay us, we pretend to work*.

The military mirrored this rot: units existed only on paper. Weapons and military supplies were sold off. The Zairian Air‑Force ran a semi‑commercial passenger‑service billing jet‑fuel to defence appropriations, carrying more civilian travellers than the national airline.

Unpaid doctors, nurses and soldiers turned to extortion. Bribes were required for nearly every administrative procedure. Hospitals sold equipment and pharmaceuticals until many stood as empty shells. Soldiers erected road‑blocks along highways to shake‑down traders.

Mobutu understood the pervasive graft. Corruption kept his patronage‑network loyal. He reportedly advised his officials: “Steal smartly; do not drain everything in one go — milk the cow slowly”.

Mobutu’s total private fortune can never be tallied precisely. Outside estimates range from 5 billion to 30 billion US‑dollars. He ranked among the planet’s richest individuals at his height, with massive wealth stashed overseas. He owned an 800‑hectare estate in Portugal, luxury villas along the French Riviera, multiple Paris properties, and at least nine buildings across Brussels, plus real‑estate in France, Belgium, Brazil and Morocco.

Back home in Kinshasa he kept a luxury yacht and private zoo stocked with exotic imported wildlife. Deep inland, 700 kilometres from the capital, he built a 15 000‑square‑metre marble palace complete with an airstrip capable of receiving his Concorde supersonic jet for shopping trips to Paris.

US diplomatic observers noted his inner‑circle of roughly eighty elites: twenty served as ministers, twenty as ambassadors, twenty lived in exile, twenty sat in prison. Mobutu rotated them relentlessly every few months. A politician might serve as cabinet‑minister, fall into exile, return as ambassador to that same host‑nation, go to jail, then re‑emerge in government office.

How did this dysfunctional regime survive so long? Critical backing came from the United States. Washington fully recognised Zaire operated as a kleptocracy, yet prioritised anti‑communist alignment. In 1988, then‑Vice‑President George H. W. Bush received Mobutu at the White House lawn, publicly declaring: “Zaire is America’s old friend, Mobutu one of our most valuable friends, and we are proud of this”.

While elites prospered, ordinary Zairians endured catastrophe. Nationalised enterprises handed to cronies collapsed from mismanagement. Decades of state neglect allowed Belgian‑era infrastructure to crumble: roads fell unmaintained; water‑supply, power‑grids and health‑systems disintegrated. Even with fertile soils, cultivated farmland remained far below potential. By the 1980s, many Congolese who once ate three meals daily struggled for one meal per day. Forty‑percent of Kinshasa residents suffered severe malnutrition. Real household incomes sank to less than one‑tenth of 1960‑levels.

By the 1990s Mobutu’s state had stripped the country bare. Starting in 1990, inflation spiralled into four‑digit territory. Per‑capita GDP dropped to 117 US‑dollars in 1993, merely 35 percent of its 1958 figure. In 1994 inflation hit 9 800 percent; consumer‑prices multiplied roughly one‑hundred‑fold within twelve‑months. Copper‑output shrank to one‑fifteenth of its 1970 volume; cobalt production fell to one‑sixtieth. One US‑ambassador famously commented: “Mobutu killed the golden‑egg‑laying hen, and did not even leave its feathers behind”.

The 1994 Rwandan Genocide set regional‑shockwaves in motion. Mobutu tried diverting domestic discontent outward, worsening tensions with Rwanda. New Rwandan president Paul Kagame harboured deep animosity toward Mobutu. In October 1996, rebel‑leader Laurent‑Désiré Kabila raised arms against the regime. Kabila had decades earlier crossed paths with Che Guevara and spent years marginalised in eastern Zaire. Fueled by popular anger and foreign backing, his rebel‑army advanced rapidly. Mobutu could no longer field effective fighting‑forces and retreated toward Kinshasa.

Negotiations to let Mobutu retain the presidency failed. On 15 May 1997 Mobutu fled Kinshasa aboard aircraft for exile in Morocco, ending his thirty‑plus‑year reign. Kabila’s troops entered the capital the next day. Eight‑months after launching his uprising, Laurent‑Désiré Kabila took office as president on 17 May 1997. Four‑months later Mobutu died in Morocco, escaping domestic trial.

A curious footnote: following regime‑change, old banknotes printed with Mobutu’s portrait remained in circulation before replacement currency arrived. Citizens hated the ex‑dictator so thoroughly they physically scratched or punched out his face from every banknote, creating defaced legal‑tender.

Mobutu’s passing did not end Congolese suffering. The ousting of Mobutu is known as the First Congo War. Though short and comparatively low in direct combat‑fatalities, it stirred fresh chaos. Hopes ran high for Laurent‑Désiré Kabila’s new‑government. Yet once in power he purged former Mobutu‑allies, sidelined rivals, and quickly quarrelled with his former Rwandan backers. Stability never arrived.

In August 1998, Kabila sought to expel Rwandan military‑units stationed on Congolese soil. Rwanda opened hostilities against his administration. Rich mineral‑resources and strategic location drew intervention from many neighbours. Troops from nine African nations became entangled in fighting. This is the Second Congo War, widely nicknamed *Africa’s World War*.

It ranks among the bloodiest late‑20th‑century conflicts. Between 1998‑2003 war‑related violence, famine and infectious‑disease killed an estimated minimum of 4.5 million people. Daily mortality‑rates were staggering. Yet global‑media paid it scant attention.

President Laurent‑Désiré Kabila was assassinated mid‑war. His son Joseph Kabila, who had been studying at China’s National Defence University, rushed home to assume the presidency. A formal cease‑fire was agreed in 2003. Even afterwards, at least one‑thousand people perished every day from conflict‑related causes inside DRC as late as 2004.

Joseph Kabila’s administration retained many dysfunctional patterns inherited from the Mobutu era: corruption, nepotism, stagnant public‑infrastructure. The DRC stayed among the world’s poorest and most‑corrupt states. National life‑expectancy stood below fifty‑years; HIV‑infection‑rates hovered between 10‑20 percent. Joseph Kabila lost national‑elections in 2019 and stepped‑down after tense political bargaining.

This brings us back to Kinshasa’s La‑Sape devotees. Called *Sapeurs* — “Society of Ambience‑Makers and Elegant People” — these ordinary low‑income men assemble second‑hand or genuine luxury‑clothing through extreme frugality. Their sharp styling matches high‑fashion runways. They gather for social‑meet‑ups to showcase and critique each‑other’s outfits.

Abraham Maslow’s hierarchy of needs posits that humans satisfy physiological and safety‑needs before chasing dignity and self‑actualisation. Sapeurs seem to invert that sequence: immaculately‑dressed yet uncertain about their next meal. International media fixates on them; Japanese and French fashion‑magazines produce photo‑essays. Powerful visual‑contrast emerges: well‑tailored gentlemen wandering rubbish‑strewn slums.

Reactions diverge: some dismiss it as vanity; others celebrate unbreakable optimism. Few grasp its deeper historical roots.

During colonial‑times, adopting Western‑style formal‑wear offered colonised peoples one avenue to signal equality before white colonisers. Under Mobutu’s Authenticité policy, wearing Western‑suits became quiet symbolic resistance against authoritarian cultural‑edicts.

As national conditions worsened, acquiring fine garments grew harder. In a land crushed by war and hunger, outward dignity appeared all‑but‑destroyed. Sapeurs could not transform systemic realities, yet they seized fleeting personal self‑respect. Putting on a suit became an act of claiming human‑dignity.

La‑Sape is more than aesthetics; it carries behavioural‑codes. Sapeurs must reject crime, heavy‑drinking and physical‑fighting. Within a violent environment they strive to embody gentlemanly conduct. Even amid devastation, individuals fight to uphold self‑worth.

Against Kinshasa’s ruined city‑scape, sapeurs smile for cameras. Behind those smiles lies a nation scarred by layered tragedy, and ordinary citizens’ stubborn struggle for human‑dignity.
