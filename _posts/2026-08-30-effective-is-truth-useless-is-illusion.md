Which matters more: a high level of education, or high‑earning potential? Everyone has their own take on this question. If you lean toward the latter answer, you may be a pragmatist.

Pragmatism is a philosophical school of thought that emerged in the United States at the end of the 19th century. It draws on the empirical foundations of empiricism and incorporates the practical‑philosophy spirit of Aristotle, while taking a critical stance toward traditional metaphysics. Its core tenets are: truth is what works; ideas are tools for action; there are no absolute truths, only continuously verified experiences.

Having evolved over more than a hundred years, philosophical pragmatism has long shifted from a purely philosophical doctrine to a way of life. It permeates our lifestyles, emotional choices and value judgments, and exerts widespread influence across society.

“How much do I need to give?”
“What can I get in return?”
“Is this worth it?”

These are the questions that preoccupy modern people.

When choosing a career, a growing number of people do not follow their interests. Instead, they focus solely on whether an industry can make money. They believe passion cannot cover rent, utility bills, meals or commuting costs. Everyone wants to live freely, yet in reality, earning money is hard, and saving it is even harder. Running out of money by month‑end is a common situation.

In romantic relationships, pragmatists no longer rank romantic chemistry as the top priority; compatibility matters more. They invest less and exercise greater caution emotionally. They care more about what they can gain from a relationship and whether the partnership delivers good value.

Learning is another major domain where pragmatism prevails. Many people only want the key takeaways from lectures and only study content that will be tested. This has become a widely accepted mindset.

Pragmatism cannot be simply labeled right or wrong. It is a rational response shaped by real‑world pressures and holds merit in many scenarios. Still, not everything should be judged merely by short‑term practical utility. Unchecked pragmatism can slide into utilitarianism, leading to emotional detachment, obsession with profit, and unscrupulous conduct.

Pragmatism serves as a survival wisdom for modern society, yet it must be balanced by ideals, humanism and morality. We ought to acknowledge its contemporary value while recognizing its limitations. We should neither worship it blindly nor dismiss it outright. Treat pragmatism as a tool for solving real‑world problems. Grounded in practicality and guided by ideals — this is the real‑world philosophy that pragmatism offers us.
