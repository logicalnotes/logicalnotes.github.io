*Lord of War*, starring Nicolas Cage as an arms dealer, hit theatres in 2005. Many of its scenes featuring Soviet‑bloc weapons are not pure fiction; the story draws heavily on the real‑life Russian figure Viktor Bout.

Between 1993 and 2008, over fifteen years, Bout built a sprawling air freight network with more than fifty cargo planes operating across the globe. His aircraft shuttled to and from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Central African Republic, Uganda, Equatorial Guinea, Somalia, Libya, the Philippines, South Africa, Sudan, Afghanistan and other states.

Ordinary freight carriers avoided these poor, unstable routes, but Bout was eager for the business. His cargo was something far more sensitive: arms. Bout traded a vast catalogue of military hardware. Unlike the film’s focus on small arms and ammunition, he shipped large quantities of tanks, armoured vehicles, artillery pieces and helicopters into Africa. Thanks in large part to him, civil wars across the continent escalated from machete skirmishes to pitched battles fought with artillery and automatic fire.

Nearly all his stock originated in Russia and Eastern Europe. These regions held mountains of surplus weaponry, most of it inherited from one single power: the Soviet Union.

Human history has its unanswerable, eternal questions: Should pineapple go on pizza? Why is the goose so angry? And exactly how many weapons did the Big Brother state churn out?

Many analysts revisiting the Cold War keep circling back to one question: Could the Soviet Union ever have won it?

The odds were stacked heavily against it. Its hard power lagged far behind the United States. The USSR started from a weaker industrial base, with an economy and technology operating on a separate tier from America’s. The catastrophic destruction of the Second World War widened that gap even further. When you compare the entire Eastern and Western blocs, the imbalance becomes starker still: America’s allies boosted its strength, while the Soviet Union’s satellite states drained its resources nonstop.

Soviet planners calculated that an industrial population of 450 million could hold its ground against the combined global market of the U.S., Western Europe and Japan. To sustain that contest for half a century was already nothing short of a miracle.

If the USSR had a realistic path to prevailing in the Cold War, ideological influence was its best lever. Yet deep flaws within the Soviet system robbed it of that advantage.

Still, it was not completely without opportunity. A narrow window opened in the 1970s, when the Cold War shifted into a phase often described as “Soviet offensive, American defensive,” peaking mid‑decade.

For Western nations, the seventies were a brutal stretch. In 1973, Arab states stung by their defeat to Israel coordinated an oil embargo and production cuts to pressure Washington, Israel’s main backer. Oil prices more than tripled overnight. America’s long economic expansion ground to a halt — this was the first oil crisis.

A few years later, tensions flared in Iran in 1978, triggering the second oil crisis and tipping Western economies into a deep recession by the end of the decade. The oil shocks cascaded into an unprecedented period of stagflation across the developed world.

At the same time, the Vietnam War sank deeper into its darkest chapter. The U.S. was trapped in an unwinnable quagmire, its global prestige hitting historic lows. Disillusionment with policy and the war fuelled a wave of self‑doubt across American society. Left‑leaning intellectuals rose to prominence, and public debate fixated on whether the American system itself was failing.

Looking back, the praise some American workers heaped on the Soviet Union in that era borders on surreal. For ordinary young Americans, the decade felt disorienting and bleak. *Hotel California* emerged out of this mood, capturing widespread suspicion toward the nation’s own establishment.

While the U.S. stumbled, the Soviet Union reached its historical apex. Oil was its top export earner. The oil crises sent crude prices up nearly tenfold through the seventies, flooding state coffers. Combined with the postwar recovery and the rollout of massive industrial projects launched in the 1950s and 1960s, Soviet national power crested in this decade.

The balance of power flipped. Washington briefly explored détente and a world split into two spheres of influence. Had Moscow seized this moment, pulled back from confrontation, and poured capital and resources into domestic economic development and institutional reform, it might not have “won” the Cold War, but a fifty‑fifty stalemate was within reach.

That chance was squandered. Leonid Brezhnev led the USSR through this period. Instead of shoring up internal stability, he exploited this strategic opening to launch a global push designed to dismantle U.S. hegemony. The massive 1977 strategic airlift operation covered earlier was part of this broader campaign. Hundreds of heavy transport aircraft were deployed for interventions in Somalia — and that kind of reckless resource waste repeated across many corners of the globe.

Soviet advisors and Soviet arms turned up everywhere: Afghanistan, South Yemen, Somalia, Angola, Nicaragua, Colombia. Brezhnev channelled staggering sums into the military, locking the USSR into a cycle of reckless militarism.

A state that cannot rein in the vested interests of its military‑industrial complex courts disaster. That dynamic plagued the Soviet Union through the Cold War, and later the United States as well.

For Soviet arms factories, production budgets were disconnected from cost‑benefit calculations. Plant managers wanted ever larger production quotas, because more output meant bigger funding allocations and greater institutional clout.

Within that system, cheaper alternatives were often discarded even when they existed. Refitting a submarine might cost one unit, building a brand‑new hull five units, with nearly identical combat performance. The USSR still opted for new construction, so shipyards could secure larger budgets.

In the Soviet lexicon, “unemployment” carried inherent political stigma. The state was committed to keeping workers on payroll, so much manufacturing existed simply for the sake of production. Shipyards and weapons plants could not downsize, so Soviet military industry only expanded throughout the Cold War.

This distorted system created stockpiles of arms on a staggering scale. Even the Soviet Navy, widely seen as its weak point, grew strong enough to challenge the U.S. Navy by the seventies.

In the 1973 Yom Kippur War, the Soviet Mediterranean Fleet, ninety‑six ships strong, forced three U.S. carrier battle groups to back down. For the first time, Soviet surface combatants could stand toe‑to‑toe with the U.S. Navy. Two years later, the Soviet Navy held the massive *Ocean‑75* exercise, deploying 11 nuclear submarines, 24 conventional subs and 76 major surface warships. After the drill, U.S. military planners concluded they could not neutralize the Red Fleet — the conclusion directly accelerated development of the Aegis combat system.

Over the same period, the Soviet Air Force fielded tens of thousands of aircraft. Against this colossal inventory, America’s traditional air and naval edge eroded sharply by the late 1970s and early 1980s.

The USSR’s greatest advantage remained its ground forces. Its tank fleet was counted in the tens of thousands, a figure almost inconceivable for modern nations. In 1980, the Soviet Army’s two million troops were equipped with 55,000 tanks, roughly 70,000 armoured personnel carriers, around 50,000 artillery pieces and rocket launchers, plus an aviation arm of 6,000 helicopters.

By comparison, all NATO members combined fielded roughly 26,000 tanks, less than half the Soviet armoured fleet. Even at that size, production rates kept climbing, not slowing. Under Brezhnev, the USSR rolled out about 3,000 tanks, 5,000 armoured vehicles, 2,000 fixed‑wing aircraft and helicopters, 50,000 surface‑to‑air missiles and 400 intercontinental ballistic missiles every single year. It was building the full military inventory of a medium‑sized power, annually.

This was also the era when Soviet steel output hit its peak. From 1978 to 1991, the USSR led the world in steel production, hitting 147 million tons in 1982 — twice the U.S. output at the time, making it the planet’s dominant heavy‑industrial power. That number looked extraordinary then; by modern standards it is modest. For context: China produced roughly 1.325 billion tons of steel in 2020.

There were multiple drivers behind this flood of Soviet weaponry. Beyond the military‑industrial complex hijacking national economic priorities, Soviet military doctrine itself played a major role. In high‑level consultations with technical specialists, Andrei Tupolev, the towering figure of Soviet aviation design, famously argued: “We should match American weapons design, building comparable systems at the lowest practical cost. If our specs fall short? To hell with it. We beat them with numbers.”

A common misconception persists: Soviet weapons are rugged and reliable, while American hardware is delicate. That stereotype holds up better for small arms, but flips when it comes to major combat systems.

The Soviet military was built around a singular, defining mission: fighting a global total war. Every Soviet weapon design was tailored to that purpose. Lessons from the Second World War taught Soviet planners that perfection was unnecessary; serviceable and functional was enough.

Soviet technology lagged America’s in many critical component areas. Their workaround was simple: innovate around limitations.

Take the SR‑71 Blackbird, which entered U.S. service in 1966 and could sustain Mach 3 flight. Its titanium airframe handled extreme heat generated at speed. The USSR could not afford titanium or master its machining requirements. Their solution was to build the MiG‑25 mostly from stainless steel. That pushed its takeoff weight up to 37 tons. Soviet engineers’ answer was equally blunt: more thrust. With two powerful engines and a custom airframe, they proved an old aviation maxim: “If you have enough thrust, even a brick will fly.”

The MiG‑25 went on to shatter world speed records, peaking at Mach 3.2, fast enough to outrun some air‑to‑air missiles. Israeli F‑4 Phantom pilots once fired Sidewinder missiles at an Egyptian MiG‑25; the pilot simply accelerated and left the missiles far behind.

Once the MiG‑25 entered service, SR‑71 overflights of Soviet airspace ceased. Its performance stunned U.S. intelligence, who assumed the USSR had unlocked some revolutionary breakthrough. Years later, when American analysts got their hands on a defected MiG‑25 and saw its stainless‑steel construction, the mystique crumbled.

Soviet engineers cobbled together competitive capability from materials U.S. designers dismissed. This pattern is later called the “MiG‑25 effect.” The MiG‑25 and Blackbird remain a defining Cold War parable. Measured by pure technical refinement, the MiG‑25 cannot compare to the Blackbird.

This production model drained national resources relentlessly over time. Most U.S. combat aircraft were multi‑role workhorses, while Soviet jets were highly specialised, built for one narrow mission and hard to repurpose. Engineers prioritised one or two core performance metrics instead of balancing every requirement. The calculus was direct: one U.S. plane handles three jobs; we send three dedicated aircraft to match it. Quantity compensated for technical tradeoffs. The cost, however, was immense.

By the late Cold War, the USSR fit the description “recklessly militarised.” Its military spending at one point exceeded the U.S. budget, yet waste and inefficiency prevented it from gaining decisive military superiority.

Mikhail Gorbachev came to power and laid bare the scale of the system’s rot. Defence spending consumed 60 percent of the state budget, 20 percent of total national output, and 80 percent of all research funding. For a peacetime nation, its military burden resembled that of a country at full war.

Resources that could have fuelled civilian growth poured endlessly into the arms race. This arrangement stayed barely viable while oil prices soared through the seventies, but cracked badly in the eighties — a major factor in the Soviet Union’s collapse.

The fall of the USSR in 1991 sent shockwaves still felt today. We have covered its massive stockpiles of heavy armour; its stockpiles of small arms were even more staggering.

At the end of the Cold War, Soviet battalions stationed across Eastern Europe numbered four or five hundred troops each. Those units might hold forty thousand rifles. The small arms stock of one Soviet battalion could fully equip an entire army in another country.

During the Cold War, many of these weapons were supplied free of charge to third‑world states; wealthier clients bought them at heavily subsidised rates. After the Eastern Bloc collapsed, those subsidies vanished, and the mountain of surplus arms became merchandise for sale.

Arms traffickers swarmed Eastern Europe. Soviet troops had gone unpaid, struggling to feed themselves, and local governments had no capacity to step in. Units quickly turned arms sales into their primary income stream. Stockpiles towered, and these weapons, built with no regard for production cost, soon sold for pennies on the dollar.

Take the AK‑47, the deadliest firearm in modern history. A widespread myth claims it is inherently indestructible in every variant. That is only true for original Soviet factory specimens.

The low‑cost reputation of Kalashnikovs applies mostly to unlicensed copies and homemade knockoffs. Original Soviet AK‑47s were not cheap to manufacture. Factories used generous material specs, hard‑chrome plated critical parts, and solid walnut stocks — the source of their legendary reliability. Copies built in Eastern Europe or China varied wildly in quality, many far less robust.

The USSR manufactured tens of millions of these well‑built AK‑47s. Once the Union dissolved, what became of them? Some sold for as little as two U.S. dollars apiece in the 1990s. Traffickers bought them by the tens of thousands. Within four months of the collapse, over a million AK‑47s were smuggled out of Ukraine alone, and that was only a fraction of the global illicit trade.

Ukraine was not the cheapest market. At remote former Soviet outposts, starving troops sometimes traded an AK‑47 for a single tin of rations. In the Russian Far East, military trucks swapped for bars of soap or blankets were not unheard of.

It was not just small arms. Tanks and helicopters were stripped and sold off; entire divisions’ inventories were liquidated as troops scrambled for cash. At the Vidyaevo naval base of the Northern Fleet, a sailor stole a precious metal temperature sensor from a nuclear submarine reactor and sold it for a mere thousand rubles. Even during the First Chechen War, Russian troops and Chechen fighters traded weapons on the battlefield.

In 2002, a Russian warehouse officer stole three communications modules from a fighter jet. He was intercepted before the parts leaked abroad; had they reached the black market, the consequences would have been catastrophic.

In the decade after the Soviet collapse, arms trafficking cases prosecuted by Russian prosecutors totalled more than 10 billion U.S. dollars in value — higher than Russia’s entire annual military budget at the time.

Viktor Bout, the trafficker mentioned at the start, was once a Soviet army major, schooled in Soviet military academies and fluent in eight languages. His posting to Angola honed his language skills and built the network that underpinned his later business.

When the USSR dissolved, he leveraged old contacts to buy three cargo planes and launch his arms‑smuggling enterprise. His first major client was the Taliban, and his trade exploded from there. His buyers included the Tamil Tigers in Sri Lanka, the Revolutionary United Front in Sierra Leone, and even Muammar Gaddafi. More often than not, he supplied weapons to parties under United Nations arms embargoes, filling a gap no legitimate supplier would touch.

In 2008, U.S. agents posing as Colombian drug lords lured him into a sting operation in Thailand and arrested him. The world got its first full look at the most prolific arms trafficker of his generation.

Bout rarely handled the weapons he shipped in person. He loved wildlife photography and regularly sent photos of African animals to his wife. He was also a donor to UNICEF, soft‑spoken and mild‑mannered, easy to underestimate. Yet this unassuming man funnelled countless weapons of death across continents.

Bout’s fate mirrors that of the AK‑47 itself. The rifle was designed originally to dominate battlefields and dismantle exploitative capitalist systems, built as a rugged instrument meant to liberate people. Instead, millions fell into the hands of African warlords, turned into a tool for profit and mutual slaughter.

Only when gunfire rings out, bullets tracing clean rifling down its barrel, and the firing pin snaps home again and again, do people catch a faint echo: this loyal sentinel of a fallen red empire, its final glow.
