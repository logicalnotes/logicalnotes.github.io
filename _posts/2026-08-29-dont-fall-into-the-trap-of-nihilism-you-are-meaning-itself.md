Today we are going to talk about nihilism.

Excessive rumination over the inner spiritual world can drag people into nihilism. Like a chronic illness, it gnaws away at your mind little by little. Once caught in its grip, you come to see all human joys and sorrows as nothing but fleeting illusions. Even history’s great heroes cannot escape death, and will be forgotten by the world centuries later.

If extraordinary individuals fade from memory, ordinary people seem to lose their purpose as well. All your joys and griefs, honour and disgrace, status and worth, are ultimately reduced to the dash etched between birth and death dates on a tombstone — while wild flowers bloom all around it.

You keep asking your inner self: Why am I alive? Why do I exist? When these questions detach from real‑life experience and turn into pure, self‑devouring intellectual speculation, it is like a blade grinding endlessly against your soul. What gets worn away is not confusion, but meaning itself.

This is nihilism: the rejection of all established meanings, values and truths. Its core conviction holds that the world has no ultimate purpose, life bears no inherent worth, and morality has no absolute standards. Everything is accidental and empty.

Nietzsche is the most renowned thinker who unpacked nihilism. His proclamation “God is dead” was no celebration, but a lament. Traditional religious and moral frameworks had collapsed. Old sources of meaning had vanished, leaving humanity rootless, adrift in an era where meaning was dissolving.

Young people today face this very crisis. Material abundance abounds, yet spiritual emptiness prevails. Phrases like “woke to reality” or “self‑sufficient, needing no one else”, which circulate widely on social media, may look like freedom. Beneath them lie profound disillusionment and alienation — clear signs of nihilistic sentiment.

Nihilism can drain your will to act, pushing you toward cynicism, self‑denial and even depression. Yet Nietzsche argued that the real struggle is not to destroy meaning, but to rebuild it after nihilism.

Nihilism is not the end of reflection. Rather, it is a prelude to insight, the darkest hour before dawn. It invalidates every thought and deed you hold dear. Still, turning back to lived experience strips away worldly utilitarian urges and ingrained social mindsets.

Ask yourself: what still matters deeply to you? When you act free from the expectations of others, from fame, gain, social norms or dogma, you reach a state of awakening. Whether you choose to do something or not springs from within, not external pressures. That is when you hear the authentic voice of your soul.

One final thought: Don’t fall into the trap of nihilism. You are meaning itself.
