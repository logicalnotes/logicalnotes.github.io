The earliest identifiable culture we can trace in Italy is the Villanovan culture. It laid the groundwork out of which Roman civilisation would later grow.

Roughly in the centuries around 1000 BC, wave after wave of migrants arrived on the Italian peninsula. Some came overland from Central Europe, others crossed the sea. Building on Villanovan traditions, these newcomers developed their own distinct ways of life and formed separate ethnic groups.

One towering 19th‑century German scholar of Roman history, Theodor Mommsen, wrote his monumental *A History of Rome* — a work that won the Nobel Prize in Literature and remains a classic for Roman history readers today. Mommsen sorted these incoming peoples into three main groups.

## 1. The Iapygians

The Iapygians were the first major group to settle after the rise of Villanovan culture. They migrated south all the way down to the “heel” and “toe” of Italy’s boot shape.
Over time, they were gradually absorbed by the Italic peoples expanding from the north and the Greek settlers arriving by sea, until their identity faded into history.

## 2. The Italic peoples

Next came the Italic peoples. They settled mostly across central Italy and pushed the earlier Iapygians further south.
Because they spread across most of the peninsula, we call them the Italics. Inside this broad group were many subgroups: the Latins, Sabines, Umbrians, plus the Marsi and Samnites further south.
The Latins matter most of all. The earliest Romans belonged to this Latin branch, and the plain they occupied became known as Latium. This is where Roman civilisation first took root.

## 3. The Etruscans

A little later, the Etruscans settled the north‑west of the peninsula, in what is now Tuscany.
Scholars still disagree about their origins. Some argue they migrated across the sea from Asia Minor, modern‑day Turkey. Others think they moved down from Central Europe. A third theory holds they were native to the peninsula all along.

Starting around the 8th century BC, seafaring Greeks launched a wide wave of colonial expansion across the Mediterranean. Greek communities sprung up along the coasts of southern Italy and Sicily. The Iapygians who had lived there were steadily assimilated by both Italic tribes and these new Greek arrivals.

Greek colonists had a clear habit: they stuck to the shoreline and rarely pushed deep inland, fitting their identity as a sea‑faring people.
So along the coasts of Italy’s southern boot, plus eastern and southern Sicily, many Greek city‑states appeared. These included Cumae, close to modern Naples, Naples itself, Croton, Tarentum and Brundisium. On Sicily you find Messina, Catania, Syracuse and Agrigentum. This whole stretch had once been Iapygian land.

Central Italy belonged to the various Italic tribes: Latins in the north, Campanians to the west, Samnites further south.
West of the Italic lands lay Etruscan territory. The Etruscans had settled there before the 8th century BC and kept pushing south. At their peak they controlled Campania north of Cumae, facing off directly against Greek power based south of Cumae.

Further north still, from Etruscan and Italic territory all the way up to the Alps, stretched the lands the ancients considered barbarian.
Nomadic tribes roamed the Po River valley. Among them were the Ligures and the fierce Gauls. The Gauls living north of the Alps would become the ancestors of the French.
