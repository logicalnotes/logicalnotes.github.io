Late on July 28, 1981, Moscow police officer Chernov was jolted awake by a shrill phone call.

He swore under his breath after hanging up. The caller reported a robbery and theft at a Moscow location.

Yet it was not the crime itself that stunned him. As a seasoned Moscow cop, he had seen murders and assaults so often they felt routine. What made his blood run cold was the address: the Arkhangelskoye Estate.

Built in the 17th century, Arkhangelskoye first served as the private residence of a Russian duke, and later became a famous social hub for the imperial nobility.

After the Soviet takeover, most of the grounds were seized and converted into a museum, sanatorium and military club. The core mansion, however, remained an exclusive venue for elite gatherings — only now, the guests were the children of Moscow’s top party officials.

These secret gatherings were nicknamed “Night Lights”. Members gathered at the estate to indulge in Western-style luxuries. The guest list was tightly guarded, and no outsiders were permitted entry.

Now something had gone wrong here. Anyone in attendance could outrank a regular policeman like Chernov. Still, he could not ignore the emergency call. With a heavy heart, as if heading to a funeral, he drove toward the estate. A waitress met him and recounted what had unfolded that night.

The “Night Lights” party was underway. After dinner, the hall lights dimmed, and guests danced by candlelight. Midway through the dance, one guest cried out that his antique 19th-century silver-gilt snuffbox had vanished.

Chaos erupted, and many others realised they too had lost personal belongings. Worst of all, the most prominent woman at the ball had also been robbed.

The guest drawing every eye that night was a 52-year-old woman. Russian women often struck people as striking in youth, and many retained formidable physical strength as they aged. This woman had been married three times. She was not conventionally beautiful, yet she commanded the room; young men vied for her attention, as sought-after as public Wi‑Fi.

Chernov wondered why she held such sway.

The waitress told him her name: Galina Leonidovna Brezhneva.

Chernov nearly blacked out. She was Leonid Brezhnev’s daughter.

Once he recovered, he asked what she had lost. What treasure could tempt someone to steal from Brezhnev’s daughter?

The waitress delivered a second bombshell. The stolen item was a diamond brooch, once owned by Empress Alexandra Feodorovna. It was a priceless national relic, and Galina had only borrowed it to wear for the evening.

Chernov quietly asked the waitress if they could pretend he had never arrived, to re‑route the emergency call. The answer was no.

He forced himself to ask the third terrible question: who had been killed?

The waitress said the victim was not one of the party guests. When the guests discovered the theft, none of them suspected a thief at first — who would dare break into a gathering under Brezhnev’s protection? They turned on the lights and searched the building. At the back of the kitchen, they found an open window streaked with blood. Outside beneath the window lay a corpse.

Chernov exhaled in relief and asked to see the body. When he laid eyes on the dead man, he was shaken a third time.

Chernov recognised the face instantly. The dead man was Nikolai Regunov, a legendary Moscow crime boss and a respected thief in law.

Anyone familiar with Soviet organised crime knows the thieves in law. These men had survived the Gulag and retained their criminal status well into the Brezhnev era. Nikolai had spent 17 years of his life behind bars, earning immense prestige in prison, with a huge network of underlings across Moscow. Now the kingpin was dead on the estate grounds. The underworld would erupt.

Elite party gathering, Brezhnev’s daughter present, a national treasure stolen, a legendary thief in law murdered. Chernov thought his ordinary police career had somehow collected four explosive scandals in one night. Still, the investigation had to proceed.

Chernov asked the waitress if she had spotted anyone suspicious. The waitress said she could not recall, and suggested he question the guests directly — a deliberate trap. Chernov’s rank gave him no authority to interrogate the children of senior officials.

But if he could not question the VIPs, he could pressure the staff. After some pushing, the waitress “remembered” a strange young man. He was handsome, the boyfriend of Inge, daughter of a senior official at the State Planning Committee. He danced clumsily, bumping into other guests, and left abruptly with Inge halfway through the night.

Finally, a lead. Ordinarily, Chernov would never have been allowed to summon someone like Inge for questioning. But because the case touched Galina Brezhneva, the Ministry of Internal Affairs granted special permission.

Inge arrived at the station uncooperative. She answered every question with denials: she knew nothing, had seen nothing, could not confirm anything. She insisted she had stolen nothing, barely knew the young man, and had no idea where he had gone.

With a regular suspect, Chernov would have used standard interrogation tactics. With Inge, he dared not raise his voice, let alone apply pressure. When interrogating other criminals, he warned them to think of their families. When interrogating Inge, he kept reminding himself to think of his own family.

Still, the veteran detective had a plan. One girl might stay silent, but her whole circle of friends would not. Chernov mapped out Inge’s social circle and tracked down an acquaintance, who gave him a name: Andrey Kurgaev.

With the name, the investigation moved forward. Detectives compiled his full record.

Born in 1962 in Ukraine into a circus family, Andrey’s father was a skilled acrobat who was left paralysed after an accident. Left unsupervised, Andrey drifted into street trouble. Then he was recruited by an unusual talent scout: an experienced pickpocket named Lenya Smik.

Unlike petty thieves, Lenya had ambitions to professionalise theft. He believed lone operators could not compete with organised training, so he set up an underground thief school in Ukraine.

He wrote lesson plans, built a curriculum, and prepared props for hands-on drills.

He spotted Andrey’s natural agility, inherited from his circus background. Lenya told the boy he was born to steal, and Andrey became his apprentice, learning the full training system.

The training unfolded in stages:
First, he practised lifting wallets from mannequins dressed in everyday clothing; he passed only if he could remove the wallet without shifting the fabric.
Second, he trained against other apprentices, stealing from them without triggering any awareness.
Third, live, unscripted field practice. He had to strike in crowded spaces, steal, and walk away without pausing or looking back.

Andrey had exceptional talent. He could slip a wallet out, take the cash, and slide the empty wallet back into the pocket without a trace.

Just as he mastered the craft, his teacher Lenya was caught red-handed while demonstrating his skills for the students. Watching the veteran thief get arrested drove home the danger. Andrey fled Ukraine for Moscow.

In Moscow, he could have found honest work with his physical gifts, but the city’s job market was brutal for outsiders. Shut out, he returned to petty theft.

One day in a park, he met Inge. He was handsome, and despite the gulf in their social status, Inge fell for him at once.

Andrey hid his real trade. He told her he was an artisan hiding from enemies. When Inge pressed him to show his craft, he demonstrated by silently lifting 300 rubles from a guest’s wallet and putting it back. Inge did not understand it was theft, and they quickly fell in love.

From then on, Inge brought Andrey to elite parties. That brought him to the fatal “Night Lights” gathering on July 28, 1981. After the party, Andrey vanished. Inge kept her mouth shut, and the Soviet Union was vast enough to hide a fugitive. Worse, the Moscow underworld was hunting him. If the gangsters found him first, the police would never get a chance to question him.

As Chernov stalled, Inge announced she was pregnant and requested leave to go home. Her status made it impossible to detain her. Chernov, however, posted surveillance around her apartment, convinced Andrey would come for her.

A few days later, a woman arrived at Inge’s door. The watching officers moved to stop her, and she pulled a knife and attacked. She was subdued immediately. When they pulled off her wig and headscarf, they found Andrey underneath.

With Andrey in custody, Chernov could interrogate him properly. Before any harsh methods were used, Andrey’s nerve broke, and he confessed to what happened that night.

Andrey had come to the party with Inge with no plan to steal. Robbing Brezhnev’s daughter was a death sentence. But Inge was caught up in the party and never introduced him to the other guests. Andrey watched everyone crowd around Galina Brezhneva, while his girlfriend was overlooked. He grew resentful. He planned a prank: steal small items from several guests and plant them on the prominent woman, to embarrass her in front of the room. At that moment, he had no idea who she was. Had he known it was Brezhnev’s daughter, he would never have dared.

While he was picking pockets, a hard‑faced man stared him down. He flashed a criminal hand signal, ordering Andrey to follow. It was Nikolai Regunov. Arkhangelskoye hosted not only elite parties, but also an underground casino run by Nikolai. He had spotted the trespassing thief and intended to discipline him, to enforce the underworld rule that no one robbed on his territory.

Andrey was a product of the thief school, with no respect for the old Gulag‑born code of the thieves in law. When Nikolai chewed him out, the young hothead pulled a knife and stabbed him in the chest.

Nikolai ran. He could have called for help, but the thieves’ code forbade him from turning to state authorities even when injured. Bleeding badly, he fled silently. Andrey chased him over the fence, and the legendary crime boss died there.

In one night, Andrey had humiliated Brezhnev’s daughter and killed a senior thief in law.

Chernov listened to the confession and largely accepted his account. But one critical question remained unanswered: where was the diamond brooch?

When the brooch was mentioned, the cooperative suspect clammed up. He admitted murder freely, but refused to admit he had taken the brooch.

Chernov saw two possibilities: either he truly had not stolen it, or revealing its whereabouts would bring a fate worse than death. Chernov leaned toward the second theory. All guests had been searched as they left, leaving only Andrey and Inge with the chance to smuggle it out. The detective believed he would crack the truth with more questioning.

Interrogations dragged on for days. Andrey broke down in tears, rambling about his foolish pride and how he had destroyed himself and Inge. He was on the verge of full confession.

But before the breakthrough, in the early hours of August 28, 1981, Andrey was found strangled in his jail cell. The 19‑year‑old fugitive died in custody, his death unexplained.

Chernov stared at the body with mixed feelings. The sensational case was closed officially, lifting the crushing pressure on him. Yet a young man was dead, and the truth was buried with him.

The official autopsy report made three findings: Andrey was the thief who took the brooch; his death was a suicide; the brooch could not be located.

The report closed the case for the state, but it sparked endless gossip across the Soviet Union. Brezhnev’s daughter, a priceless royal jewel, a gifted young thief, a murder, and elite scandal — all the ingredients of a myth. While Brezhnev lived, no one dared turn it into art or literature, but rumours spread everywhere.

Theories multiplied. Some said Andrey was a pawn for powerful people and silenced after the job. Others whispered that Galina Brezhneva had wanted the brooch for herself and framed Andrey for the theft. Galina already had a controversial reputation, so many believed she was capable of it.

The full truth has vanished into history. The diamond brooch has never resurfaced. The young Ukrainian thief remains only as a tragic figure in Soviet folklore.
