Do you tend to suffer from mental overthinking? Some people keep ruminating over a casual remark they made days ago. Others lie awake all night because of a hurtful comment from someone else. They drain enormous energy through endless pointless obsessions, regrets and self‑criticism. But why does this happen?

More than two thousand years ago, a group of Roman philosophers figured this out: the Stoics. Stoicism is a philosophical school from ancient Greece and Rome. Its core idea is to focus on what we can control, accept what we cannot, and approach life with reason.

Its first principle is to draw a clear line between what is within our control and what is not. Our thoughts, beliefs and own actions fall under our control. External events, other people’s opinions, life‑and‑death matters and wealth, by contrast, are beyond our power. Only by letting go of attachment to uncontrollable things can we attain true freedom and avoid unnecessary suffering.

Suppose you send a message to a friend and get no reply for hours. Your mind starts racing: Did I say something wrong? Am I being too pushy? Does this person actually dislike me? You scroll back and forth through your chat history, staring at their profile picture and getting lost in speculation.

Stoicism reminds us that the other person’s response, mood, behaviour and the ultimate fate of the relationship are outside our control. Stop fighting things you cannot change. Shift your attention to what you can influence, and you will live with greater clarity.

Stoics also hold that the highest way to live as rational human beings is to follow the laws of nature. To follow nature is to follow reason. Seasons change, lives begin and end — these are all part of the natural order. Resistance to what is inevitable brings pain; acceptance is an act of reason.

The philosophy advocates easing negative emotions through rational thinking to achieve inner peace. According to Stoicism, negative feelings such as anxiety, depression, anger and fear stem not from events themselves, but from our flawed judgements about them. When insulted, for instance, our anger arises from the assumption that “others ought to respect me”. Reason tells us other people’s attitudes are out of our hands; all we can govern is our inner world.

Epictetus wrote: “Difficulty is what wakes up the genius.” It urges us to embrace whatever life throws our way. Since hardship cannot always be avoided, we may as well treat it as an opportunity for growth.

In today’s anxious, hyper‑competitive world, Stoicism offers a sober yet resilient philosophy of life. It teaches us to separate the controllable from the uncontrollable and invest energy in our own judgements and actions. Faced with pressure, failure and public opinion, we preserve inner calm. We do not run from hardship, nor do we rely on external validation. We stay true to ourselves amid adversity. Even when everything outside seems to fall apart, we retain the capacity to keep our inner world steady.
