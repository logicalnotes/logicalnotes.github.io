There is a counter‑intuitive insight in psychology: the more certain a person’s inner world is, the more it may point to psychological dysfunction.

We often come across people who fly into rage and lash out the moment they encounter opinions conflicting with their own. This is a clear sign of high paranoia, and the degree of paranoia serves as a key indicator of one’s mental‑health status.

Many mistake the absence of inner conflict and consistent beliefs for mental maturity. The truth is quite the opposite: inner doubts, conflicts and uncertainties are markers of relatively sound mental health. They reveal an open‑minded personality, one capable of forming fluid connections with the outside world. Such people can take in new information and allow their perspectives to shift when exposed to alternative viewpoints.

By contrast, individuals stuck in rigid inner certainty hold fast to the belief that their views are absolutely correct. They tend to build all kinds of snob hierarchies and place themselves at the top to judge others. This condescending attitude is not genuine self‑confidence, but pathological narcissism. Their fragile sense of self relies on dismissing others for validation. Whenever opposing views emerge and threaten their belief system, they defend themselves with anger and contempt. This pattern calls for deeper self‑reflection and psychological exploration.

Letting go of paranoia grants us genuine inner ease. This ease is neither total apathy nor a lack of principles. It means we stop clinging to one single set of answers. We can embrace the comfort of slowing down, while also choosing to strive for growth. We can admire the warmth of extroverts as well as the quiet steadiness of introverts, rejecting rigid either‑or thinking.

I have met many senior figures in psychology, most of whom are humble and open‑minded. Even when they disagree with other schools of thought, they seek common ground while respecting differences, acknowledging the value behind diverse theories.

The optimal mental state lies in this flexible mindset of non‑attachment to fixed conclusions. By embracing inner uncertainty instead of locking every thought into place, we break down cognitive barriers and broaden our horizons.

As F. Scott Fitzgerald put it: “The test of a first‑rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in the mind at the same time, and still retain the ability to function.”

Truly robust minds do not demand permanent certainty. They dare to accommodate contradictions and keep living fully amid uncertainty.
