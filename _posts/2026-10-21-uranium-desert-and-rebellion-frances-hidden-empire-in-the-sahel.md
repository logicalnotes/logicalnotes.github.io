On February 13, 1960, deep in the Sahara, a camel’s pupils caught the glow of two suns. Dozens of kilometres away at a nuclear test site, soldiers stared, their tricorn insignia glinting. They burned with the thrill of redeeming old humiliations.

Twenty years had passed since the collapse of the Maginot Line, seven since the shame of Điện Biên Phủ. All of that was now supposed to be behind them. Their nation remained Western Europe’s largest power, one of the five great global powers. This newly detonated atomic bomb, they believed, would forever secure the glory of the French Republic.

While the nation celebrated, few noticed that ants of the Sahara had already crept into French military boots.

To reach this moment, France had dissolved the Fourth Republic and brought Charles de Gaulle back to power. This glowing fireball was meant to intimidate the rising Algerian independence movement and stop Vietnam’s defeat from repeating itself across Africa. History would soon prove the bomb failed to preserve de Gaulle’s French Community. Yet the nuclear technology it unlocked promised France lasting prosperity and strength.

From the lights of Paris beneath the Eiffel Tower, to crowded docks in Marseille; from Airbus A380 assembly lines to the French nuclear aircraft carrier *Charles de Gaulle*. Today, over 70 percent of France’s electricity comes from nuclear power. Its reactors are, in a real sense, France’s stomach, feeding the pride of the Gallic rooster and the image of Marianne.

Few stop to ask what fuels this stomach. Behind that abundant consumption, who is paying the price?

To find the answer, we leave the blue coasts of Provence and return to where this story began — sand and dust, carrying the stench of exploitation.

Welcome to the Sahara.

Most picture the Sahara only as a land of death. Few know its winds lift 500 million tonnes of mineral nutrients each year, carrying them across the Atlantic to fertilise the Amazon. The Sahara nurtures desolation, yet feeds life far beyond its borders. It is a natural metaphor for the human order here.

Deep in the desert sits Niger, West Africa’s largest country by land area and one of the world’s least developed nations. On the surface, 80 percent of its territory lies under the Sahara. But the deeper reason is that its most valuable industry does not belong to its people: uranium mining.

Niger’s uranium mines are run by a state-style enterprise — only this enterprise flies a French flag. It is Areva, once the world’s leading nuclear energy firm. Every year, Areva ships roughly 3,000 tonnes of uranium ore out of Niger, supplying one‑third of France’s uranium needs.

For a nation built on nuclear power, Niger’s uranium is a critical support beam. Canada is another major uranium supplier. While Canada was loosely tied to France’s old colonial sphere, Paris never held real sway there. Niger, by contrast, was a battery France kept firmly in its grip.

The trouble is that once France drained this “battery”, Niger was left as an empty agricultural shell. Most Nigeriens remain farmers and herders. Their daily lives resemble those of ancient desert nomads: three hours each way just to fetch water, tending cattle and sheep, surviving at the mercy of the weather.

In some ways, modern Tuareg and Nigerien herders face worse harm than their ancestors. Radiation from Areva’s mines has poisoned pastureland and eroded local health, a threat no pre‑colonial nomad ever knew.

Still, modern people hold an option their forebears lacked: to flee. When home offers no way to survive, people move toward opportunity.

This path is travelled not only by Nigeriens. West Africa’s poor have taken it for years. The European refugee crisis is often misunderstood: the biggest source of refugees is not the well‑publicised Syria, but West Africa. Syrian refugees dominate headlines, yet the bulk of migrant arrivals come from Africa.

Among these migrants is a group, the poorest of the poor, the most marginalised and wronged. Over the past decade, they sparked a storm that shook France’s old order in West Africa and forced the former colonial power to deploy troops to clean up a crisis it had helped create.

This is where our central figures enter: the Tuareg, a Berber people, heirs to Carthaginian traditions and Ibadi Islam, stateless and displaced.

Most readers will not recognise the name. The Tuareg are indigenous to the Sahara, a Berber subgroup, and one of the most marginalised Amazigh communities. Students of North Africa know Berber is a cultural identity for nomads who resisted Arab linguistic assimilation. The Tuareg still use the ancient Tifinagh script, unlike many other Berber groups who adopted Arabic writing.

They are a gathering of the dispossessed across the Sahara. Tuareg communities stretch across Algeria, Libya, Niger, Mali and Burkina Faso, spanning most of the desert, yet they form a minority inside every one of these five states.

This fragmented condition is itself a legacy of French rule. During the 19th‑century Scramble for Africa, European powers drew borders for political convenience, ignoring geography and indigenous societies. When African nations won independence in the late 20th century, they inherited these arbitrary colonial boundaries. Some attempted federal unions, such as the Mali Federation linking Mali and Senegal, but internal disputes and outside interference tore it apart.

Groups like the Tuareg were split across multiple new countries. From the old imperial perspective, this division was useful: it let former colonial powers retain influence, act as mediators and intervene when convenient. For Africans, it is a crippling colonial hangover. Geographic splits and ethnic friction have slowed development for generations. Healing these wounds takes immense time and pain.

When French troops pushed into West Africa in the late 1800s, they met fierce Tuareg resistance. The Tuareg had built tribal confederations and waged a guerrilla war that lasted thirty years. Their tribal alliances proved tougher than many older West African kingdoms.

It is no surprise that during decolonisation in the 1960s, France used border demarcation to weaken them. Divide and rule is an old imperial playbook, and it worked. For decades after independence, the Tuareg could not mount large‑scale uprisings, yet discrimination and exclusion within their host states never stopped.

Still, West Africa had long been a tinderbox. The Tuareg’s accumulated suffering only needed a spark.

That spark came in 2011, from Muammar Gaddafi.

In March 2011, the French carrier *Charles de Gaulle* sailed toward Libya’s coast. Gaddafi warned NATO that his fall would unleash uncontrolled migration toward Europe. His warning failed to save him, yet it proved prescient.

Gaddafi’s regime had long acted as a safety valve, managing the migration route from the Sahel to the Mediterranean. Libya’s oil wealth let him contain migrant flows, and he maintained a fragile working relationship with Western Europe, France included. Libya was also one of France’s suppliers of oil and uranium. Years later, former French president Nicolas Sarkozy faced charges of accepting cash from Gaddafi.

Gaddafi’s rupture with NATO and his overthrow have been widely analysed elsewhere. What matters here is how he shaped the fate of the Tuareg.

Gaddafi was a pan‑Africanist who spent lavishly to cast himself as an African leader. Libya was also run along traditional tribal lines, marked by deep distrust between clans. These two facts together led him to rely heavily on foreign mercenaries: loyal, battle‑hardened fighters. He turned to the Tuareg. As migrant labour networks tend to cluster by kinship, large numbers of Tuareg men joined his forces.

In Libya’s civil war, these Tuareg mercenaries fought fiercely for their patron, forming one of Gaddafi’s most reliable units. But they could not stand against French Rafale jets. When the regime collapsed, the unemployed fighters headed home, bringing their weapons with them. Once guns returned to the desert, conflict became inevitable.

Gaddafi’s fall unlocked two floodgates: one sending migrants north toward the Mediterranean, another flooding the Sahel with weapons from Libya. Arms and displaced men rippled south, destabilising a region already teetering.

Back in their homelands, the returning Tuareg fighters faced grinding poverty with no outlet for their military skills. Some saw armed rebellion as the only way to end systemic exclusion. They began training in desert camps, waiting to strike.

In January 2012, Tuareg fighters attacked Malian army positions and won early victories.

Mali itself is a state cobbled together by colonial borders. Its sparsely populated north belongs to the Sahara, while the political and economic heart lies far to the south. The two zones differ sharply in ethnicity and power sharing. The Tuareg uprising hit Mali at its weakest point.

After a string of military defeats, Malian soldiers frustrated by government corruption seized power in March 2012. International pressure deepened the chaos, pushing Mali close to statelessness.

Tuareg rebels kept advancing in the north and seized Gao, the ancient capital of the Songhai Empire. On April 6, 2012, the National Movement for the Liberation of Azawad (MNLA) declared the independent state of Azawad. “Azawad” means “land of migration”. Notably, the MNLA leader announced this independence during an interview on France 24, a news outlet with huge reach across West Africa.

In its declaration, the movement pledged to respect the UN Charter and existing colonial borders. Yet no country recognised Azawad. Both ECOWAS and France affirmed Mali’s territorial integrity.

By May 2012, under external pressure, the MNLA allied temporarily with Islamist armed groups. Their goals were irreconcilable, and the alliance broke apart within a month, followed by fighting between the two sides. That brief partnership, however, muddied the situation in northern Mali and gave outside powers a pretext for military intervention.

France moved to protect its interests when Mali’s government teetered. François Hollande took office as French president in May 2012. When France’s sphere of influence was threatened, the left‑wing president acted without hesitation.

Northern Mali held oil and uranium deposits. More critically, an Azawad state bordered Niger, which was also home to large Tuareg communities. Chaos there could easily spill into Niger and threaten France’s core uranium supply.

A formal pretext was ready. Invited by Mali and backed by several West African governments, France pushed for intervention under a UN framework. Late in 2012, France deployed over four thousand troops alongside regional allies. In early 2013, Malian and French forces defeated the MNLA and retook all major northern cities within three months.

In June 2013, Mali’s government and Tuareg armed groups signed a peace deal. Peace never truly settled. Low‑level clashes between Malian forces and Tuareg factions continue to this day. French troops maintained a long‑term presence across West Africa after 2012.

After Gaddafi’s collapse, armed insurgency and migrant crises spread in waves across the Sahel: Mali first, then Chad, the Central African Republic, Mauritania and Niger. As late as 2021, France kept more than five thousand soldiers stationed in West Africa.

Azawad vanished quickly, yet the first shot of this long crisis was fired by the Tuareg. Still, they are not the root cause, nor was Gaddafi, nor Sahelian jihadist groups. The poverty and unrest across this region revolve around Niger’s uranium — and the whole colonial‑era order built around that resource.

Against great powers, the Tuareg are small, without a state of their own. But in the court of facts, logic and basic justice, they stand equal to every major nation. Their struggle reveals truths about global empire as profound as any chapter in history.

To see the full galaxy, you cannot stay at its centre. You must stand on the edge.
