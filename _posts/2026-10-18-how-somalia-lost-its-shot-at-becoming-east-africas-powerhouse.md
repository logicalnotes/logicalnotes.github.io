In 2001, the American film *Black Hawk Down* hit theaters. It recounted the botched 1993 capture operation by US troops stationed in Somalia. Thanks to its gritty, hyper-realistic style, Ridley Scott earned a nomination for Best Director at the 74th Academy Awards. The movie also introduced the world to Somalia, a chaotic nation on the eastern edge of Africa.

Somalia’s next moment in the global spotlight arrived years later: the rise of Somali piracy. Sitting astride critical maritime shipping lanes, its unique geographic position made piracy thrive. Once Somali pirates emerged, the Gulf of Aden turned into a lawless marketplace. Ships sailing the route either got hit for ransom money, or boarded and attacked.

At the height of the piracy boom, pirate financing schemes even popped up. Pirate networks raised capital directly from villagers: one villager contributed an AK‑47, another an RPG. They formed crews to raid shipping, heading out in small open boats to hijack massive ocean-going freighters, staring death in the face on every voyage.

That largely sums up Somalia’s place in global headlines. Beyond war, piracy and crushing poverty, the world knows almost nothing else about the country. Few people realize Somalia was not always this broken. Only half a century ago, Somalia stood as a dominant regional force in East Africa. Its fall stems from a dramatic conflict fought with its neighbor, Ethiopia.

Somalia sits on Africa’s eastern horn, at the junction of the Red Sea and Indian Ocean, covering roughly 630,000 square kilometers. Modern Somalia was not a naturally formed nation-state; for centuries, Britain, France and Italy colonized its lands in turn.

After World War II, Africa’s independence movement swept across the continent, and Somalia sought its own freedom. Since Somalis lacked experience governing a modern state, the United Nations authorized Italy to administer Somalia under trusteeship, tasked with building the basic framework of a functional country. In 1960, the ten-year trusteeship period ended, and Somalia gained formal independence.

Contrary to popular online claims of free universal healthcare and education, Somalia was desperately poor from the start. Its 1960 per capita GDP stood at merely $28. Worse than poverty was deep internal division; Somalis had little shared national identity. Clan factions formed competing political parties that bickered endlessly in parliament.

The deadlock persisted until 1969, when General Mohamed Siad Barre stepped forward. “Enough of this. No meaningful governance can happen with all of you squabbling.” He dissolved all political parties and parliament, and suppressed clan power. “I hold the presidency. Do you object?”

Siad Barre was a career military officer who attended military school in Italy, a common path for African officers of that era. Many such strongmen fell into corruption and empty rhetoric, but Barre was different. Though a military ruler, he held bold ambitions. Once in power, he rejected lavish personal indulgence. Instead, he set out to build Somalia into a genuine modern nation.

Barre believed a poor, fractured nation like Somalia required strong centralized rule. In 1970, he aligned Somalia with the Soviet Union. He seized power while Leonid Brezhnev led the USSR, during an era of global Soviet expansion. Somalia’s strategic location offered Moscow a foothold in the Horn of Africa. Brezhnev and Barre’s interests aligned perfectly.

The Cold War was a golden age for small nations. The Soviet Union readily poured aid and cash to loyal allies.

Portraits of Lenin and Brezhnev hung across Mogadishu, Somalia’s capital. Pleased with Somalia’s allegiance, Moscow signed a treaty of friendship in 1974. The Soviets supplied vast quantities of industrial goods and infrastructure, sending more than 3,000 military advisers and technical specialists, launching Somalia’s rapid development.

Beyond material and economic aid, Soviet support focused heavily on the military. The USSR delivered MiG fighter jets, T‑62 tanks, Il‑28 bombers and other advanced hardware. Backed by Soviet arms, Barre consolidated his grip on power.

He launched sweeping domestic reforms: erasing clan identities, creating a written Somali script, nationalizing foreign-owned enterprises. He mandated schooling for young Somalis, used radio and media to denounce clanism, replaced mosque authority with grassroots state organizations, and forged a stronger national identity.

Under Barre’s rule, Somalia advanced rapidly toward modern statehood. By the late 1970s, once destitute Somalia had risen into a regional East African power under Barre’s leadership and Soviet backing. Its military, bolstered by $164 million in Soviet military assistance, outmatched neighboring states in equipment and training. It stood as one of the USSR’s most celebrated Cold War success stories.

Barre deserved credit for these gains, yet Somalia’s collapse ultimately began with him. The visionary general became overambitious.

Africa’s national borders were largely drawn arbitrarily by colonial powers, ignoring ethnic and geographic realities. Somalia was a nation for ethnic Somalis, yet many ethnic Somalis lived outside its borders. The most prominent example was the Ogaden region inside Ethiopia. The land had long been home to Somali communities, populated by over half a million ethnic Somalis, yet colonial treaties placed it under Ethiopian administration.

This rankled Somalia’s leadership. They nurtured the dream of a “Greater Somalia”: using military force to reclaim ancestral Somali lands. As a desperately poor nation, this vision once seemed nothing more than fantasy. But Barre’s military buildup turned the dream into a seemingly achievable goal.

Circumstances handed him an opening. While Barre grew bolder, neighboring Ethiopia descended into crisis.

Ethiopia was one of Africa’s ancient civilizations, a major Black African state. The Ethiopian Empire traced its royal line back to King Solomon and fought for centuries against Arab and Christian powers. Thanks to its long history, Ethiopia resisted European colonial conquest; no colonial power fully occupied it.

Its most famous moment came in 1896, when it defeated Italy, forcing Italy to sign a treaty and pay reparations — the only African nation ever to make a European colonial power pay war indemnities.

Impressed by Ethiopia’s triumph, Haiti once urged Ethiopia’s emperor to become leader of all Black people worldwide. The emperor reportedly replied, “You are Black; your whole nation is Black.” Though Ethiopians are dark-skinned, their historical self-image framed them as “sun-tanned whites.” The Latin root of “Ethiopia” means “burnt face.” For this reason, Ethiopia long looked down on poorer African nations and maintained ties with Asia and Europe.

Italy finally conquered Ethiopia in 1936. Emperor Haile Selassie fled to Britain. In 1941, with Allied support, Ethiopia reclaimed independence.

Trouble began under Haile Selassie himself. The emperor earned immense respect for resisting Italy and championing African independence, making him a national hero. Yet his foreign policy skill contrasted sharply with rotten domestic governance and extravagant royal spending. In this agrarian nation, royal expenditure was four times the national agricultural budget.

Ethiopia suffered erratic rainfall and repeated famine. A devastating famine struck in 1972, killing over 200,000 people and leaving more than four million facing severe food shortages. Haile Selassie ignored the crisis. A photograph showing him feeding raw meat to lions during the famine sparked nationwide outrage. Amid public fury, the military arrested Haile Selassie in 1974, ending Ethiopia’s millennium-old monarchy.

In 1977, Mengistu Haile Mariam took charge of Ethiopia’s military junta. Lacking deep legitimacy, he launched a brutal purge. Most senior army and air force officers were executed, nearly all of Ethiopia’s fighter pilots killed.

Ethiopia had long leaned toward the United States, but Mengistu, who had endured racial discrimination in America, harbored fierce anti-American sentiment. After seizing power, he severed ties with Washington. The purge wiped out much of Ethiopia’s technical elite, and the cutoff of US aid plunged the country into chaos.

Barre watched this crisis closely. Coincidentally in 1974, a Swedish corporation announced it had discovered oil reserves in the Ogaden.

Barre calculated that although Ethiopia’s population was roughly nine times larger than Somalia’s, its internal turmoil paired with Somalia’s growing strength created a window of opportunity to challenge Ethiopia.

As early as 1975, Somalia began backing the Western Somali Liberation Front (WSLF), ethnic Somali guerrillas fighting Ethiopian troops in the Ogaden. Ethiopian government forces struggled badly, pushed back by insurgent attacks. By 1977, Ethiopian troops in the Ogaden were trapped inside major towns, stripped of mobility.

That same year, Mengistu seized power and Ethiopia descended into full disorder. The guerrillas attempted a surprise assault on Jijiga, the largest city in the Ogaden, but lost more than three hundred fighters and failed to capture the city. This failed siege exhausted Barre’s patience for proxy warfare. He stepped out from behind the scenes. If Ethiopia was this vulnerable, there was no more need for pretense.

Barre made his decision. The Ogaden War began.

Somalia’s surprise invasion resembled an African version of Operation Barbarossa. Though smaller in size, Somalia’s army was far better prepared than Ethiopia’s. Knowing the vast gap in national manpower, Barre gambled on a swift, decisive victory.

In 1977, Somalia launched a full-scale invasion, deploying twelve mechanized brigades, 250 tanks, 600 artillery pieces, backed by fighter jets, striking the Ogaden along three fronts. Somali forces held numerical and equipment superiority in the region, yet still failed to eliminate over ten thousand Ethiopian troops holding fortified outposts. Even Somalia’s elite army could not escape the brutal realities of African warfare.

After the invasion, Mengistu scrambled to reorganize, sparing officers awaiting execution. In exchange for allowing Ethiopian Jews to emigrate, he secured Israeli pilots to fly combat aircraft and contain Somalia’s air force. Still, Ethiopian ground troops could not halt the Somali advance; most of the Ogaden fell under Somali occupation.

Desperate, Mengistu took a high-risk gamble and contacted Moscow for military aid. This seemed absurd at first: Somalia had long been the USSR’s ally. Mengistu bet that the Soviet Union valued Ethiopia more. He had no guarantee Moscow would answer his plea.

Within Soviet strategy for the Horn of Africa, Ethiopia had always been the primary target. US investment there had long blocked Soviet overtures, and Somalia was merely a backup option. When Ethiopia voluntarily turned to Moscow, the Soviets readily accepted. The two sides signed sweeping cooperation agreements, and the USSR promised military support for Ethiopia.

The Soviet Union disliked Somalia’s reckless expansionism. When Somalia first invaded, Moscow sent a letter urging Barre to halt the war and withdraw his troops. Barre refused, convinced Somalia could seize the Ogaden alone.

Moscow had already grown frustrated with Barre. Ethiopia’s overture made the USSR shift support fully to Ethiopia, sending military instructors. Somalia then discovered Moscow was supporting both sides. Enraged, Barre made a bold choice to defy Brezhnev: he cut diplomatic ties with the USSR and expelled more than seven thousand Soviet advisers.

As Soviet advisors withdrew from Somalia, Moscow reassigned over a thousand of them to Ethiopia. The same instructors who had trained Somalia’s army now went to train Ethiopia’s troops. The war turned into a strange case of Soviet advisors playing both sides.

The Soviet advisors knew Somali tactics intimately, which should have made it simple to drive Somali forces out. Yet once they arrived in Ethiopia, they faced a frustrating reality: no amount of expert planning could compensate for poor troops. Ethiopia’s military was already mediocre by African standards, and Mengistu’s purges had wiped out most skilled technical officers.

After the USSR switched sides, the war fell into stalemate. Barre launched multiple offensives but failed to win a quick victory; Ethiopia also lacked strength to expel Somali troops. Mengistu pressed Moscow with a pointed question: could the Soviet superpower deliver on its promises?

This struck a raw nerve for the USSR. Following setbacks in the Middle East, Arab nations questioned Soviet reliability, and Egypt had defected from the Soviet bloc. Whenever small nations weighed alignment with Moscow, they asked whether backing the USSR was a viable path.

As one of the world’s two superpowers, the Soviet Union could absorb criticism and military setbacks, but not doubts about its credibility. Mengistu’s implied threat to turn back to the United States alarmed Moscow. Angry at Somalia’s rebellion, the USSR craved a decisive win to prove its power. At the end of 1977, Brezhnev convened a Moscow summit and prepared to demonstrate Soviet power to Ethiopia.

On November 25, 1977, Brezhnev ordered Soviet forces across the globe to launch a massive strategic airlift to Ethiopia. It ranks among the largest air logistics operations in human history, mobilizing assets across Asia, Europe and Africa: stretching from Kazakhstan and the China-Mongolia border eastward, to the Caspian Sea and Mediterranean in the west. Hundreds of transport planes loaded weapons and supplies and flew toward Ethiopia. The An‑22 and Il‑76 heavy cargo aircraft formed the backbone of the operation.

To confuse NATO, Soviet air command plotted extremely complex flight paths. Aircraft based in Asia flew south over Afghanistan and Pakistan; some crossed the Indian Ocean west toward Ethiopia, others detoured to Mozambique then headed north. European planes flew south across the Mediterranean and Arabian Peninsula, passing Libya, Niger and Chad before reaching Ethiopia.

To hide Soviet intentions from US intelligence, planes flew along seven separate routes, alternating loaded and empty runs, submitting falsified flight plans, and sometimes flying without advance notice. Soviet transports appeared everywhere, from Mongolia to Greece, Turkey and Madagascar. Western analysts assumed the whole deployment was just a massive military exercise, even after the airlift concluded.

The USSR launched a dedicated communications satellite to coordinate the operation. Between 70 and 100 transport planes departed every day. The Soviets stockpiled fuel and equipment inside Ethiopia to sustain operations. At Addis Ababa airport, a heavy transport plane landed roughly every twenty minutes. Marshal Vasily Petrov commanded the operation on the ground. Tanks and armored vehicles were flown in whole when possible. Larger hardware such as helicopters were disassembled, flown in pieces, and reassembled locally.

Midway through the airlift, one heavy transport overran the runway and disrupted operations. After consulting Moscow, Petrov initially ordered the aircraft destroyed. At the urging of mechanics, they removed its landing gear instead, and two tanks towed the plane off the airfield.

Over three weeks, the Soviet airlift delivered enough tanks, armored vehicles and rocket artillery to equip three full divisions, plus 40 MiG fighters and 150 military helicopters. It also brought 1,200 Soviet military advisers and 17,000 Cuban soldiers. Small arms arrived in uncounted quantities.

The volume of Soviet weaponry was staggering. Years after Mengistu fell from power, surplus Ethiopian tanks sold cheaper than used cars.

This airlift sealed Somalia’s fate. It also stunned the United States. The Soviet Union demonstrated it could sustain a client regime thousands of miles away in Africa whenever it chose. NATO estimated this operation used only 15 percent of the USSR’s total airlift capacity. If Moscow could deploy such strength in Africa, what could it drop on Europe by air?

Soviet intervention in Ethiopia ended US hopes of détente. In the 1970s, under the “Soviet offensive, US defensive” global dynamic, Washington once considered détente and a global power-sharing arrangement with Moscow. The overwhelming Soviet projection of force erased that idea. The US pivoted toward deep cooperation with China by the late 1970s, entering an anti-Soviet alignment.

For Somalia, these great-power calculations no longer mattered.

The USSR did not merely arm Ethiopia; it brought allied troops into combat. Cuba’s celebrated General Arnaldo Ochoa led over ten thousand Cuban soldiers against Somali forces, pushing them back steadily. Somali officers had to man machine-gun firing squads to execute deserters and hold the line.

After Cuban troops won a string of victories, Marshal Petrov ordered elements of the Soviet 76th Guards Airborne Division to parachute twenty kilometers behind Somali frontlines. After that, the Somali army collapsed completely.

Desperate, Barre begged for aid across the world. He appealed to Arab states and North Korea, which sent him a medal. Barre grumbled: “I do not want to become Somalia’s version of Brezhnev; what good is a medal?”

With no support forthcoming, he reluctantly turned to the United States, offering three Indian Ocean ports in exchange for intervention. Washington agreed to help, but set its own strict terms. Barre accepted in desperation.

Ironically, the US ultimately delivered only a small shipment of worn surplus arms, secondhand helmets and military caps. America effectively took the ports on offer for almost nothing.

At the start of the 1970s, the US backed Ethiopia and the USSR backed Somalia. By the decade’s end, the US became Somalia’s protector while Ethiopia turned into Moscow’s staunchest African ally.

The USSR had no desire to widen the war further. In April 1978, Soviet and allied forces eliminated all Somali military units operating inside Ethiopia, and restrained Ethiopian forces from pursuing retreating Somali troops. The war killed 150,000 people across both nations and caused $55 billion in economic damage, ending with borders restored to their pre-war status.

As for the supposed Ogaden oil discovery: to this day, not a single barrel of oil has been extracted there. The Ogaden remains one of the poorest regions on Earth.

The Ogaden War is little-known in modern history, yet its ripple effects changed global affairs profoundly.

For Somalia, defeat shattered the credibility of Barre’s regime, wiping out all progress it had built. Huge stockpiles of war weapons flooded civilian hands. Clan leaders released under US pressure formed anti-government militias, and Somalia descended into full civil war. The familiar sequence followed: civil war, famine and epidemic disease.

By 1991, Somalia slipped into stateless collapse. The government lost control over most territory, even its own capital. In September 1992, Audrey Hepburn, working as a UNICEF goodwill ambassador, traveled to Somalia to visit children devastated by famine. A photograph captured Hepburn holding a skeletal Somali child, looking toward the camera as if asking whether anyone would help. Even in her later years, Hepburn retained immense global influence. Once published worldwide, the photo sparked an outpouring of public concern.

In December 1992, the United Nations, led by the United States, passed a resolution launching Operation Restore Hope. US troops deployed to Somalia to protect humanitarian food deliveries. This stands as one of the rare American military interventions driven by humanitarian ideals rather than material interest. The USSR had collapsed, and Somalia lost much of its strategic value to the US. Still, America retained some idealism and sought to prove it could uphold global order. Troops deployed to capture warlord Mohamed Farrah Aidid, who disrupted UN relief work.

This leads to the events depicted in *Black Hawk Down*. Between October 3 and 4, 1993, roughly 120 US soldiers fought an intense day-and-night battle against local militias. They withdrew after suffering 19 killed and over 70 wounded. On October 4, photographs showing Somalis dragging the bodies of fallen US troops through the streets dominated front pages across the globe. President Bill Clinton faced fierce domestic backlash. The US decided to withdraw, abandoning this costly, unrewarding mission.

One year later, the Rwandan genocide erupted. Nearly a million people were killed in eighty days. The world called for UN troops to intervene, but the bitter lesson of Mogadishu made the United States refuse deployment. Other nations stood aside, watching the slaughter unfold. In 1995, the final UN peacekeepers pulled out of Mogadishu. The UN humanitarian mission in Somalia failed. Somalia remains unstable and among the world’s poorest countries today, with per capita GDP barely more than half that of Haiti.

Mengistu’s rule over Ethiopia also collapsed after the Ogaden War. He ruled through brutal repression, and disastrous economic policies worsened ethnic tensions and poverty. Famine and civil war defined Ethiopia’s years under his leadership. In 1991, the same year Barre’s regime fell, rebel forces captured Addis Ababa. Mengistu fled with loyalists to Zimbabwe.

In 1989, decorated Cuban general Arnaldo Ochoa was executed. In 1991, all three principal figures of the Ogaden War met their ends.

Who bore blame for this brutal conflict? Barre sought to forge a unified national identity and reclaim land historically inhabited by his ethnic kin; his goals were not inherently reckless. Mengistu fought to defend his country, and that too was understandable. The Soviet Union and United States threw vast resources into the conflict, though their gains never matched their investment. Both superpowers acted out of geopolitical self-interest, yet they did not deliberately ignite the war. They supplied aid that, objectively, brought both nations into contact with modern institutions. Everyone held some fault, yet no single side was entirely guilty. Responsibility remains hard to untangle.

In 1984, catastrophic famine struck Ethiopia again. Civil war and drought left two-thirds of the population facing severe food shortages, ultimately killing more than a million people. Journalists photographed the starving population and described Ethiopia as the closest place on Earth to hell. Mengistu appealed to the world: Europe enjoyed record harvests, so why could it not send aid?

In 1984, more than a dozen British pop stars recorded *Do They Know It’s Christmas?*, raising £8 million for famine victims. The next year, they staged the historic Live Aid concert, watched by 1.5 billion viewers. The British musicians’ action inspired US artists. Michael Jackson and Lionel Richie spent four days writing a song.

On January 28, 1985, Michael Jackson assembled 45 leading American singers to record the track. The song raised $60 million for African famine relief. Its title: *We Are the World*.
