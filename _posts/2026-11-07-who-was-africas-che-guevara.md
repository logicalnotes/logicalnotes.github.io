After Sangoulé Lamizana — the leader who’d tightened the nation’s belt and buried it in debt — was overthrown, a new strongman, Saye Zerbo, stepped into power.

For Lamizana, it was something of a relief. Zerbo’s first move was to import massive quantities of grain, then resell it at inflated prices to people in drought-stricken areas. Most families in those regions had almost no money left, but to survive, they had to sell everything they owned. Under Zerbo’s rule, more than a million people fled their homes in Upper Volta. Next to Lamizana, who’d at least held things together, Zerbo didn’t just take a step backward — he threw the country into outright darkness.

That said, it wouldn’t be fair to claim Zerbo did nothing of consequence. As a military ruler, he packed his government with army officers. Among those he promoted were two men who would shape the fate of Upper Volta for decades to come.

Thomas Sankara was born in August 1949 to a military family in Upper Volta. His father, an African veteran of the French army, had spent the entirety of World War II in a German POW camp. When he returned home after the war, his service to France and solid reputation earned him a post as a colonial official. So even without any extraordinary luck, Sankara was on track for a privileged life: he enrolled in a Catholic school, groomed to join the country’s elite.

But destiny had other plans. After finishing secondary school, he turned away from the path of the ruling class and enrolled at the Prytanée Militaire de Kadiogo. There, he met a professor named Touré, a left-wing intellectual who taught his students about the Soviet and Chinese revolutions, explained communist theory, and dissected the nature of imperialism and neocolonialism. It was Sankara’s first real political awakening.

In 1970, fresh out of military academy, Sankara was sent abroad for further training. Of course, with a national treasury so bare that even thieves would have left a donation out of pity, study in Britain or France was completely out of the question. His destination was a more modest one: Madagascar.

The military academy there was even stricter than the one back home. It taught not just combat skills, but farming too. Sankara picked up a solid base of agricultural knowledge during his time there. He also witnessed two popular uprisings, saw firsthand the power ordinary people could wield, and began reading the works of Marx and Lenin.

When he returned home in 1972, Sankara took the most decisive step of his life: he fully embraced communism.

Back in Upper Volta, he began training the country’s paratroopers in the model of the Soviet Red Army. He gave every soldier political education, telling them: “A soldier without patriotism is nothing more than a potential criminal.”

Under his leadership, Upper Volta built a formidable paratrooper corps, which won a decisive victory in a 1974 border conflict with Mali. It was common knowledge that being a paratrooper in 20th-century Africa was as promising a career as being a painter in Europe — it all but guaranteed a path to power.

The battle against Mali made Sankara a national hero. The public soon discovered he was far more than just a soldier: he organized concerts, promoted sports, and seemed to excel at everything he touched. What won people over most, though, was that despite his rising status, he never put on airs. He rode a bicycle to work every day, no different from any ordinary citizen.

His popularity exploded; he became a national sensation, drawing adoration comparable to that of a global pop star.

Fresh off his military fame, Sankara was sent to Morocco for advanced training. It was there he met the most important person in his life: Blaise Compaoré.

For Sankara, Compaoré was like looking in a mirror. Both came from military families; both had fathers who’d served in the French army and spent time in German POW camps before returning home as colonial officials; both ended up joining the military themselves.

Compaoré’s path to the army was a little unusual, though. He’d wanted to be a soldier since childhood, but his father, scarred by the war, refused to let him enlist. Instead, he pushed Compaoré to pursue an education, sending him to teacher’s college. But Compaoré had no interest in following that path — he organized student protests and marches while still in school.

Under Lamizana’s rule, the government didn’t want to crack down directly on student movements. Instead, they came up with a more devious solution: if you can’t beat them, conscript them. The army rounded up student activists and forced them into military service. Compaoré was among those drafted. Thanks to his higher education, he was assigned to the presidential guard.

In 1976, he too was sent to Morocco for training — and ran into Sankara.

Sankara was two years older. They were close in age, shared a love of music and sports, were both career soldiers, and most importantly, both believed in Marxism-Leninism. They admired each other immediately and became inseparable. Sankara’s own father, who often found fault with his son, adored Compaoré and invited him over for meals constantly. He would tell people Compaoré was like a second son to him.

In Morocco, Sankara didn’t just befriend Compaoré — he met a whole circle of like-minded young officers. Together, they formed a secret group: the Communist Officers’ Corps. In a small country like Upper Volta, it was only a matter of time before this group shook the nation to its core.

In 1981, Sankara left active military service and was appointed Minister of Information by Zerbo. His old unit was handed over to Compaoré. Zerbo had brought military officers into government to shore up his own power — but he soon realized he’d let a wolf right through the front door.

No sooner had Sankara taken office than he gave up his official government car and kept riding his bicycle to work. He also abolished press censorship and encouraged investigative journalism. Since Zerbo’s government had little to show for itself beyond mismanagement, newspapers quickly filled up with scandal after scandal. With Sankara’s push, local television aired its first live broadcasts, laying bare every bit of corruption in the Zerbo administration.

Zerbo was stunned. He’d promoted Sankara to help him run the country — not to tear his government apart.

In April 1982, Zerbo found an excuse to throw Sankara in prison. A few months later, he exiled him to the country’s remote northwestern border. The message was clear: keep your mouth shut in the desert, or get shot.

What Zerbo failed to understand was that you don’t exile a man as popular and influential as Sankara and expect to get away with it.

To free Sankara from exile, Compaoré rallied the paratroopers under his command in November 1982. They planned to overthrow Zerbo, march on the capital, and seize the presidency.

Under Compaoré’s direction, the paratroopers disguised themselves as football players, stashed submachine guns in travel bags, and slipped quietly into Ouagadougou, scattering across the city to wait for the signal.

At 10 a.m. on November 7, paratroopers across the capital struck simultaneously, seizing radio stations, gendarmerie headquarters, and all other key sites. The coup met almost no resistance. Government officials, seasoned from years of coups past, surrendered without a fuss — so smoothly it was almost sad to watch.

Compaoré was surprised by how easy it had all been. But within hours, he realized a critical problem: they couldn’t find President Zerbo. If Zerbo had escaped, the entire coup would be a failure.

Under his orders, paratroopers searched the entire city, and finally got a tip on Zerbo’s whereabouts. The president hadn’t run — he’d been captured by someone else.

As it turned out, November 7 was apparently a very auspicious day for coups in Africa. At 6 a.m. that same morning, army chief of staff Gabriel Somé Yorian had launched his own coup against Zerbo. A conservative with limited forces at his disposal, Somé had gone straight for the head: he’d broken into Zerbo’s home and seized the president before anyone knew what was happening.

Two separate coups, targeting the same man, in the same city, on the same day. The odds of that were about as slim as dropping a marble from a MiG-25 and landing it perfectly in a hamster’s nostril. But against all logic, it actually happened.

Faced with this absurd situation, the two coup factions sat down to negotiate. The core question was simple: who would be the next president?

Compaoré and Sankara represented the left-wing revolutionary faction; Somé, who’d captured the president, represented the conservative elite. Neither side would back down. In the end, they settled on a candidate both sides could tolerate: Jean-Baptiste Ouédraogo.

Years later, Ouédraogo would denounce the decision in his memoirs — he never wanted the job in the first place. He had no real political agenda; he was an army doctor, a highly regarded pediatrician. Why a pediatrician ended up president? Well, that’s just the bizarre political logic of small African states.

Ouédraogo had no popular mandate and no power base of his own. He was purely a figurehead pushed forward by competing factions.

After he took office, Sankara was named prime minister. The charismatic Sankara quickly overshadowed him entirely, which left Ouédraogo feeling humiliated. Add to that constant goading from conservative politicians and Guy Penne, an advisor to the French president, and Ouédraogo made a reckless decision: he ordered Sankara’s arrest.

That was a huge mistake. What followed was a repeat of a now-familiar story.

At 6 p.m. on August 4, 1983, Compaoré led his troops toward Ouagadougou, ready to “reason with” the president. By 10 p.m., the paratroopers — now old hands at this — had seized the capital again. Ouédraogo resigned. Sankara and Compaoré were now the undisputed leaders of the country.

In Africa, coups were so common they outnumbered the air in a bag of potato chips. The international community barely paid attention to the 1983 putsch. When asked who’d won, foreign correspondents on the ground would just shrug and say, “Whoever came out on top, I guess.”

No one realized at the time that August 4, 1983, wasn’t just another coup. It was a revolution.

On August 5, Sankara addressed the nation and declared a national holiday. Crowds poured into the streets to celebrate him. Over the next few days, he ordered senior civil servants to keep government running while members of the officers’ corps fanned out across the country to take command of the military.

On August 24, Sankara formed a new cabinet. With an average age under 35, it was an extraordinarily young leadership team.

Immediately upon taking office, he laid out his agenda: pursue a communist path, solve the country’s livelihood crises, and reset relations with France.

Most new governments hold lavish ceremonies to celebrate a new era. Sankara’s first act in office? Selling the presidential limousine.

He’d always ridden a bike to work, but as president, that was no longer safe. Even so, he insisted that all the Mercedes-Benzes assigned to senior officials be sold off and replaced with the cheapest Renault models available locally. He also had the air conditioner removed from his office, arguing that such luxuries had no place in a country crippled by power shortages.

He went further. He cut his own salary from the official $2,000 a month down to $450 — less than his own ministers earned. On that tiny income, he supported his elderly parents, his wife, and 15 children — only two of whom were his biological offspring; the other 13 were orphaned children he’d adopted.

His household was so poor that he often ate at Compaoré’s house.

By tradition, a new president’s portrait would hang in public buildings across the country. Sankara flatly rejected the practice. “I am one of the people,” he said. “There are seven million Sankaras in this country.”

Even as president, he almost never changed his clothes. He wore military fatigues and a beret almost every day, a constant reminder to stay true to the revolutionary cause.

In October 1983, with his power fully consolidated, Sankara began rolling out his policies. First on the list: solving the food crisis.

Burkina Faso’s agricultural conditions were brutal — cycles of crippling drought and devastating floods. It seemed like an impossible problem, but Sankara identified the root cause.

Under French colonial planning, most of Upper Volta’s farmland had been turned over to cotton production. Cotton fetched high prices, so wealthy landowners loved it. But you can’t eat cotton. Previous governments had tried to encourage farmers to switch to food crops, but landowners refused — famines never hurt them, after all.

Sankara’s solution was land reform. He redistributed land directly to peasant farmers, raised the government purchase price for grain, and encouraged people to grow wheat, corn, and sorghum. He also pushed for wider use of fertilizer to boost yields. Most notably, he was the first leader to propose the concept of a “Great Green Wall” — a massive tree-planting campaign across the southern edge of the Sahara to halt desertification.

In just two years, grain production surged. Upper Volta achieved near self-sufficiency in food, and for the first time, famine was no longer a constant threat.

In healthcare, Sankara had a famous line: “Why should an official’s child get flown to Paris for a cold, while peasant families can’t even afford the most basic medicine?”

Despite severe budget constraints, his government vaccinated 2.5 million children against common diseases, cutting infant mortality by 6 percentage points.

He also believed education was the foundation of all progress. He launched a nationwide literacy campaign, slashed school fees, and pushed primary school enrollment from 6% up to 18%. That number was still low by global standards, but for Upper Volta, it was an unprecedented achievement.

Beyond healthcare and education, what made Sankara truly remarkable was that he was among the first leaders in West Africa to advocate for women’s liberation.

Unlike authoritarian leaders elsewhere who exploited harmful traditions for power, Sankara banned the brutal practice of female genital mutilation. His government outlawed forced marriage and polygamy, and encouraged women to pursue education and join the workforce. He led by example, calling on husbands across the country to go to the market and cook a meal to understand what their wives went through every day. His cabinet included West Africa’s first female government minister.

Speaking on women’s rights, he said: “Revolution and women’s liberation are one and the same. Women hold up half the sky.”

On his orders, Upper Volta established people’s tribunals, where ordinary citizens could participate in oversight and help try corrupt officials. He refused to grant the tribunals the power to impose the death penalty, to avoid irreversible miscarriages of justice.

Under his leadership, Upper Volta came alive like never before. For the first time since independence more than two decades earlier, the country felt like it was actually developing.

In 1984, Sankara decided to rename the country. He discarded “Upper Volta” — a purely geographic label imposed by the French — and renamed it **Burkina Faso**, meaning “Land of the Upright People”. He designed the national coat of arms himself and wrote the national anthem, *Anthem of Victory* (Le Ditanyè).

In February 1987, Sankara pushed through a mandatory asset declaration system for all public officials. His own filing revealed his entire net worth: a modest home bought on loan, a 9-year-old Nissan car, two bicycles, two television sets (one kept in his office), and two refrigerators (one broken and used only for storage).

That was everything the president of Burkina Faso owned.

Sankara was a leader of grand ambition. He wanted a nationwide road network, 350 community schools, a clinic in every village. He wanted so much — but he never got to build most of it.

February 1987 marked his fourth year in power. He had only eight months left to live.

If Sankara was a blessing for Burkina Faso, he was a nightmare for France.

He was a staunch anti-imperialist and anti-colonial fighter, earning him the nickname “Africa’s Che Guevara”. For France, that made him completely unacceptable.

When Sankara first took office, he didn’t seek out conflict with France. But France was openly hostile to his communist government. At his first state banquet, France deliberately sent Guy Penne — the same advisor who’d pushed for Sankara’s arrest — to attend, a deliberate and public insult to the new president.

Sankara also tried to build good relations with neighboring countries, but most of them were under French influence. Shortly after taking office, he made a diplomatic visit to Côte d’Ivoire — only to be denied entry at France’s direction. It was an insult not just to Sankara, but to the entire nation of Burkina Faso.

The French assumed Sankara would just take it. After all, Burkina Faso was far too weak to stand up to France.

But they didn’t know Sankara. He was never the type to swallow humiliation.

To Sankara, France’s posturing all came down to one thing: they were afraid Burkina Faso would default on its debts, ruining their system of economic colonialism. Well, he wasn’t just going to default — he was going to call on every African nation to do the same.

Sankara repeatedly used international platforms to call on African countries to repudiate their debts to imperialist powers. “The poor and the exploited have no obligation to repay the rich and the exploiters,” he said.

His words sent shockwaves across the continent. Governments across Africa began talking about debt default, and France seethed.

You might wonder: if he openly called for default, would no one ever lend him money again?

That’s exactly right. He refused to borrow, on principle.

“Whoever feeds you controls you,” he argued. He kept his distance from both the US and France, and even from the Soviet Union, despite their shared ideology. By the time he took office, Brezhnev was dead, and Soviet subsidies were off the table anyway. More than that, Sankara rejected Soviet-style hegemony and interference.

Refusing foreign loans was a point of pride, a stand for national sovereignty and dignity. But reality doesn’t bend to idealism. In this world, not every country is allowed to have dignity.

The price of rejecting foreign debt was that Sankara could only fund his development plans by slashing domestic spending.

Under his orders, official perks were cut again and again. Salaries were reduced, and officials were regularly pressured to donate to public projects. But civil servants in such a poor country didn’t earn much to begin with. Sankara expanded the austerity measures to police officers and teachers as well.

That triggered a fierce backlash. The poor celebrated his policies, but officials and the middle class resented him deeply.

Compaoré, far more pragmatic than Sankara, tried to talk him down. You can squeeze officials, he argued, but don’t cut teachers’ and police pay. If you keep this up, all the educated people will leave the country. And yes, helping the poor is good — but some people in this country want cars, air conditioning, a better standard of living. You can’t take that all away.

Sankara poured almost all foreign currency into national development. Consumer goods became almost impossible to find, and the country’s elite grew furious.

Sankara saw it differently. Where else would the money for schools and vaccines come from? Luxuries could wait, he believed. Sacrificing a little comfort was worth it to build the country.

Both men had a point. Neither could convince the other.

Since Sankara took power, Compaoré had been the second-most powerful man in the regime, serving as both Minister of Internal Security and commander of the capital’s military district. He’d earned the position as a hero of the revolution. But their disagreements went far beyond domestic policy — they clashed even more sharply on foreign affairs.

Compaoré argued that no matter how unreasonable France was, a small poor country had no business picking fights with it. France was powerful. Prudence meant holding your tongue.

Sankara, fiery and uncompromising, didn’t just oppose France — he openly criticized neighboring French-backed states, calling them reactionary regimes. For his entire time in office, Burkina Faso had terrible relations with its neighbors, especially Côte d’Ivoire and Mali. Tensions ran so high they often seemed on the brink of war.

To Compaoré, even if you didn’t want to be a pushover, you didn’t go out of your way to antagonize every country around you. Constantly teetering on the edge of conflict — wasn’t that just being reckless, like Gaddafi?

These rifts over domestic and foreign policy drove the two brothers further apart. And in 1985, the divide deepened.

That year, Compaoré married Chantal Terrasson de Fougères, granddaughter of a French colonial governor in Mali.

If Compaoré’s disagreements with Sankara were over policy, Chantal’s dislike of Sankara was personal and visceral.

At one state banquet, Chantal asked for a glass of champagne — something Sankara had banned from official events. To be polite, Sankara personally tracked down a glass and handed it to her.

Chantal didn’t thank him. Instead, she complained loudly to people around her: “The hotel has champagne, he just won’t let anyone drink it. If he’s going to ban it, why let the hotel import it at all? He’s just faking being a revolutionary.”

After that, Sankara never went over to Compaoré’s house for meals again. They saw each other less and less. Their once unbreakable friendship was fraying.

In 1986, French president François Mitterrand visited Africa and made a stop in Burkina Faso. At the welcome banquet, instead of giving a standard welcoming speech, Sankara spoke with unflinching frankness. He accused France of poisoning its relations with former colonies, of exploiting them without mercy, and of using so-called “aid” to trap African countries in cycles of debt, borrowing new money just to pay off old loans.

Mitterrand, a seasoned politician, shot back with sarcastic remarks of his own. Neither backed down. They traded barbs all night, and by the end, it was a draw.

After that dinner, relations between the two countries collapsed completely. From that point on, Mitterrand’s view of Sankara was simple: this man had to be removed.

In February 1987, right as Sankara rolled out the asset declaration rules, two senior officials were caught embezzling large sums of money. What hurt Sankara most was that both men were old comrades from the Communist Officers’ Corps.

Sankara fired both of them and scheduled public trials to make an example. When Compaoré heard the news, he begged Sankara to reconsider — to spare the old comrades the humiliation of a public trial, for old times’ sake.

The argument turned explosive. Compaoré accused Sankara of being stubborn and refusing to listen to anyone. Sankara accused Compaoré of siding with corrupt officials and sabotaging his work. They parted on terrible terms.

In the end, Sankara backed down. The two men never faced public trial. According to his secretary, Sankara had never looked so defeated. After Compaoré left, he sat silently at his desk for hours.

In June 1987, to address the country’s shifting political landscape, Sankara proposed forming a new political party to unify the country’s progressive forces. To his shock, the strongest opposition came from his oldest comrades — Compaoré and another veteran of the group, Jean-Baptiste Boukary Lingani.

They rejected the proposal outright, accusing Sankara of acting unilaterally and shutting everyone else out. Betrayed by his own friends, Sankara grew so heated during the meeting that he threatened to resign.

By this point, the rift between Sankara and Compaoré was public knowledge. The infighting at the top of Burkina Faso’s government was no secret.

Sensing an opening, France sent a new ambassador in August that year: Alain Deschamps. A veteran diplomat with a reputation — wherever he was posted, a coup seemed to follow. He’d never missed.

Soon, pamphlets attacking Sankara began appearing in cities across Burkina Faso. Sankara was furious. At a senior leadership meeting, he said: “The enemy is sitting right here in the front row. There are traitors in this room.”

Compaoré exploded. “If you have proof, show it. Don’t throw around vague accusations.” The two men shouted at each other in front of everyone.

After that, posters began appearing on the streets: “Whoever opposes Sankara opposes the revolution.” Sankara always claimed he had nothing to do with them, but Compaoré didn’t believe him.

On October 8, 1987 — the 20th anniversary of Che Guevara’s death — Sankara called his old comrades together to try to talk things through and mend fences. But Compaoré and the others sat in silence the whole time. The meeting ended in failure, just like all the others.

By then, the conflict was beyond repair. Sankara’s security detail drew up a plan to arrest Compaoré and other senior officials first, before they could strike. Sankara refused to approve it.

But that unsigned, rejected coup memo soon ended up on Compaoré’s desk. Rumors swept Ouagadougou that a coup was imminent.

There was a force pushing both men’s anger higher and higher, turning every disagreement into a crisis.

And that force was France. Declassified French documents from 2015 confirmed it: all the street posters, all the rumors — they were all planted by the French embassy. The rejected coup plan? Leaked to Compaoré by French intelligence.

To Compaoré, it looked like his life was on the line. To protect himself, he decided to launch a coup immediately.

October 15, 1987, started like any other day. Sankara left home in athletic clothes. He had a meeting that afternoon, and planned to jog home afterward. He didn’t even bring a bodyguard.

Deep down, he still couldn’t believe his oldest friend would ever hurt him.

The meeting had barely begun when gunfire erupted outside the window. “What now?” Sankara said, standing up. Then a voice shouted from the doorway: “Everyone inside — come out with your hands up!”

Sankara understood immediately. He turned to the other advisors in the room: “Stay here. They’re here for me.”

He stepped through the door and was cut down in a hail of bullets. The coup troops stormed the room. All 12 people at the meeting were shot. Only one advisor survived by playing dead — and he’s the one who told the world what happened that day.

After Sankara’s death, coup forces rounded up his supporters across the city. Loyal troops fought back, but were killed to the last man.

When Compaoré took power, he rolled back almost all of Sankara’s policies. He restored relations with France, reinstated full salaries for civil servants and teachers, and reversed Sankara’s nationalization programs in favor of privatization.

Two years later, he had his two other old comrades — Lingani and Henri Zongo — executed. By then, it came easy. He’d had practice.

And so, 13 years after the Communist Officers’ Corps was founded in Morocco, it met its end: brothers-in-arms turned on each other, until almost none remained.

In 1991, months before the Soviet Union collapsed, Compaoré formally abandoned Marxism and returned the country to capitalism. He rejoined the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and became a prominent figure in African diplomacy.

Unlike Sankara, who’d been isolated internationally, Compaoré was well-liked abroad. He built lavish mansions in his hometown and in Côte d’Ivoire. Being president suited him far better than it had Sankara. Everyone was happy with him — everyone except the poor, whom he never had to see.

“Stability” was the word people used to describe his rule. But after twenty-some years of that “stability”, Burkina Faso slipped back into being one of the world’s poorest, most heavily indebted countries. Famine became a fact of life again.

Today, the average person in Burkina Faso lives on less than two dollars a day. Half the population lives below the international poverty line. One and a half million children suffer from malnutrition. A country of 20 million people has only eight hospitals. There is one doctor for every 30,000 people. Life expectancy hovers in the fifties. The child mortality rate is 15 percent.

Female genital mutilation and polygamy — practices Sankara had banned — came back. Only one statistic stands out: Burkina Faso now has the second-highest illiteracy rate in the world, at 78 percent.

But no one really talks about Burkina Faso anymore. After Sankara died, this tiny, forgotten country faded back into obscurity. It only makes the news when terrorist groups massacre villagers there. Nothing more.

In 2014, after 27 years in power, Compaoré was ousted by popular protests and fled into exile in Côte d’Ivoire — just like Sankara’s family had before him. The difference was night and day: Compaoré left with mansions and bank accounts, no hardship ahead.

On March 2, 2019, a statue of Thomas Sankara was unveiled in Ouagadougou, the capital of Burkina Faso. It was the last legacy of the man they called “Africa’s Che Guevara”.

Beyond that statue, almost nothing remains of what Sankara built. The statue stands as the last flicker of hope in a desperately poor country. No one knows if there will ever be another leader like him. He might come tomorrow — or he might never come at all.
