When this question comes up, many people’s first‑thought reaction is that it sounds reasonable. The rich hold massive fortunes they could never spend in multiple lifetimes, while many in society struggle just to cover basic needs. Why shouldn’t wealthy people give some of their money to help those less well‑off?

Yet this discussion is not about voluntary kindness or charity. It is about legal obligation. The question is not whether the rich should generously choose to assist the poor out of compassion. It asks whether the law ought to mandate that for every ten dollars a rich person earns, three or five must go toward supporting poorer members of society as a legal duty.

We will set aside cases where wealth comes from corrupt or illegal sources. Those scenarios make the debate far too simple. Our working assumption is that all wealth held by the rich is legally earned under commonly‑agreed‑upon social rules. The poor have simply not succeeded within this system. Under these conditions, are the rich morally required to hand over part of what they have built through hard work and talent? Is such an arrangement just? This is a core political‑philosophy question about distributive justice.

We want a society that supports vulnerable people, yet we also expect individuals to take responsibility for their own material circumstances. Three major philosophers offer competing answers to this dilemma: John Rawls, champion of egalitarian liberalism; Robert Nozick, libertarian thinker; and G.A. Cohen, defender of luck egalitarianism.

Let us begin with Rawls. From his egalitarian‑liberal perspective, Rawls supports taxing the wealthy to benefit the poor.

Critics sometimes claim Rawls simply takes sides with the poor. That misreads his project entirely. Rawls’s political philosophy aims to examine distributive justice from an impartial standpoint.

Ordinary public debates about taxing the rich often collapse into partisan positioning: which group is someone speaking for, the rich or the poor? Our views on justice are frequently shaped by our own lived circumstances. Wealthy people tend to favour lower taxes; poorer people tend to support higher taxes on the rich. If argument reduces to competing power and volume of voice, rational reasoning falls apart.

Rawls seeks to strip away personal bias and arrive at principles any reasonable person would accept, whether rich or poor.

His famous mental tool for achieving impartiality is the **veil of ignorance**. Imagine a curtain that blocks all knowledge of your real‑world position. It works much like a card game where cards stay face‑down; you cannot know what hand you will draw.

Behind this veil of ignorance, you do not know if you will end up rich or poor, male or female, or which racial group you belong to. You have no advance knowledge of your intelligence, physical appearance or personal preferences. Without knowing your own identity, you cannot favour or discriminate against any group. Rawls calls this hypothetical condition the **original position**.

People in the original position are freed from self‑interested bias and make choices purely through reason. What kind of distributive system would they design?

Given guaranteed basic liberties and fair equality of opportunity, rational actors would reject a system of extreme inequality. When the veil lifts, any person could turn out to be the worst‑off member of society. Designers behind the veil would therefore lean toward a more equal arrangement.

This does not mean they would demand absolute equality. Perfect equality removes incentives for talented people to work productively, which harms overall social well‑being. Some inequality can be accepted — but only under one strict condition: inequalities must work to improve the position of the least advantaged people in society. In other words, gaps between rich and poor may exist only if they benefit those who fare worst.

Some might object: “I am a risk‑taker. Even behind the veil, I would gamble. If I end up destitute, I accept my fate; if I win big, I thrive.”

Rawls counters that this is nothing like an ordinary gamble. Normal bets can be repeated; you get another chance to win later. The choice made in the original position happens once and is permanent. If you draw the worst possible life, there is no second chance. It is like staking your entire present and future livelihood on a single roll. Even risk‑seeking people would avoid a setup where losing means total ruin. They would want security: if they turn out worst‑off, their situation should still be bearable.

That is Rawls’s case for redistributive taxation. No reasonable person would agree to live in a society that abandons its most vulnerable, because anyone could become that vulnerable person. For Rawls, justice carries an inherent commitment to equality. Within the bounds of basic freedoms, inequality must stay within reasonable limits.

Next comes Robert Nozick and his libertarian position. Nozick firmly opposes using taxation to redistribute wealth from rich to poor. He makes a provocative claim: forced redistributive taxation amounts to theft or robbery.

No institution may compel wealthy citizens to fund the poor through taxes. A well‑meaning thief still has no right to steal from the rich and give to homeless people.

For Nozick, we cannot judge justice only by looking at final wealth outcomes. It is not enough to observe huge gaps between billionaires and destitute people, then demand redistribution. Unequal results do not automatically justify unjust processes. The critical question is: how was this property originally acquired?

Nozick sets two standards for legitimate ownership. Property is justly held if someone appropriates previously unowned resources, or if goods are transferred through fully voluntary exchange. If these two rules are followed, owners hold an inviolable entitlement to their property; no authority may take it away.

If no one gained wealth through wrongdoing, any distribution resulting from free‑market exchange remains just — no matter how unequal final outcomes become.

Consider a professional basketball star who earns an enormous salary because huge crowds voluntarily pay to watch him play. He may donate money to the poor out of charity, but he bears no legal obligation to give away his earnings. His wealth comes from voluntary transactions with the public and should not be seized by force.

Nozick’s bold argument rests on the principle of **self‑ownership**: each person owns their own body, abilities and whatever they produce using those abilities. No individual or state may rightfully take these away.

Income taxation, in Nozick’s view, seizes part of the fruits of your labour. Suppose you work forty hours a week and earn $1000. A 25‑percent redistributive tax takes $250, leaving you with $750. You could earn that $750 in thirty hours of work. Effectively, the state compels you to work ten hours for nothing. That is forced labour, which Nozick equates to slavery. If we reject slavery, we ought to reject redistributive taxation that treats people as tools for others’ benefit.

Nozick does not condemn voluntary mutual aid between individuals. He also does not celebrate inequality; great wealth gaps are unfortunate. But he rejects reshaping society purely in pursuit of equal outcomes, which he believes undermines genuine justice.

We now turn to G.A. Cohen. The Canadian philosopher advocated egalitarianism and supported taxing the wealthy to help the poor. Yet his thinking underwent a major intellectual crisis after reading Nozick’s *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. Cohen rejected Nozick’s conclusions justifying capitalist inequality, yet found his internal logic difficult to refute — especially the self‑ownership thesis, which also underpins Marxist exploitation theory.

Marx argued exploitation is unjust because capitalists seize surplus value created by workers’ labour. This critique shares its foundational premise with Nozick: people own their own labour power.

Cohen faced a sharp dilemma. If he accepted self‑ownership, much of Nozick’s reasoning followed, meaning inequalities stemming from differences in natural talent could be legitimate, and egalitarianism would collapse.

He spent twenty years wrestling with this problem. His options were limited: abandon egalitarianism and accept inequality, or discard the self‑ownership premise. At first he tried to keep self‑ownership while reconciling it with equality. Eventually he conceded that the logical pull toward inequality was too strong, and gave up self‑ownership to defend his egalitarian convictions.

Cohen drew inspiration from thinkers like Rawls. Behind the veil of ignorance, many advantages people enjoy turn out to be arbitrary products of luck. Natural talent is not earned through choice or effort. Innate strengths and weaknesses are simply assigned at birth. Why should all the benefits of unchosen good fortune belong exclusively to the lucky individual? Can a system built largely on random luck ever be just?

Cohen formulated **luck egalitarianism**: a distribution is unjust whenever someone ends up worse‑off than others through no fault or choice of their own. Justice requires us to offset these arbitrary strokes of fortune.

Take two basketball players as an illustration. If a superstar’s massive salary comes purely from hard work, that pay is fully justified. Imagine another player, Jack. Jack loves basketball just as much and trains even harder, yet earns far less. His disadvantage may stem simply from innate height, something he could never choose or change. A large share of the star’s high income flows from lucky natural endowments. Justice demands that part of this luck‑generated gain go toward compensating people who drew a bad hand.

Luck egalitarianism builds on left‑liberal thought but pushes equality much further. Mainstream left‑liberalism mostly fixes social disadvantages, leaving natural inborn differences untouched. Cohen insists society must also mitigate inequalities rooted in innate gifts. Good luck and bad luck that fall on people without their choice ought to be shared collectively across the whole community. This intervention goes considerably further than standard left‑liberal proposals.
