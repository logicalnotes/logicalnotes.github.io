This is a remarkable story.

Albania, a country familiar to older generations in the world, has quietly faded from public awareness. Few young people today even know it exists. When news about Albania surfaces now, it is rarely positive, often tied to theft, robbery and human trafficking. What happened during these decades, after the country was once hailed as the “beacon of Europe”? Albania lived through a surreal, winding chapter of its own.

Located in the southwestern Balkans, Albania barely registers in ancient European history. Its people revere their national hero Skanderbeg and call their homeland the “Land of the Eagles”. It is a tiny nation: roughly 28,000 square kilometres in area, with fewer than three million residents.

Yet this small state once punched far above its weight on the global stage. Much of that fame stemmed from one towering, deeply divisive 20th-century figure: Enver Hoxha, First Secretary of the Party of Labour of Albania, who ruled for nearly half a century. For most of the 1900s, any mention of Albania almost inevitably led back to him.

Born into a merchant family in southern Albania, Hoxha studied in France in his youth. During World War Two, he led Albania’s anti-fascist guerrillas and became the country’s leader after the war. From 1945 until his death in 1985, Hoxha held power for four decades, a figure of immense controversy.

Under Hoxha’s rule, Albania abolished outdated traditional religious customs and evolved into a secular state. Industry, agriculture and healthcare all saw notable progress. Measured against its own past, his domestic governance brought real improvements. But solid domestic administration alone would never have earned him such global notoriety. Hoxha became infamous because Albania’s foreign policy under him was wildly unconventional. Some historians describe Hoxha as an idiosyncratic internationalist, shaping diplomacy by personal conviction rather than national interest.

After Albania emerged as an independent socialist state, Hoxha admired Stalin and aligned closely with the Soviet Union, severing diplomatic ties with Britain and the United States. But this was only the start of Albania’s dramatic diplomatic shifts.

In 1948, relations between Yugoslavia and the USSR broke down. Despite earlier substantial Yugoslav support for Albania, Hoxha turned sharply against Yugoslav leader Tito, siding fully with Moscow and splitting with Belgrade.

In 1956, Nikita Khrushchev denounced Stalin. A committed Stalinist, Hoxha launched fierce attacks on Khrushchev and the Soviet Union, ultimately cutting all ties with Moscow in 1961. Even after Khrushchev left office, Hoxha remained firmly anti-Soviet, continuing to condemn Soviet policies.

Once estranged from the USSR, the United States reached out, hoping to turn Albania into a state like Yugoslavia. Albania’s reply was unyielding: “Albania has not, does not, and will never have any relations with US imperialism.”

The 1968 Prague Spring intervention alarmed Hoxha. Fearing Albania would be the next Czechoslovakia, he withdrew Albania from the Warsaw Pact and turned against the Soviet-aligned Eastern European states.

Having quarrelled with nearly everyone, Albania’s only remaining friend was China. Yet despite massive Chinese aid, Albania showed little gratitude. It treated this support as an entitlement and kept demanding ever more resources. Hoxha even presumed to lecture China on its foreign policy, questioning why Beijing had not consulted Tirana beforehand on key diplomatic moves. By the 1980s, Albania broke with its last ally, and also denounced the Non-Aligned Movement and the wider Third World.

Yugoslavia, the Soviet Union and China had all poured critical assistance into Albania’s development, yet none retained its friendship for long. Over decades, Albania managed to alienate the US, USSR, China, Britain, Yugoslavia and most of Eastern Europe. It became a rare Cold War state that fell out with both superpower blocs and the Non-Aligned Movement. Among the five permanent UN Security Council members, Albania maintained normal relations only with France.

Virtually isolated from the whole world, Albania regarded itself as the sole true guardian of Marxism-Leninism. Hoxha even dreamed of leading the global communist movement, advancing what became known as Hoxhaism. In his worldview, justice existed nowhere outside Albania; “righteousness always belongs to us”. The education system removed foreign language courses, on the premise that Albania no longer needed to engage with the outside world.

Hoxha’s radical foreign policy turned Albania into an international pariah. Few nations traded or maintained diplomatic relations with it. The country sank into near-total isolation, and generations of Albanians grew up with little understanding of the outside world — with devastating long-term consequences.

Convinced enemies were poised to invade, Hoxha developed acute paranoia. After the split with Moscow, he warned that invasion could come at any moment. He ordered a strange rule for the air force: every mission required two fighter jets. One carried out the task, while the second flew overhead to monitor it. If the pilot below defected, the second jet had permission to shoot it down, acting as an airborne overseer. Later, Hoxha decided two planes were insufficient, adding a third jet to watch the second: an overseer to watch the overseer.

If this paranoia only wasted aviation fuel, the damage would have been limited. But Albania had almost no strategic depth, and Hoxha needed a plan to resist a land invasion. His answer: build bunkers, countless concrete bunkers. For more than twenty years, this small country constructed over 700,000 concrete fortifications, roughly one completed every hour. Hemispherical underground pillboxes dotted every corner of the land. By the late Hoxha era, there was roughly one bunker for every three Albanians, earning Albania the nickname “the Bunker State”.

The bunkers consumed staggering national resources. A single bunker cost as much as a two-bedroom apartment; ten bunkers equalled the cost of one kilometre of highway. Over two decades, Albania poured more concrete into these fortifications than France’s Maginot Line, spending twice the Maginot Line’s total budget. The nation exhausted its wealth on defensive works that, in practice, had almost no military value.

These bunkers were largely useless. They lacked supply lines and supporting logistics, and from a geopolitical perspective, no country had any real incentive to invade poor, resource-poor Albania. The Soviet Union, which had crushed Czechoslovakia in 1968, calculated that seizing Albania would require 400,000 troops and 3,000 tanks, taking two and a half years to subdue all the scattered bunkers. Even if they conquered the whole country, Albania had almost nothing of value to offset the enormous cost of the campaign.

Still, Hoxha’s defence buildup left one lasting legacy beyond the bunkers: massive stockpiles of weapons. The Albanian army had only 18,000 soldiers, yet the country held 750,000 Chinese-made rifles and more than a billion rounds of ammunition, not counting arms supplied earlier by Yugoslavia and the USSR. Hoxha turned Albania into a giant armoury, with extraordinarily high weapons per capita. Decades later, these stockpiles would have unexpected ripple effects on organised crime across Europe.

After decades of internal and external strain, Albania’s economy collapsed. Without the USSR’s vast resource base, the country could not sustain such burdens. By Hoxha’s death, Albania ranked as Europe’s poorest nation.

Enver Hoxha died in 1985. The Party of Labour declared him immortal, marking his grave only with his birth year, 1908, and omitting any date of death. To this day, his supporters still commemorate “the years since Comrade Hoxha’s eternal life”. Ironically, his grand mausoleum did not survive. Years after his passing, the new government exhumed his remains and reburied him in an ordinary public cemetery.

After Hoxha’s death, Albania could not sustain its isolation indefinitely. The Revolutions of 1989 reached Albania. In 1991, the Party of Labour reorganised into the Socialist Party, multi-party elections were held, and the country was renamed the Republic of Albania. In 1992, Albania fully adopted a capitalist system — and immediately plunged into crisis.

In 1919, Charles Ponzi devised a financing scheme in Boston, USA. He claimed he could profit from international postal reply coupons, promising investors a 40% return within three months. At first, he honoured interest payments on schedule. Within months, people across Boston and then America rushed to invest. Rich and poor alike handed him their savings. In just one year, Ponzi raised 15 million US dollars. Many hailed him alongside Columbus and Marconi as one of the three greatest Italians. To the public, Ponzi had “discovered money”, a man with a Midas touch. The fraud finally collapsed in August 1920. Ponzi had bought only two postal coupons in total. His supposed trading business generated no real profit. All interest paid to early investors came from fresh deposits by new participants. This model, repaying old debts with new investors’ capital, became known worldwide as the Ponzi scheme.

For a hundred years, Ponzi schemes have repeatedly resurfaced in different forms, always ending in collapse. But in Albania, the Ponzi scheme reached its most destructive historical peak. Albania’s tragedy showed the world just how much damage such a scam could inflict.
