On the western shore of Lake Jugla in Riga, capital of Latvia, sits the Riga Motor Museum. It is the largest antique car museum in the Baltic region, home to more than 230 rare vintage automobiles.

Among its prized exhibits is a black 1966 Rolls-Royce, displayed in the southeast corner of the main hall. Surrounded by gleaming, immaculate classics, this one stands out for all the wrong reasons. Its front end is crumpled and crushed, as if run over flat.

Lean closer, and you will spot a wax figure in the driver’s seat, its face drawn and eyes cast upward in visible distress. The poor driver looks utterly trapped. Yet once visitors recognise that signature heavy, curved eyebrow shape, they gasp: that is Leonid Brezhnev.

It all began one autumn night in 1980, on the outskirts of Moscow. A deafening crash jolted nearby residents out of sleep. Curious locals hurried over, and found the source by the roadside: that sleek black luxury sedan had slammed head-on into a heavy truck.

No luxury car stood a chance against a heavy lorry. The truck barely moved, while the Rolls’ front was wrecked. But the crowd found no driver inside, sparking a baffling question: had the Soviet Union already invented self-driving cars?

The truth emerged a few days later. This Rolls-Royce had been a gift from Queen Elizabeth II. At the time, imported cars were politically awkward in the USSR, so the vehicle sat unused, stored west of Moscow for years. Later, Brezhnev ordered his driver to bring it into the city — and that was when the crash took place. The real driver had left immediately to call for help, which explained the empty cabin.

Muscovites were not convinced by the official version. A rumour quickly spread: right after impact, a broad, bulky grey figure scrambled out of the wreck and sprinted away, vanishing before the truck driver could get a clear look. All he caught sight of was that famous pair of thick, arched eyebrows.

This urban legend is certainly false. Brezhnev was well into his seventies by then, far too slow to run off like that even if he wanted to speed away. Still, the myth did not come from nowhere. Leonid Brezhnev was genuinely the most famous amateur speed demon among Soviet leaders.

A deeply controversial head of state, Brezhnev had wide-ranging hobbies: smoking, drinking, hunting, and he happily accepted gifts from subordinates and foreign guests. Next to his obsession with medals, cars were his favourite collectibles.

Nobody knows the exact date he first learned to drive, but it traces back to the Great Patriotic War. World War Two produced a whole generation of fast Soviet drivers. Back then, what they raced against were German Stuka dive bombers; lag even a little, and a bomb could land on you. Drivers from that era treated sharp cornering and rapid take-offs as basic skills. That was where Brezhnev fell in love with the rush of speed and automobiles.

His medal mania only took hold after a stroke, but his passion for cars went back much earlier. As early as 1955, someone gave him a Chevrolet as a present. Once he consolidated power, his obsession grew bolder, and he often got behind the wheel himself, even as head of state.

A well-known Soviet joke captures the mood. Moscow police once ordered strict crackdowns on speeding vehicles, promising tickets for everyone caught. One day Brezhnev was riding in his limousine and told his driver: “Move to the back. I’ll drive for a while.” He hit the gas and raced down the road, breaking speed limit after speed limit. Patrolman Ivan saw everything, yet dared not write a ticket.

His superior demanded an explanation. “Why no citation?”

“I can’t,” Ivan replied. “This man is far too high-ranking.”

“We were told to treat everyone equally. Who could possibly be above the law?”

“I don’t know his name,” Ivan said, “but his passenger is Brezhnev.”

It is only a joke, but it grew from real life.

In 1972, Henry Kissinger travelled to Moscow for arms control talks with Brezhnev. Between negotiating sessions, Brezhnev warmly invited Kissinger out to an island retreat and offered to drive him to the boat dock personally. Kissinger noticed that none of the Soviet officials dared climb into the car with them. Flattered, he thought Brezhnev reserved this special honour only for him, and hopped in.

Within minutes, Kissinger was terrified. He later wrote in his memoirs: “We tore down narrow, winding country lanes at terrifying speed. I prayed a policeman might appear at the next junction and end this deadly adventure. Even if one did, no cop would dare stop the General Secretary’s car.”

They reached the dock safely, and Brezhnev sent Kissinger onto a hydrofoil boat. Thankfully, he had no plans to pilot the watercraft. To Kissinger, Brezhnev set a new speed record among every world leader he had ever met.

If Kissinger was an unwitting hostage to Brezhnev’s reckless driving, Richard Nixon practically volunteered for the ride.

In May 1972, Nixon visited Moscow. Hoping to impress his Soviet counterpart, he brought his presidential Lincoln limousine along. Brezhnev admired it openly, then bluntly asked Nixon: “Can I get one of these too? A standard version, no custom work, please.”

Nixon had not expected the direct request and had little choice but to agree.

In June 1973, Brezhnev travelled to the United States for a return visit. Nixon gave him a navy-blue Lincoln, trimmed with black velvet inside, engraved with the line: “With warm wishes for happy memories.”

Brezhnev was overjoyed. Ignoring the protests of security staff, he pushed Nixon into the passenger seat, hit the throttle, and shot off.

This was no smooth highway. They were in Camp David, hilly, winding countryside unfamiliar to Brezhnev. He ignored “slow, sharp bend” signs and kept his foot down. To Nixon, it felt like every turn could be their last.

He threw diplomatic caution aside and shouted repeatedly: “Slow down! Slow down!” Brezhnev paid him no mind. At eighty miles an hour, he drifted through a downhill bend before slamming on the brakes; tyres screamed, and Nixon nearly blacked out.

Nixon’s security detail were horrified. That speed was far beyond presidential safety protocols, yet they could not intervene. If the Soviet leader crashed an American gift car on US soil with the US president beside him, who would be held responsible?

When they finally stopped, Brezhnev stroked the bodywork, praising the Lincoln nonstop. Nixon, relieved to be alive, had to smile and compliment him back: “You are an excellent driver. That cornering is something I could never master.” Years later, Nixon complained in his memoirs that diplomacy had never felt so dangerous.

Word of Brezhnev’s weakness spread. Host nations knew to gift him cars and medals to win favour during negotiations. Senior Soviet figures like Defence Minister Ustinov and Foreign Minister Gromyko were shrewd negotiators, but Brezhnev’s hobby made him far easier to read. A fine car, generous aid packages, or a fresh batch of medals, and he would light up.

But why would a superpower like the USSR import foreign cars and collect them? Could Soviet domestic auto industry not satisfy him? The story stretches back to the very beginning of Russian motoring.

Yakovlev, the founding father of Russia’s automotive sector, had served as a naval lieutenant and travelled widely at sea. Dismissed from service for a disciplinary offence in 1883, he turned to internal combustion engines. He built Russia’s first internal combustion engine in 1889 and its first finished automobile in 1896.

Four years later, a truck designed by Boris was exhibited at the Paris World’s Fair. Inspired, Tsar Nicholas II planned to build a national auto industry and poured 11 million roubles into six new factories. Just as the industry was ready to take off, 1917 brought revolution. Civil war erupted between Bolsheviks and White forces, and those newly built plants were destroyed.

Once the civil war ended, the young Soviet state found its automotive sector all but ruined. The largest Moscow plant could barely repair vehicles, never mind mass-produce them. Blockaded by hostile Western powers, building a complete automotive supply chain from scratch seemed impossible.

Then the Great Depression hit the United States in 1929, tearing down trade barriers. Cash-rich Stalin became a prized customer for Western corporations desperate for business. The USSR imported massive amounts of Western technology. Ford shipped an entire complete car factory, blueprints, production lines and worker training included. That plant became the famous Gorkovsky Avtomobilny Zavod (GAZ), home to GAZ vehicles, Ford-derived models and the elegant Chaika limousine.

The old Moscow AMO plant, once little more than a repair shop, also secured American partners, technical guidance and steady parts supply. Renamed Zavod Imeni Stalina, later the Likhachev Plant, it built the USSR’s premium ZIS and ZIL limousines.

By 1939, Soviet annual car output topped 200,000 units, a genuine mass-production milestone. Most of those, however, were trucks. Passenger cars made up less than 10 percent of total production. World War Two soon broke out, and the whole auto industry shifted to military trucks; civilian sedan manufacturing nearly halted.

After the war, Soviet car production slowly resumed, still built largely on adapted General American designs. The industry’s priority remained heavy trucks until 1959, when passenger cars finally got more attention. In 1966, the Soviet Union partnered with Italy to build the AvtoVAZ plant for affordable family cars. Its output bore the name Lada, not Volga.

Production numbers surged. 610,000 vehicles in 1965; 1.96 million in 1975; over two million by 1976, making the USSR the world’s fifth-largest car producer.

Rising output collided with soaring consumer demand. Living standards rose under Brezhnev, and many Soviet citizens could afford a car financially. Under the planned economy, however, affordability meant nothing next to availability. This is the famous Soviet car queue, a classic symbol of how the planned economy worked.

Factories followed rigid state production targets, not market demand. Even with massive unmet consumer demand, they had no incentive to expand output. State planners adjusted quotas slowly, always lagging behind real needs.

The result was a uniquely Soviet ritual: pay the money first, then join the waiting list. The wait could stretch months or years, decided by luck and connections. Another classic Soviet joke captured this absurdity. A man paid for a new car, and the clerk told him he could collect it in ten years.

“Morning or afternoon?” the buyer asked.

“Why bother? It’s ten years away,” the clerk said.

“Because my television is being delivered that same morning.”

Quantity was not the only problem. Soviet cars were notoriously inconsistent in quality. The Soviet automotive system excelled at functional heavy vehicles, especially specialised military trucks. The MAZ trucks built in Minsk were legendary. They thrived on mud-choked Siberian roads where tanks struggled, started reliably down to minus fifty degrees Celsius, and often used tank-derived engines to tow artillery and intercontinental ballistic missiles.

Capable as they were, MAZ cabins were brutally spartan, with metal dashboards and almost no comfort features. Soviet engineering logic was simple: the vehicle must run. Fancy extras were unnecessary.

When that mindset was applied to family sedans, the results were dismal. The character Petrov in *House of Cards* perfectly summed up the reputation of the Lada: shoddy build quality, cramped cabin, windscreen flying forward under hard braking, flickering lights, no air conditioning, seats hot enough to stick skin to them in summer. A rolling coffin on wheels.

The Lada was representative of Soviet passenger cars. Production volumes climbed, yet quality declined. Poor quality control produced bizarre failures: windscreens popping out under hard braking, wheels detaching mid-drive. Designers cared only that the car moved and was cheap enough to mass produce. Hot? Roll down the window. Cold? Put on more layers. Those were not considered engineering problems.

Ordinary citizens complained, and even senior officials grew fed up. At a Supreme Soviet session in 1987, officials blasted the Ministry of the Automotive Industry: out of more than sixty car models made that year, only three passed quality standards.

Why did factories refuse to improve? This was a deep flaw within the planned economy. Upgrading quality brought no reward for plant management. There was no domestic competition. No matter how shoddy the product, demand outstripped supply. Factories saw no reason to risk falling short of output quotas by retooling lines for better quality.

This was a widespread sickness across Soviet industry. Outside the military sector, innovation lagged consistently behind the West. The USSR had extraordinary research power: by the 1970s, one in four scientists worldwide worked in the Soviet Union, and its annual new inventions ranked second globally, just behind Japan. Yet most research papers sat gathering dust in institute archives. With no competitive pressure, factories had no urge to turn ideas into products. Only six to seven percent of Soviet researchers worked inside manufacturing firms.

Only one quarter of Soviet domestic research ever reached production, with an adoption cycle of ten to twelve years. By contrast, 85 percent of American research entered production within five years. In some surreal cases, Soviet inventions were commercialised in the United States first, then imported back into the USSR as advanced foreign technology.

That said, the USSR was capable of building fine passenger cars. Those three top-rated models praised in the Supreme Soviet report did exist, though almost all of them were reserved for export. By 1984, around 400,000 premium Soviet cars sold abroad annually, with a steady customer base in Western Europe.

Few high-quality cars reached Soviet buyers domestically. The ZIS‑101 from the first Five-Year Plan was one of the rare exceptions. Stalin ordered the Stalin Plant to build a world-class luxury limousine. The stakes were high: success meant promotion, failure meant severe consequences. The plant delivered the ZIS‑101 in 1936. It had every luxury feature of its era: powerful engine, elegant styling, spacious cabin, heater and radio, top-tier equipment for its time. Stalin was pleased and signed off for mass production. It became the official car for senior Moscow officials and even served as a Moscow taxi.

Yet this remarkably refined Soviet limousine was almost a direct copy of the 1932 Buick 32‑90.

As a Soviet luxury sedan, it carried a strong American flavour. Compared with the cramped Volgas and Ladas that came decades later, the ZIS‑101 was cavernous inside, seating four adults comfortably in the back. Its chrome front grille stood out against drab Soviet design norms. Powered by a 5.8-litre straight-eight engine inherited from Buick, it delivered strong torque — a practical necessity on Russia’s rough roads, not just a luxury toy.

That abundant power, generous space and creature comforts won Stalin’s approval, though no subordinate dared tell him its blueprint came from a capitalist Buick.

By faithfully recreating the 1930s Buick design with minor local tweaks, the ZIS‑101 outperformed all other Soviet cars of its day. Buick became the blueprint for the USSR’s first generation of luxury limousines.

General Motors expanded rapidly in the 1930s, and Buick was its core brand, balancing luxury and practicality well. The brand has kept evolving, releasing models such as the modern Verano Pro GS. This compact sedan carries Buick’s classic refined character while catering to younger drivers. It features a distinctive honeycomb grille, black sport trim and gunmetal grey bumpers for layered, muscular styling. Its modular 1.5-litre four-cylinder engine balances performance and fuel economy.

Inside, a wide dual-screen display handles navigation, media and voice commands, with OTA over-the-air updates. Modern automotive technology has come a very long way from the antique cars in Brezhnev’s collection.

Brezhnev owned roughly a hundred automobiles in total, most gifted from overseas, with a small number presented by domestic officials. In his final years, poor health stopped him from driving, yet he kept collecting cars, just as he amassed more medals than any other person in history, whether or not he truly deserved them.

Brezhnev’s colourful, controversial automotive obsession ended with his death in 1982. His daughter Galina tried to inherit the collection, but the Soviet state refused. The cars were state gifts to the USSR; Brezhnev only held use rights, and they were never his private property.

Yuri Andropov took office and set about cleaning up the excesses of the Brezhnev era: stripping unearned medals and honorary titles, removing his ghostwritten autobiographies from bookstores, and erasing Brezhnev’s name from war memorials.

His cars met mixed fates. His ZIL‑115 limousine was stripped for parts, only one seat preserved. A GAZ Chaika he received on his seventieth birthday was destroyed entirely. The rest scattered across museums and storage.

That crumpled Rolls-Royce from the opening story was too badly damaged for restoration. Left unwanted, it was sold to the Riga Motor Museum, where it remains a strange, tangible relic of that eccentric old leader’s passion for driving. Few visitors realise that this wrecked luxury car is one of the surviving physical witnesses to Brezhnev’s famous love affair with the wheel.
