Have you ever been insulted by strangers online? Or subjected to their sneers and sarcasm? When you come across people who are cynical and full of pent‑up hostility, they may well be cynics.

Cynicism originated in Ancient Greece as an early school of philosophy. It advocated returning to nature, rejected hypocrisy, and held wealth and authority in contempt. The most renowned cynic was Diogenes. He rejected money, power and fame, deeming them meaningless, and stressed that people should pursue nature and freedom. He defied all social conventions. He even lived in a large jar and behaved like a dog — hence the name “cynic”.

Traditional cynicism was essentially a radical philosophy of liberalism. It held that so long as one lived authentically and freely, everything else was superfluous.

In modern times, however, cynicism has degenerated into an extreme mindset born of disillusionment with reality. Simply put, it means believing in nothing and sneering at everything, a far cry from original cynicism.

Modern‑day cynics tend to be sharp‑tongued. Beyond mere doubt and apathy, their attitudes carry aggression, sarcasm and nihilism. They lay bare what they see and then mock it.

For instance, when someone says, “I want to do something meaningful,” they retort, “Quit pretending. You just want online clout.”
When someone states, “I will work hard to get a promotion and a raise,” they sneer, “Save your fantasies. Those with connections have already climbed the ladder.”
When someone shares, “My partner and I share true love,” they scoff, “What a joke that anyone still believes in love in this day and age.”

Modern cynics ridicule the world, mock convictions, and dismiss true love, yet refuse to take concrete action to change things. They are not inherently malicious. More often than not, repeated setbacks and deep disillusionment prompt them to over‑protect themselves, turning them cold, distrustful of everything, and prone to sarcasm.

Contemporary cynicism acts as psychological self‑defence against the enormous gap between reality and ideals. It works both as a tool for criticism and as an act of retreat.

What makes cynicism dangerous is not its tendency to question everything, but how it robs people of the courage to try to believe. It leaves people appearing sharp‑witted and unbreakable on the surface, while their inner selves gradually grow desensitised and numb.

Many young people today grow accustomed to living amid cynical laughter and self‑deprecation. They mock ideals and question sincerity, seeming intelligent and perceptive. Quite a few people lean towards cynicism.

At the end of the day, cynicism is merely a defensive shell formed after being hurt. True maturity is not to sneer at everything, but to strive for tiny glimmers of light even after witnessing life’s bleakness. To see the world for what it is, yet still choose to love life — that is a higher form of clarity than cynicism.
