In January 2021, a migrant caravan of more than 8,000 people surged across Guatemala’s border. The frontier defenses were as fragile as a sheet of paper. Guatemalan police ultimately threw in the towel, letting the migrants break through, firing only two warning shots into the air. Their job was simply to earn the pay sent by the Biden administration.

These migrants were bound for the United States. They faced a 3,000-kilometer trek on foot, crossing Guatemala and Mexico to reach the U.S.–Mexico border. Penniless and sleeping rough, they faced constant extortion from gangs and drug traffickers along the way. The odds of successfully entering the U.S. were minuscule. Even so, they set out without hesitation.

What drove them to abandon their homes was their homeland: a cyberpunk nation called Honduras.

In 1502, Christopher Columbus arrived near present-day Honduras and gave the land a fateful name — “the depths.”

Honduras sits in Central America. To most people, Central America is a cluster of obscure small nations, and that impression holds largely true. The region has eight countries. Apart from Mexico, which has some recognition, the remaining seven are Honduras, El Salvador, Belize, Guatemala, Nicaragua, Panama and Costa Rica. They share one trait: extremely low global profile. They make perfect tricky geography quiz questions.

Still, among Central American states, Honduras has relatively higher notoriety. It repeatedly appears on rankings published by international organizations: the world’s most dangerous countries, the globe’s most violent cities, the riskiest tourist destinations, and more.

Sandwiched between the Pacific Ocean and the Caribbean Sea, Honduras covers 112,000 square kilometers with a population of over nine million. Its per capita GDP stood at roughly 2,600 U.S. dollars in 2019. Seventy percent of its people live below the poverty line. Education and healthcare systems are in ruins, with high infection rates for malaria, HIV and other diseases among Latin America’s worst.

Yet poverty alone cannot push so many people to flee. That migrant caravan mentioned at the start made up nearly one-thousandth of Honduras’ total population of nine million. Those leaving Honduras are not merely migrants — they are refugees. Their exodus stems from the fact that Honduras has become one of the world’s most violent nations.

The country boasts stunning scenery, seemingly an earthly paradise. In reality, it is a haven for criminals.

So how did Honduras end up in this state?

Honduras gained independence more than 130 years ago, but its government has seen constant, violent turnover. Compared to a Honduran president’s job, serving as South Korea’s president would count as comparatively safe. A nation with such short-lived governments is clearly abnormal.

The reason Honduran regimes never last is that it functions like a cyberpunk state: presidents come and go, while giant corporations hold permanent power.

Fictional sci-fi conglomerates that shape national destinies are often portrayed as sophisticated entities — think Umbrella Corporation developing bioweapons, or alien corporations running social experiments on humans. Yet these fictional enterprises pale in power and influence next to the company that once controlled Honduras.

The corporation that ruled Honduras sold a deceptively simple product. Grand titans of industry sometimes only need the plainest goods: it was a banana exporter. Its deeds remain etched in the collective memory of all Latin Americans. This was the **United Fruit Company**.

Americans first took an interest in bananas around 1870. The tropical fruit quickly grew wildly popular across the U.S. To this day, bananas remain Walmart’s top-selling fruit nationwide.

During the banana trade boom, a dock vendor named Samuel Zemurray struck it rich. Using his profits, he established banana plantations spanning more than 2,000 hectares in Honduras. Honduras’ climate was ideal for banana cultivation. To secure long-term operations, Zemurray bribed local officials and secured tax-exempt status.

Just as his banana business flourished, the Honduran government hit a major crisis.

The 19th century belonged to the British Empire. Britain ran a predatory lending empire worldwide, backed by Royal Navy gunboat diplomacy to collect debts. Honduras was one of its borrowers. Having taken on debts far beyond its repayment capacity, Honduras defaulted, and Britain prepared to send naval forces to collect.

This alarmed the United States. America regarded Latin America as its sphere of influence and deeply resented European powers meddling in the Americas. In 1910, U.S. President William Howard Taft ordered the U.S. government to pay off Honduras’ massive British debt.

The money was not a gift. The price of repayment was U.S. takeover of Honduras’ customs service, with all tax revenues managed by American officials.

What began as a diplomatic arrangement between governments landed hard on Zemurray. The U.S.-controlled customs authority refused to recognize his old tax exemption and insisted on charging one cent tariff per pound of exported bananas. This levy would bankrupt him.

Zemurray appealed to the U.S. government for leniency, but Washington was unsympathetic: “Bankruptcy alone is hardly cause for complaint. You may lose everything, but American capital stands to lose substantial tariff revenue.”

Rebuffed in Washington, Zemurray returned to New Orleans facing ruin. But this self-made dock merchant was no ordinary man. What he did next was extraordinary.

Stuck in New Orleans and facing collapse, Zemurray hired mercenaries and brought large sums of cash. He backed the exiled Manuel Bonilla and returned with him to Honduras.

Once back home, Zemurray poured money into media campaigns denouncing U.S. control over Honduran customs and foreign manipulation of the national government. This public outcry ignited a revolution, toppling the sitting Honduran government. Bonilla was hailed by popular sentiment, won the presidency again, and riding the wave of public anger forced the U.S. to abandon its customs control.

Zemurray’s investment yielded staggering returns. Once in office, Bonilla repaid the favor: restoring Zemurray’s 25-year tax exemption, granting him a 500,000-dollar loan, and ceding more than 10,000 hectares of land for plantations for free. The U.S. money used to settle Honduras’ British debt went up in smoke, while Zemurray amassed an enormous fortune.

Most importantly, Zemurray learned a vital lesson from this episode: if an existing government creates problems you cannot solve, replace the government itself.

For decades afterward, bananas became an inseparable theme in Central American history. Much of the region’s poverty and conflict existed largely to supply fruit to the United States. Nations whose economies were held hostage by foreign capital came to be mockingly called “banana republics.”

In 1930, United Fruit Company, then the world’s largest fruit firm, bought Zemurray’s business for stock worth 13 million U.S. dollars. Zemurray did not step aside after the acquisition. Instead, he joined United Fruit’s core leadership and rose three years later to become the corporation’s chief executive. The boss of an acquired subsidiary had taken control of the parent giant. The *New York Times* dubbed Zemurray “the minnow that swallowed the whale,” making this one of America’s most famous business legends.

Zemurray’s corporate triumph marked deepening suffering across Central America.

Bribing presidents became United Fruit’s signature tactic. Under Zemurray’s leadership, the company meddled heavily in Honduran domestic politics and exported this playbook across Latin America: buying politicians, backing warlords, interfering in state affairs, rewriting laws.

Why did United Fruit need to plunge so deeply into regional politics?

Local governments could not only grant tax breaks; they could suppress labor unrest for the company.

Banana farming requires little advanced technology; it is labor-intensive, with harvesting labor forming its biggest cost. As capitalists, United Fruit kept plantation workers’ wages as low as possible. Banana harvesting was dangerous work: swinging machetes could maim workers instantly. After pesticides became widespread, highly concentrated chemicals damaged workers’ eyesight, leaving many blind or crippled after just a few years of labor. Workplace injury insurance barely existed. Wages were meager, barely enough for subsistence.

Workers inevitably resisted. At the same time, global labor movements surged in the early 20th century, and military governments became extremely valuable to the corporation.

Even before Zemurray took power, atrocities had already unfolded. In November 1928, the Banana Massacre unfolded when workers on United Fruit plantations in Colombia launched a strike. They demanded better medical care, housing, and weekly rest days. Pressured by United Fruit, Colombia’s military government unleashed a brutal crackdown on striking workers. Machine guns killed thousands; at least one thousand died. Gabriel García Márquez wove this massacre into *One Hundred Years of Solitude*, leaving an indelible scar on Colombia’s national memory.

Any government that refused to comply would be replaced.

Latin America produced leaders who tried to change this status quo. In 1951, Jacobo Árbenz took power in Guatemala. Panama disease, a banana wilt fungus, had ravaged many banana fields. United Fruit chose to leave vast stretches of land idle rather than give it to poor farmers. The Árbenz administration seized four million acres of unused United Fruit land and redistributed it to landless peasants.

In 1952, United Fruit produced the *1952 Guatemala Report*, falsely accusing Árbenz of collaborating with the Soviet Union. The U.S. Central Intelligence Agency intervened, backing Árbenz’s opponents to stage a coup. The military regime that followed caused roughly 200,000 Guatemalan deaths over the next three decades.

After Guatemala, United Fruit realized even military governments could not always be trusted. It built private armed forces, with military camps right beside plantations. Records show during its operations in Latin America, United Fruit suppressed more than 5,000 labor strikes, with countless casualties.

Contemporary accounts claimed United Fruit’s 1961 net profit was roughly ten times Honduras’ total national fiscal revenue that year. Across Central America, it owned private military camps, ports and railways, and exerted tight control over transportation, industry, oil and communications. Contemporary writers nicknamed United Fruit executives “the Green Popes.”

The novel *Strong Wind* described its power:

“With a flick of his finger he could order a ship to sail or anchor; with one word he could buy a republic; with a sneeze presidents, generals and celebrities might fall; swiveling back in his office chair, he could spark a revolution.”

This banana corporation wielded power far greater than Apple today. It functioned as a political entity above nation-states, known as “the uncrowned king of Central America.”

At this point, one might ask: after decades of exploitation by United Fruit, did Honduras never produce a leader like Árbenz to resist?

Honduras did fight an external conflict, but ironically its anger was directed not at United Fruit, but its neighbor El Salvador.

Honduras’ most famous historical event was its war with El Salvador. The conflict was small-scale and brief, yet widely remembered. Its trigger sounded absurd: the two nations fought over football. Posterity named it the Football War.

Honduras and El Salvador shared a border and were both poor. The difference lay in their geography: Honduras had abundant land and sparse population, focused on banana cultivation. El Salvador was tiny and densely populated, reliant on coffee plantations.

The root of their friction came from differing national conditions. With plentiful land, Honduras attracted large numbers of Salvadoran migrants to work. Some migrants built businesses and grew wealthy; others acquired farmland. These newcomers gradually became resented by native Hondurans, accused of seizing local land and draining national wealth.

In 1968, Honduras’ president enacted a policy reclaiming land held by Salvadoran migrants and returning it to Honduran citizens. The policy displaced tens of thousands of Salvadorans, who were repatriated home, worsening land pressure inside El Salvador. Bitterness between the two nations flared sharply. The U.S. government was happy to stoke ethnic division to divert attention from its own interests. By the late 1960s, bilateral relations were on the brink of collapse.

By chance, the 1970 FIFA World Cup was hosted in Mexico. The Central American and Caribbean qualifying zone had two berths. Host Mexico automatically took one spot. Honduras and El Salvador defeated other contenders and fought for the remaining World Cup ticket.

On June 8, 1969, the first leg was played in Honduras. The night before the match, Honduran fans gathered outside the Salvadoran national team hotel, banging gongs and setting off firecrackers nonstop. The Salvadoran players got no sleep. Exhausted, the stronger Salvadoran side lost 1–0 to Honduras.

Honduras’ dirty tactics enraged public opinion in El Salvador. A young Salvadoran female fan committed suicide out of despair. El Salvador held a state funeral for her, attended by the president. Media framed her death as grief over national humiliation. The incident fully ignited Salvadoran nationalist fervor.

June 15 saw the second leg in El Salvador. Now the Honduran team faced retaliation. Salvadoran fans did not merely create noise; dead rats and rotten eggs were thrown into the players’ rooms. When Honduran players called police, officers sided with the fans. Honduran players traveled between hotel and stadium in armored vehicles. Two Honduran spectators were beaten to death. Under this immense pressure, Honduras lost 0–3.

News of defeat reached Honduras and sparked nationwide fury. Citizens looted Salvadoran shops and assaulted Salvadoran residents, in a Honduran version of Kristallnacht. More than 10,000 Salvadorans were expelled. Cross-border animosity reached its peak.

June 23: El Salvador declared a state of emergency and mobilized its military. War loomed.

Under the qualifying format of that era, with one win each, the two teams needed a decisive third match. On June 27, 1969, the playoff match took place in Mexico City. Mexican authorities enforced heavy security. The 100,000-capacity stadium only admitted 20,000 spectators, with large buffer zones separating fans from each country to prevent riots.

After 90 minutes of regular play, the score stood 2–2. In extra time, El Salvador scored the winner to take the match 3–2 and secure the World Cup berth. El Salvador erupted in celebration. Honduras descended into chaos, with continued violent persecution of Salvadoran residents and hundreds of casualties. On the same day of the playoff match, El Salvador severed diplomatic ties with Honduras.

War was now nearly inevitable.

July 14, 1969: El Salvador launched the first strike. Tiny El Salvador, covering just 20,000 square kilometers, launched a blitzkrieg against much larger Honduras, and the Football War began.

The smaller nation’s surprise attack on its bigger neighbor was dramatic. Both air forces flew vintage U.S. World War II piston fighters: El Salvador used P-51 Mustangs, while Honduras fielded F4U Corsairs. Aerial combat looked like an American WWII air battle reenacted.

Combat grew even more bizarre. These aircraft lacked proper bomb racks. Pilots leaned out of cockpits and tossed bombs by hand, tactics almost reminiscent of World War I. This air campaign marked the last combat engagement between piston-engine fighter planes in human history.

Ground fighting was equally chaotic. Combined regular army troops from both countries totaled merely fifteen battalions. Neither side had prepared for large-scale war. Honduran border troops retreated after token resistance. Salvadoran forces easily seized some border territory, yet their supply lines collapsed within two days of fighting. Honduras hastily recalled more than a thousand veterans to the front and won minor gains, yet soon ran low on ammunition.

After just three days of combat, one army ran out of supplies and the other ran out of bullets. The battlefield fell into an eerie lull.

July 18, 1969: Mediated by the United States and the Organization of American States, the two sides agreed to a ceasefire and later withdrew troops to their own territories. The whole war lasted roughly one hundred hours, causing about 3,000 casualties in total.

There were no winners.

El Salvador’s war failed to resolve its land pressure. Its domestic situation worsened, plunging the nation into a long civil war that only ended in 1992.

For Honduras, the war destroyed the Central American Common Market. Its economy, which had shown faint signs of recovery, sank once more.

Although El Salvador qualified for the World Cup, wartime exhaustion devastated its team. At the 1970 World Cup, it lost all three group matches without scoring a single goal and returned home defeated.

So the 1970 World Cup closed against the backdrop of tragedy for Honduras and El Salvador. Both nations entered the 1970s scarred and broken.

Yet change came to Central America in the 1970s. Starting in the 1960s, a wave of national awakening swept Latin America, and U.S. foreign intervention grew less sustainable.

Hurricane Fifi struck Honduras in 1974, killing 11,000 people. Massive United Fruit plantations were destroyed. The corporation never fully recovered. It later rebranded as Chiquita. It remains a major fruit conglomerate today, yet no longer holds the power to overthrow national governments.

United Fruit’s decline should have benefited Central America. But fortune carries misfortune in its wake. After the “Green Pope” departed, the “White Pope” arrived: drug cartels.

Pablo Escobar died in 1993, familiar to viewers of the TV series *Narcos*. One of history’s most infamous drug lords, Escobar’s death prompted Colombian authorities to intensify anti-drug crackdowns. Trafficking networks relocated their operations outward, and Honduras became one of their most critical hubs.

Thanks to its geographic position, Honduras had long served as a transit route for narcotics. In the 1990s, the drug trade upgraded locally: sourcing production expertise from Colombia in the south, connected to Mexico’s distribution networks to the north. Criminal groups fully exploited this geographic advantage.

The United States inadvertently fueled gang growth in Honduras during the 1990s.

From the mid-20th century onward, hundreds of thousands of Central Americans migrated to the U.S. Many migrants from lower socioeconomic backgrounds turned to crime. American prisons held tens of thousands of Honduran and Salvadoran gang members. These violent inmates became a headache for U.S. law enforcement. In the 1990s, the U.S. found a convenient solution: instead of continuing costly incarceration, deport all these criminals back to Central America, offloading the problem.

Tens of thousands of hardened gang members were sent back to Honduras and El Salvador. Honduras’ vice president lamented at the time that the nation was on the verge of collapse.

For countries with populations of only a few million, suddenly receiving tens of thousands of professional violent offenders had catastrophic consequences. Deported gang members quickly built organized networks, and gangs proliferated across Central America. Honduras, once reasonably peaceful, turned into a battlefield for gang wars within a few short years.

Some law enforcement assessments estimated that roughly 80% of cocaine entering the United States transited through Honduras during certain periods. Annual capital flows tied to the drug industry here are enormous, far more profitable than banana farming ever was. Joining a gang in Honduras pays at least ten times the wage of plantation workers. For masses of unemployed people, gang membership holds strong appeal.

Huge illicit cash flows, massive unemployment, and seasoned violent criminals converged to push Honduras to the top tier of the world’s most violent countries.

By some UNODC-era estimates, Honduras’ murder rate exceeded 90 per 100,000 residents, far above the global average, placing it among the world’s most violent nations; neighboring El Salvador also recorded extremely high homicide figures.

San Pedro Sula in Honduras earned the moniker “City of the Devil,” with a murder rate of 173 per 100,000 people, deadlier than many active war zones.

Gangs profit from every source of revenue, big and small. They charge steep protection fees to local merchants. In this deeply poor nation, failure to pay or any slight offense can bring execution, sometimes killing entire families. This forms the core driver behind the Honduran refugee crisis.

One may wonder: gangs run rampant, so why does police not act?

Only 4% of homicide cases in Honduras lead to convictions. This means a perpetrator has a 96% chance of escaping punishment. Often police do not arrest gang members; they collude with them, paid to fight one gang faction for another. The state treasury teeters on collapse, and police officers need to survive.

Even imprisonment is hardly punishment. Some Honduran prisons have operated at more than 500% of their official design capacity, according to local monitoring reports. Prisons are largely ruled by inmates; guards dare not enter cell blocks. Prisoners receive family visits and can even arrange prostitution inside. In December 2019, a gunfight between inmates sparked a prison fire. The guard holding the keys abandoned their post and could not unlock gates to fight the blaze. 357 people died and dozens escaped, showing just how shattered the justice system is.

What about the national government?

Many senior Honduran officials have deep ties to drug trafficking networks. In 2018, evidence surfaced that President Juan Orlando Hernández took part in drug money laundering. He used cartel funds to buy military support and relied on military interference to reverse election results and secure re-election in 2017.

Within such a system, eradicating violence is nearly impossible. Official estimates state 2–3% of Hondurans have gang affiliations, while civilian estimates put the figure at 10–15%.

The government has put on cosmetic performances: to shed its reputation as a violent state, it once ordered police to reduce patrols and discourage crime reports, manipulating statistics to make crime look less severe.

This context explains why Hondurans choose to embark on this grueling, perilous journey rather than stay home. On the land Columbus named “the depths,” hope is scarce.

Honduras’ tragedy mirrors many Latin American nations. Looking back through history, many historians argue that decades of foreign intervention were a major contributing factor to Honduras’ descent into widespread violence. Ironically, opinion polls show Honduras consistently ranks among the countries with the highest approval ratings toward the United States. Ordinary people cannot dwell on old historical grievances; they simply have no other options.
