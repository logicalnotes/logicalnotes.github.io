February 14, 1984, Valentine’s Day.
Dr. Gutiérrez, a small-town Venezuelan doctor, poured his aged rum into a glass. Today he was celebrating an old friend’s birthday — and saying goodbye.

His friend ran a local stationery shop. This 72-year-old Spaniard had lived a life far more dramatic than anyone would guess from his quiet shopfront. He was finally packing up to move to Caracas and retire with his son.

The two old friends drank deep into the night. Half-drunk, the stationery shop owner started boasting. “I was something back in my day. The best spy in all of Europe. I’ve dined with statesmen, met countless powerful men.”

Dr. Gutiérrez heard this same story dozens of times over their decade-long friendship. He knew the routine: the old man was just drunk again.

They drank themselves into a stupor and parted ways, no formal farewell.

Months later, on June 6, 1984, Gutiérrez sat watching TV, tuning into the 40th-anniversary commemoration of the Normandy landings. Then a familiar face flashed across the screen. His heart skipped a beat. *Could every crazy thing the old man said actually be true?*

Juan Pujol García was born on February 14, 1912, in Barcelona, Spain.

To put it gently, 1912 was not a good year for Spain. Less gently: Spain had barely known a good year since the Spanish-American War of 1898.

That four-month war stripped Spain of nearly all its colonial empire. The United States took over its holdings across the Americas and the Philippines. What remained of Spain’s once-vast empire was a handful of poor, remote territories in Africa, such as Equatorial Guinea.

Defeat sent Spain spiraling. Political factions clashed, class tensions boiled over. By the 1909 Barcelona riots, the government barely kept order. The unrest only festered. Anyone visiting Spain back then got the same quiet warning: run, while you can.

That was the world Pujol was born into.

His family was comfortable, all things considered. His father ran a dye works and kept out of politics, smoking and watching the world go by, content to take each day as it came.

Raised under that laid-back influence, young Pujol hated school and skipped class constantly. His father never punished him harshly, trying to set him up with apprenticeships and French lessons, but nothing stuck.

Then in 1931, he signed up for a holiday course to learn chicken farming. Pujol settled down not out of newfound wisdom, but because his father had passed away. The family income collapsed, and debts piled up. He learned to raise chickens and took work at a poultry farm, hoping to live a simple, quiet life.

But some societies do not leave ordinary people alone to live quietly.

In 1931, Republican forces deposed the king and forced him into exile. Fascism surged across Europe, and Spain’s far-right grew powerful. The two sides collided in 1936, igniting the Spanish Civil War — a brutal precursor to the Second World War.

The Spanish Civil War was one of modern Europe’s bloodiest conflicts. On one side stood the International Brigades, on the other the fascist forces aligned with Nazi Germany. Volunteers from every corner of the globe poured into Spain to fight.

Both sides grew ruthless, and the violence tore apart ordinary families. Pujol’s family was released after arrest, but all their property was seized. To avoid conscription, Pujol hid in a tiny Barcelona room for a full year, surviving on food his girlfriend snuck in. He lost forty pounds. At twenty-five, he looked forty.

That gaunt appearance helped him dodge military checks. He got forged papers listing his age as forty-five and went looking for work. In wartime Spain, there was none. Cornered, he enlisted.

The haggard Pujol presented himself to the Republican government. The recruitment officer was impressed by the “older man’s” patriotic spirit, so he assigned him to signal duty with the International Brigade, away from frontline combat.

It sounded safe, but Pujol was miserable. The Republican supply lines were crumbling. Every meal was beans: lentils cooked with lard for breakfast, pork and lentils at noon, greens and lentils for dinner. Day after day, beans.

His brigade comrades were driven by ideology, ready to endure hardship for their cause. Pujol was only fighting to survive. Sick of the terrible rations, he decided to defect to the Nationalists — he’d heard they ate better.

Late in 1938, he slipped over with a small group of men. They did get a proper meal, but the Nationalists immediately locked them up and tortured them. He nearly died.

Only in 1939, after they convinced interrogators he was not a spy, did they hand him a rifle and send him toward the front. That betrayal scarred him deeply. Compared to the abuse he suffered, the bean-heavy rations of the International Brigade felt tolerable. He grew to hate fascism with every fiber of his being.

Luckily, the war was nearly over. He never saw combat before marching into Madrid with the Republican forces as the conflict ended.

Pujol had lost everything. His family was broken, and his girlfriend — the woman who had kept him alive while he hid — was dead in the fighting. The war taught him one harsh lesson: no one cared about the fate of a poor young man on Madrid’s streets. Even he could not have guessed that history would hand him a chance to change the course of the world.

After the war, Pujol scraped by on odd jobs. Sharp and resourceful, he saved enough to marry Araceli González. They wed in Madrid and tried to rebuild their lives.

Then war came again.

Hitler invaded Poland in September 1939. By 1940, Nazi armies rolled across France and crushed its defenses. The Nazis dominated continental Europe. Spain remained neutral, yet the war shook every part of Spanish life.

For Pujol, old traumas resurfaced. He resolved to do something to stop the Nazis. Whoever fought Hitler, he wanted to help.

The Soviet Union was still allied with Germany at that point, and he had no way to reach them. That left only two options: Britain and France.

France was out of the question; it had fallen too fast. Britain was his only choice.

In January 1941, Pujol walked into the British embassy in Madrid. He begged clerks and diplomats alike, saying he admired Britain and was ready to serve as an agent.

The British embassy brushed him off every single time. “Thank you for your offer. We will contact you if we need you. Please go home.”

He tried three times, with the same reply. Many later mock British diplomats for missing this chance, but their caution made sense. The factions of the Spanish Civil War were tangled, and recruiting random Spaniards risked huge diplomatic trouble for neutral Spain.

If Britain would not take him, Pujol thought up a wild plan. He would approach the Germans instead. He would trick them into paying him, feed them false intelligence, and quietly undermine Hitler’s regime.

He studied *Mein Kampf* until he could speak like a committed Nazi. Once he mastered the rhetoric, he contacted the German embassy in Madrid and requested a meeting with their intelligence attaché.

Nazi intelligence, the Abwehr, was famously flawed. While Germany fielded strong military tactics and hardware, its spy service was inconsistent at best. Admiral Wilhelm Canaris, head of the Abwehr, once supported Hitler but grew disillusioned and secretly leaned toward the Allies. Under his leadership, the Abwehr’s work was often shambolic.

Karl-Erich Kühlenthal, the senior Abwehr intelligence officer based in Spain, became notorious among British intelligence. He was easily fooled, quick to accept fake reports, and skeptical of genuine intelligence. Allied agents joked that he was their favorite enemy handler.

When Kühlenthal got word of Pujol’s request, he sent his local Madrid officer, Friedrich Knappe-Ratey (codenamed Frederico), to meet him. Knappe-Ratey was unimpressed at first. Pujol claimed he could deliver any intelligence out of Britain. Knappe-Ratey thought: Who does this man think he is? Churchill’s cousin?

But Pujol’s grasp of Nazi ideology won him over. Knappe-Ratey was stunned that a Spaniard understood Nazi doctrine better than he did. Pujol was unreliable, sure, but his loyalty seemed genuine. He got his shot.

“You claim you can gather intelligence in Britain. Travel documents are tightly controlled. How do you prove you can get there?”

Pujol knew what to do. He traveled to Portugal and bought a forged passport. It would never pass British border checks, but it looked convincing enough to fool Knappe-Ratey.

Convinced, the Germans gave him the codename Arabel, a bottle of invisible ink, a codebook, and three thousand US dollars in startup funds. His orders: travel to Britain and gather intelligence.

Pujol immediately hit a massive problem: he barely spoke any English. Incredibly, the Germans never noticed this gap.

He could not go to Britain, and staying in Madrid risked running into his German handlers. He settled on Lisbon, Portugal.

Once in Lisbon, he tried again to approach the British embassy. This time, they would not even let him inside.

He was stuck. The Germans expected intelligence from him, and he had no British network to source anything. If his letters carried Lisbon postmarks, the Germans would instantly spot the lie.

That was when he invented his first fake sub-agent. He wrote to Knappe-Ratey: “I have recruited a contact. He is a Catalan separatist, a fellow countryman, working as a KLM flight attendant. For secrecy, I cannot name him. To avoid British mail checks, all my reports will be carried by hand to Lisbon and posted here. Send your replies to Lisbon, and my courier will pass them to me.”

Knappe-Ratey was dazzled by this clever security setup. He accepted the Lisbon postmarks without question, and their correspondence began.

With the postmark problem solved, Pujol checked into a comfortable Lisbon hotel and started his deception. He sent three coded letters to Madrid:
First: he claimed he had recruited three sub-agents in Liverpool, Glasgow, and Switzerland.
Second: he wrote that he had landed a job at the BBC and could stay in Britain long-term.
Third, the most important: his Liverpool contact reported a British fleet of five warships had sailed, bound for Malta.

Where did this intelligence come from? Pujol could not read English and knew almost nothing about Britain. He pieced together every report from a British tourist guide and local Lisbon newspapers.

His reports were full of holes. One fictional Glasgow agent was described as a drunk who would sell secrets for a liter of wine. Anyone familiar with Scotland would know wine was not popular there, and Britain used imperial units, not liters.

Pujol did not know that — and Knappe-Ratey knew even less. He spotted none of the flaws. Excited, he translated the reports and sent them to Berlin, sending more money and asking for details on British troop numbers, unit designations and weapons.

While the German handlers happily processed these fake British reports, MI5 was thrown into panic. They had a mole inside the Abwehr, and the mole passed along Pujol’s Malta fleet report. By pure luck, a real top-secret British convoy was indeed sailing toward Malta. The dates and agent counts were wrong, but the destination matched.

MI5 feared they had a serious leak inside Britain. They launched a nationwide manhunt to hunt down this mysterious enemy spy.

Pujol had no idea he had caused such alarm. He was stressed: the Germans were asking for troop and equipment details he could not invent from guidebooks. He thought he might soon run out of material and planned to take one final payment and vanish.

Then his wife gave him an idea. “Don’t only focus on Britain. Japan and the United States are fighting in the Pacific. Why not reach out to the Americans?”

In 1942, Pujol walked into the US embassy in Lisbon. American officers listened to his wild story and immediately grasped its enormous value. They told him to stay put and wait.

The US passed the whole story to British intelligence. MI5 was still frantically searching for the phantom spy. When they learned their feared deep cover agent was a Spanish man who barely spoke English, they were embarrassed.

The case was handed over to MI6.

On April 24, 1942, Pujol and his family flew to Britain. Two British intelligence officers took charge of him. His primary handler was Tomás Harris — a man who counted Kim Philby, one of the Cambridge Five Soviet spies, as a close friend.

So a Soviet mole, working as a British intelligence officer, managed a Spanish double agent. That was the tangled, shadowy world of Second World War intelligence.

The British gave Pujol a clear mission: keep feeding the Germans intelligence. The Germans had to believe Arabel’s network was priceless, otherwise they would build new spy rings in Britain.

With British support, Pujol’s gift for fabrication bloomed. In letters and later radio messages to Madrid, he built an entire fictional spy network. By the end, he claimed to command twenty-eight sub-agents, all invented.

Each fake agent had a distinct personality, background, education, politics and quirks. Some were university professors, others factory workers. Pujol adjusted his tone for every character, so each report sounded like it came from a different person. The German team in Madrid spent hours decoding every incoming message.

Karl-Erich Kühlenthal was thrilled with his star agent. When someone suggested recruiting more spies in Britain, Kühlenthal said Arabel’s network was perfect. They needed no one else.

Most of the intelligence the British let Pujol send was true, but timed carefully. The information arrived late enough that it could not hurt Allied operations. Delayed dispatches were easy to explain: courier delays were normal in wartime.

In November 1942, just before Operation Torch, the Allied invasion of North Africa, Pujol sent a priority airmail report: a fleet painted for Mediterranean service had sailed, likely heading for North Africa. The letter was postmarked to look like it had been sent the moment the fleet departed.

But the flight carrying the mail arrived in Madrid only after the invasion had begun. Knappe-Ratey wrote back, frustrated. “Your report is excellent, but it arrived too late to be useful.”

The Germans would inevitably grow suspicious if critical intelligence kept arriving late. Pujol anticipated their doubts. Before they could question him, he sent a message: the Liverpool sub-agent responsible for forwarding reports had fallen ill and died. Delays would end now.

The Germans sent condolences to the widow of this nonexistent spy and paid a pension.

To stop future hold-ups, the Abwehr sent Pujol their latest radio set in August 1943 and gave him access to their most secure encryption channel. After Turing’s team cracked the Enigma cipher, this second German secure channel also fell under British surveillance.

The Allies poured so much effort into this deception for a simple reason: a double agent like Arabel could change the war, if the enemy trusted him.

By January 1944, Allied commanders in the West were ready to invade occupied France. Everyone knew an invasion was coming. The Abwehr ordered Arabel to uncover the exact Allied landing plan.

At the same time, Pujol received orders from Allied high command: convince the Germans the main attack would land at Pas de Calais.

Pujol was no longer just a pawn. He held the threads of one of the greatest deceptions of the war.

From January to June 1944, he pushed himself to exhaustion, writing and transmitting over five hundred radio messages, sometimes twenty in a single day, speaking through all twenty-eight of his invented identities. He described a phantom army and a fake battle plan.

Built on years of trusted reports, the lie stuck. German high command became convinced the main Allied strike would hit Pas de Calais. Multiple intelligence sources all “confirmed” the same story, so the German generals trusted it.

His finest hour came on D-Day.

Late on June 5, 1944, Pujol radioed Madrid: the invasion is imminent. He knew the message would not get an immediate reply. Back in February, Hitler had lost faith in the Abwehr and handed many of its duties to the Gestapo. Communications were chaotic, and radio operators were not always on duty.

The reply came at eight o’clock the next morning. The Allies were already ashore. The German intelligence team apologized profusely.

Pujol berated them fiercely: “I will not accept excuses for failures in this critical moment. I am disgusted by this lapse. I will walk away from this work if this happens again.”

The Madrid handlers panicked and begged him not to abandon his post.

From then on, they believed everything he sent. His next bombshell report: the Normandy landings were only a diversion. Twenty-five Allied divisions remained in reserve, waiting to strike Pas de Calais, likely under General Patton.

Berlin cross-checked the intelligence and accepted it. The Germans kept twenty-one divisions stationed around Pas de Calais, even as Normandy’s front cried out for reinforcements. They refused to move those troops.

This deception saved countless Allied lives and was critical to the success of the Normandy campaign.

Once the landing was secure, Pujol could no longer sustain his massive fictional spy ring. By late June, he was burnt out. His British handlers staged his arrest by MI5. He was released weeks later, and British newspapers published an official apology letter from the Home Secretary for the unlawful detention.

Pujol messaged Madrid again, saying he had been exposed and had to go underground. Knappe-Ratey, his loyal German handler, still believed he was a loyal Nazi agent. Even as the Third Reich crumbled, he petitioned Hitler to award Pujol the Iron Cross Second Class — an award normally reserved for frontline soldiers.

The radio message reached Pujol: “It is with great joy and satisfaction I inform you the Führer has awarded you the Iron Cross for your extraordinary service. This honor is rarely given to non-combatants. Congratulations.”

Pujol replied: “I cannot express my gratitude for this award. I feel unworthy; I have only done my duty.”

Agent Arabel’s work for the Nazis ended. Months later, King George VI signed a secret citation making him a Member of the Order of the British Empire. David Petrie, head of MI5, handed him the medal privately before Christmas.

Juan Pujol García became one of the very few people in the Second World War to receive decorations from both the Axis and the Allies.

When the war ended, Pujol first traveled to the United States. J. Edgar Hoover, the famous director of the FBI, invited him to dinner. Back in London, British intelligence offered him two paths: a job with a security firm, or a far more dangerous assignment — infiltrating the Soviet Union, which was recruiting former German agents.

Following British advice, he chose the second option. Before setting off, he returned to Madrid to find his old handler Knappe-Ratey. He ran into Karl-Erich Kühlenthal, who put him back in touch with Knappe-Ratey.

Even after Nazi Germany collapsed, the two German intelligence officers still admired Pujol. They never learned he had fooled them completely. They gave him more money. Knappe-Ratey warned him: “Leave this life. Spies rarely get happy endings.”

That advice made him pause. He abandoned the plan to infiltrate the USSR and headed for South America.

South America was dangerous. Many fleeing Nazi war criminals had settled there, and Pujol feared retaliation. In 1949, he returned to Britain. With help from his British contacts, the government publicly announced that Pujol was moving to Angola.

The official story: he contracted malaria and died there.

Months later, a new employee joined Shell’s Venezuelan oil division. He traveled with geological survey teams across the country, friendly and well-liked. He remarried a local woman, settled down, and used his savings to open a stationery shop. He fished, drank rum, and lived quietly.

In 1971, a British writer named Nigel West stumbled across the story of this legendary double agent. Nigel West was the pen name of Rupert Allason, a Conservative politician with rare access to intelligence circles.

Decades had passed, many witnesses were dead, and files were scattered. It took until 1984 for Rupert Allason to track Pujol down.

Pujol wanted to stay hidden. He remembered Knappe-Ratey’s warning. But Allason’s words changed his mind: “Would you not like to meet your old colleagues again?”

On May 20, 1984, Pujol met Allason in the United States. He traveled back to Britain with him, where Prince Philip received him and showed him Windsor Castle. Old intelligence comrades gathered in London to see him.

On June 6, he sailed to Normandy. He had never fought there, yet the beach was forever tied to his legacy. He attended the memorial service honoring the fallen, then flew back to Venezuela.

Four years later, on October 10, 1988, Juan Pujol García died peacefully surrounded by his children and grandchildren. The man who had lived between two worlds was buried far from his Spanish homeland.
